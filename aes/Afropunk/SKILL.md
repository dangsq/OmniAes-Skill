# Afropunk

Afropunk names the presence of Black people in punk and alternative subcultures: a music genre, a community and a look that has existed since punk's 1970s origins but acquired its name and its visual self-consciousness after the 2003 documentary *Afro-Punk* by James Spooner and Matthew Morgan, and its gathering place in the Afropunk Festival founded in 2005. Visually it is a fusion grammar — punk's tartan, leather, bondage hardware and DIY damage welded onto Black hair culture, festival flamboyance and street style — recorded mostly in candid photography: festival fields, club doorways, chainlink fences, brick walls. Note honestly that the style's written fashion canon is thin (the movement's own fashion and characteristics documentation is unfinished), so its visual rules below are distilled from its stated motifs and values (DIY above all; rebellion, independence, anti-conformity, social justice, self-expression, Black liberation) plus the photographic record of its festivals, zines and scenes.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** A municipal, desaturated ground — concrete gray (#8C8C8C–#B5B5B5), brick red-brown (#6E4433–#7A4A32), chainlink silver, denim indigo (#3A4A6B–#4A5A78), festival grass — carries a costume of black (#0A0A0A–#1A1A1A) punctured by one to three high-voltage accents. Saturation lives in the body, never the background: hair dyed magenta (#E0308A–#FF2E9A), violet (#7A3FBF–#9B4DDB), teal-green (#1F7A6B–#2E9E83) or bleach-blonde; garments in acid yellow (#D8E000–#F2E200), tartan red (#8E1B1B–#C22424), mustard or sunflower color-blocks. Metallic sparks of gold and silver hardware (#C9A227) sit at necks, wrists and hips. Two licensed variants: a carnival mode where bright co-ords (yellow, green, orange) rival the black, and an editorial mode where the whole frame desaturates except a single saturated head of braids.

**Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence.**
1. *DIY alteration* — the movement's only named key motif: cut-off hems, safety pins, hand-sewn harnesses, patches, cracked screen-printed band tees, mismatched tartan panels, garments visibly made or remade by the wearer.
2. *Festival gathering* — the Afropunk Festival is the style's significant manifestation: wristbands, stage scaffolding on the horizon, blurred crowds, daylight plazas and grass.
3. *Punk lineage regalia* — inherited from the named pioneers (Poly Styrene, Don Letts, Death, Pure Hell, Bad Brains, Fishbone, Betty Davis): tartan plaid, leather and vinyl jackets, bondage straps, dreadlocks and mohawks, band beanies.
4. *Zine and print matter* — the *Shotgun Seamstress* and *Black and Now: RESIST* lineage: photocopied flyers, cut-and-paste xerox typography, stapled booklets held or pinned to jackets.
5. *Documentary snapshot* — the 2003 film's ethic: grain, candid framing, unposed bodies.

**Photographic-record motifs (supporting evidence).** Hair as manifesto: locs, braids, afros, frohawks, shaved heads, dyed electric or crowned with flowers, sunflowers, headwraps and bandanas. Hardware: chest harnesses with O-rings, chains, chokers, layered necklaces, tinted round sunglasses, dark matte lipstick, fishnet sleeves and stockings, combat boots. Sheer mesh and crochet tops baring midriffs; visible tattoos; festival cups in hand.

**Materials and textures.** Patent vinyl and leather shine against matte cracked-print cotton; fishnet and sheer mesh over skin; handmade crochet and lace softness; tartan wool; raw denim; cold chain and ring metal; environmental grit of brick mortar, chainlink diamond mesh and poured concrete. Sweat and sunscreen sheen in daylight scenes.

**Recurring subjects.** Black individuals and crews, frequently androgynous or gender-nonconforming in presentation; friend groups with arms interlocked; couples; performers mid-set; solo sitters crouching, dancing or flashing peace signs. Bodies carry self-possession: direct unsmiling gaze or wide grin, hands on hips, chins up.

**Composition and lighting.** Environmental portraiture: full- or three-quarter-body figures at eye level or slightly below, background legible and municipal (fence, brick, scaffolding, crowd bokeh). Group shots pack bodies edge-to-edge; solo shots keep community energy through wristbands, stickers, a friend's hand at frame edge. Snapshot framing with slight tilt or casual crop is authentic; a rare formal register places one sitter against raw concrete with a single prop. Light comes in four registers: direct on-camera flash at night (hot highlights, deep shadow, glowing brick); overcast open shade (even, true color); hard festival sun (squint, contrast, sheen); flat single-source studio light with a muted grade. Grain and minor imperfection are features, not faults.

## Mood & Values

Defiant joy. The style expresses rebellion, independence, anti-conformity, social justice, self-expression and Black liberation, and it does so communally: toughness (boots, straps, dark lips) held together with tenderness (flowers in hair, arms around shoulders, crochet made by hand). It is the look of belonging forged by people excluded from both mainstream white punk and from respectability politics — alienation answered with self-made splendor. Confrontation here is posture, not violence: a stare that does not ask permission.

## Era & Origins

Roots in the 1970s, when Black bands and performers (Pure Hell, Death, Bad Brains, Fishbone, X-Ray Spex's Poly Styrene, Don Letts) built punk alongside its white scenes; naming and wider popularization in the 2000s via the 2003 Spooner/Morgan documentary and the 2005-founded festival; global expansion since through film series, anthologies (*Black Punk Now*, 2023), memoir (*Dreadnaught: King of Afropunk*) and zines. The style reacts against two erasures at once: the whiteness of mainstream punk narratives and the conformity demanded of Black self-presentation.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Black subjecthood is definitional; this is a culture, not a costume.
2. Show DIY evidence: at least one altered, patched, hand-sewn or thrifted element.
3. Treat hair as a primary canvas — locs, braids, afros, shaved crowns, electric dye, floral or wrapped adornment.
4. Fuse punk hardware (tartan, leather, harness, fishnet, boots) with Black street and festival flamboyance rather than choosing one.
5. Set bodies in municipal or festival space: fences, brick, concrete, grass, crowds, stage rigs — never luxury or speculative sets.
6. Shoot with snapshot ethics: flash or daylight, grain, candid posture, imperfect crop.
7. Prefer community: touching bodies, shared frames, crew energy; solo portraits imply the crowd.

**Variation axes.** Subject: festival crowd, stage performance, street portrait, club doorway, protest march, zine collage, market stall, studio editorial. Composition: edge-to-edge group cluster, single full-body environmental portrait, mid-motion candid, tight crop on hair and hardware. Lighting: night flash, overcast shade, hard sun, flat studio. Color handling: black-dominant with one electric accent; full carnival color-block; desaturated frame with saturated hair only. Mood: defiant, joyous, tender, confrontational. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Seamless white-studio e-commerce lighting and poses; luxury logos, aspirational bling and designer sportswear as focal points; chrome, CGI cosmos, holographic techwear (Afrofuturism); kente-and-dashiki heritage styling with no punk abrasion (80s–90s Afrocentrism); soft linen, houseplants, golden-hour cottage glow (Earthy Black Girl); satin flares and sequined stage glamour as the whole statement (Funk); airbrushed murals, lowriders, sneaker-head styling (Hip-Hop); generic white punk clichés detached from Black subjecthood; polished salon hair and retouched flawless skin; head-to-toe new boutique clothing with no handmade trace; stock-photo smiles and sterile backgrounds.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Their tell | Afropunk's tell |
|---|---|---|
| 80s–90s Afrocentrism | Kente, dashikis, cowrie shells, warm earth tones, heritage celebration | Same pride plus tartan, leather, safety pins, dyed dreads, confrontational stance |
| Afrofuturism | Chrome, cosmic CGI, sleek techwear, speculative narrative | Analog, hand-made, worn-in, present-day street and festival ground |
| Earthy Black Girl | Linen, plants, soft golden light, minimal makeup | Vinyl, fishnet, dark lipstick, flash-lit urban night or hard daylight |
| Funk | Satin, sequins, flares, platforms, polished stage glamour | DIY damage, mesh and harness, combat boots, anti-glamour grit |
| Hip-Hop | Logo sportswear, bling chains, sneakers, aspirational luxury | Thrift and zine culture, band patches, androgyny, anti-brand styling |
| Mainstream (white) punk | Mohawk-and-tartan uniform on non-Black bodies, nihilist coldness | Black bodies at center, color-play in hair, festival warmth, community touch |

When in doubt, ask: is there handmade evidence, is the hair a statement, is the ground municipal or festival, and does the frame feel like a friend took it? If yes to all, it is Afropunk.
