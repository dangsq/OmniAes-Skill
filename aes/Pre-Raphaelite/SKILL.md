# Pre-Raphaelite

The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood was a secret society of seven painters, poets and critics founded in London in 1848 — Hunt, Millais, Dante Gabriel Rossetti, William Michael Rossetti, Woolner, Collinson and Stephens — who rebelled against the Royal Academy's worship of Raphael and its formulaic academic conventions. They looked backward instead, to the detail, bright color and emotional sincerity of Quattrocento art before Raphael (helping revive Botticelli's reputation), and forward to a practice of direct, attentive observation of nature. Their paintings combine narrative seriousness with gem-like surface richness: a literary or sacred story told through species-identifiable flowers, woven damask, strand-by-strand hair, and saturated local color that never muddies into academic brown. After the Brotherhood dissolved by 1853, the style persisted and evolved: Rossetti's sensuous, symbolic femme fatale of the 1860s–70s, then Burne-Jones and Morris's more decorative medievalism, which seeded the Aesthetic and Arts and Crafts movements. Recognizing "Pre-Raphaelite" therefore means recognizing a family of two phases — early hard-edged realism and later dreamy symbolism — united by jewel color, luminous detail and moral-emotional intensity.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule: local color stays saturated, separate and jewel-bright; whites glow rather than grey; shadows are colored, never bituminous brown. Grounds may go deep and dark in the later phase, but the figure's hues remain clear against them.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how strongly the text documents them)

1. **The idealized woman / femme fatale** — the movement's central motif: long, flowing, abundant hair (copper, red-gold, auburn, or blue-black), pale skin, strong jaw, full bowed lips, heavy-lidded or far-away eyes, an ethereal quality tinged with tragedy or longing. Often caught mid-gesture with a comb, mirror, flower or thread.
2. **Literary and Arthurian narrative** — scenes from Tennyson, Keats, Dante and medieval romance: heroines at crisis moments (drowning, drifting enchanted in a boat, bound by a spell), knights, banquets, partings.
3. **Meticulously rendered nature** — "truth to nature": species-identifiable blossoms, reeds, water plants, individual blades of grass; streams, ponds, meadows painted from direct observation.
4. **Medievalism** — doublets, hose, brocade and coifs, oak furniture, tapestries, heraldic and damask patterns, Quattrocento-style interiors.
5. **Religious and mythological subjects** — biblical scenes staged with unidealized, workshop realism in the early phase (the scandal of the carpenter's shop), symbolic pagan figures (Proserpine, Lilith, Astarte) in the later phase.
6. **Symbolic attribute objects** — hand mirror, lit candles, pomegranate or cut flower, loom and thread, boat, open book, curling incense smoke: small legible emblems carrying the story's meaning.

### Materials, textures, medium

Oil paint worked to an enamel-hard, glazed finish; the early Brotherhood's craft of thin glazes over a wet white ground produces the characteristic gem-like luminosity. Brushwork is concealed — no impasto, no painterly bravura. Textures are enumerated with love: brocade and embroidered hems, damask wallpaper, fur trim, polished oak and wood shavings, glass and metal candlesticks, still water surfaces, individual flower petals, and hair rendered strand by strand.

### Composition and space

Tendencies, not templates: frieze-like lateral arrangements of figures along a table, bank or shoreline; shallow, compressed space recalling early Renaissance painting; a horror vacui impulse that fills negative space with pattern — foliage, roses, damask, tapestry — so the ground is never empty; vertical single-figure portraits set against dark but incident-rich grounds (smoke, leaves, gilt); figures embedded in dense botanical surround. Silhouettes stay crisp against their patterns.

### Light

Early phase: bright, even, shadowless daylight in which every blade of grass is lit; no dominant cast shadow, no atmospheric haze. Later phase: interior gloom, candle flames, mirror glints, luminous skin and white drapery glowing against deep green-black grounds — but the modeling remains linear and crisp. Soft chiaroscuro and smoky sfumato are precisely what the style revolted against and never appear.

## Mood & Values

Sincerity, truth to nature, moral seriousness: art as a spiritual and ethical endeavor, not technical display. The emotional register is heightened and heartfelt — grief, longing, temptation, devotion, quiet doom — later softening into dreamy languor and sensuous symbol-laden reverie without losing its charge. Underlying values are anti-academic, anti-formulaic, and (through Morris) anti-industrial: hand-made intensity, direct observation, genuine ideas to express.

## Era & Origins

London, 1848; exhibited from 1849 signed with the secret monogram "PRB"; ideas published in the journal *The Germ*; public scandal in 1850 over Millais's unidealized Holy Family (Dickens called it blasphemous); the Brotherhood effectively dissolved by 1853. A second generation — Burne-Jones, Morris, and associates like Waterhouse — carried the style through the late nineteenth century into decorative arts, the Aesthetic movement and Arts and Crafts. Its afterlife runs through twentieth- and twenty-first-century fantasy illustration, fashion photography and Victorian-romantic pop culture.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Everything in focus: foreground and background rendered with equal devotion; no blurred distance.
2. Jewel local color: each object keeps its saturated hue; whites luminous ivory; shadows colored, not brown.
3. Crisp contour, concealed brushwork, enamel-glazed oil surface.
4. Fill the ground: pattern, foliage, textile or architecture occupies negative space; if the ground is dark, give it incident — smoke, leaves, gilt.
5. Give the picture a story or a symbol: an attribute object (mirror, candle, flower, thread, boat, book) carries meaning.
6. Faces: pale luminous skin, strong jaw, full bowed mouth, heavy-lidded or distant gaze; hair abundant and rendered strand by strand.
7. Historicize dress and setting: medieval or Quattrocento costume, doublets, hose, brocade, coifs, oak and tapestry; no modern objects.
8. Keep the register sincere: tender tragedy, longing, devotion — never ironic or casual.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any of biblical, Arthurian, Dantean, Keats/Tennyson, mythological, medieval domestic scene, muse portrait, or figure-in-nature study. Composition: horizontal multi-figure tableau, vertical single figure, close-up head, figure embedded in dense landscape, patterned interior, water or boat scene. Light: open even daylight (early manner) through interior gloom with candle and mirror glints (late manner), always crisp-edged. Color handling: full jewel range — red-gold Rossetti warmth, green-blue water scenes, plum-and-teal late portraits — with luminous white constant. Detail density: microscopic botany (first phase) to broader decorative stylization (second generation); both are valid Pre-Raphaelite. Mood: moral earnestness, dreamy languor, or quiet melancholy. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Soft chiaroscuro, sfumato or smoky brown tonality; visible impasto or impressionistic broken brushwork; beige/greige or muted earth-only palettes; desaturated pastels or neon digital color; photographic bokeh, soft-focus backgrounds, lens flare; modern clothing, hairstyles, objects or architecture; empty minimalist negative space or flat textureless studio backdrops; Art Nouveau whiplash stylization and poster-flat graphic line; airbrushed generic-fantasy smoothness or anime simplification; generic academic prettiness in faces (Pre-Raphaelite idealization is idiosyncratic — strong necks, asymmetrical, individualized features); ironic, casual or merely titillating registers — sensuality here must be symbolically charged.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Aestheticism** — closest descendant; shares jewel color and beauty-worship. But Aestheticism drops narrative and moral stakes for art-for-art's-sake arrangement (peacock feathers, blue-and-white china, japonisme, sunflowers). Pre-Raphaelite work, even at its most sensuous, still tells a story and hurts: grief, temptation, doom.
- **Romanticism** — shares literary sources and intense feeling. Romanticism paints loose, painterly, sublime: storm skies, swirling atmosphere, tonal golds and browns. Pre-Raphaelite paints botanically still, hard-edged, saturated, with medieval specificity instead of wild sublime nature.
- **Art Nouveau** — shares flowing hair, floral abundance, medievalized women. Art Nouveau flattens everything into whiplash line, arabesque and poster silhouette; Pre-Raphaelite keeps modeled volume, observed botany and illusionistic depth even at its most decorative.
- **Arts and Crafts** — shares medievalism and the Morris lineage. Arts and Crafts is design: repeating flat pattern, handcrafted textiles, tiles and furniture without narrative figures. A Pre-Raphaelite-looking floral field with no figure, story or depth is Arts and Crafts ornament.
- **Bohemian** — shares the anti-academic posture only. Bohemian aesthetics are modern, urban, casual, improvisational and sketchy or photographic; Pre-Raphaelite is costumed, staged, historically framed and meticulously finished.
