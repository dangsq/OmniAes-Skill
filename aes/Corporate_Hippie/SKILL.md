# Corporate Hippie

Corporate Hippie — also sold under the names Capitalist Hippie, Corporate Psychedelia, Psychsploitation and Hippie Exploitation — is not a subculture but a marketing tactic: the moment Madison Avenue, record labels, department stores and appliance makers borrowed the visual language of the hippie movement to sell product to a mainstream that had adopted the look without the politics. It flourished from roughly 1967 to 1973, growing out of the mainstream popularity of Flower Power and sitting exactly halfway between the Pop Art and Psychedelia of the late 1960s and the Earth Tones advertising of the 1970s. Think of it as psychedelia with the danger removed: same core visuals, less abstraction, more consumer-friendliness. Every artifact of this style is secretly a sales pitch — a fridge, a soda bottle, a sewing pattern, a stereo console, a furniture line, a compilation album — dressed in peace-and-love plumage.

## Visual Identity

### Palette: the whole spectrum, printed flat
The style's declared key colour is "all," and in practice that means any combination of highly saturated inks, usually three to five hues fighting cheerfully against one ground. The working band runs from hot magenta and pink (#E63E7C–#F06292), tangerine and poppy orange (#F26522–#FF8A3D, #D93A1E), sunflower and gold yellow (#F5C400–#E8B100), chartreuse and lime (#B5CC32–#D7E04A), turquoise and cyan (#1FB6C9–#4DD3DE), to violet (#6C3FA0–#8E5AC8) and periwinkle blue (#5F83CF–#7FA0E8), with kelly green (#3E9B4F) for leaves. Grounds are either warm cream/off-white paper (#F3EDDC), solid ink black (#171310), or one dominant saturated field against which the others stripe and bloom. Late-period work (1971–1975) may let mustard, olive and harvest brown (#C89A2B, #7A7A33, #8A5A2B) creep in at the edges — the bridge toward the Earth Tones that would replace the style. Colours arrive as flat printed ink: no gradients, no modelling, no atmospheric depth.

### Lettering as hero
Bold, distorted text is the style's first among equals. Display type is liquid and inflated: bubble letters, warped and melting letterforms, words arched like circus marquees, words stacked and mirror-flipped into pattern, logotypes tilted on diagonal banners. Type frequently *is* the image — a radial mandala built from a repeated warped word, or a towel-sized LOVE dominating a flower field. Against this carnival display sits the ordinary apparatus of advertising: small justified copy blocks, track lists, price-free offer text, mastheads and brand lockups, written in borrowed youth slang — exclamatory, rhyming, list-happy, sprinkled with "groovy," "unisex," "underground," "teensy."

### Motifs, ranked by how centrally the style documents them
1. **Distorted, warped visuals and lettering** — the defining gesture; liquid type, bent perspective, swirling contour lines.
2. **Themes of love** — hearts, the word LOVE, doves, embracing couples, "peace & love" as literal copy.
3. **Bright saturated colour** — the full ink set above, deployed flat and loud.
4. **Flowers and flourishes** — daisies, tulip shapes, paisley scrolls, petal borders, vine tendrils curling into corners.
5. **Kaleidoscopic and abstract patterns** — radial mandalas, concentric rings, sunburst rays, mirror-symmetric repeats.
6. **Flat yet maximalist graphics** — horror vacui filled with orderly solid-ink shapes; busy but never painterly.
7. **Subtle drug iconography** — a mushroom here, a wink in the copy ("the trip," "underground"); never explicit paraphernalia.
8. **Hippie-fashion figures** — bell-bottoms, fringed and patterned vests, crochet and knit waistcoats, headscarves, flowers in long hair, go-go poses.

### Materials, textures, medium
This is a print culture: offset lithography on cream paper and card, laminate record sleeves, magazine pages, sewing-pattern and studio-card envelopes, linen and cotton textile prints. Photographed areas show matte emulsion and visible halftone dots; colour separations may sit a hair out of register. Surfaces are matte ink on paper — no foil, no gloss varnish, no embossing. Craft textures (crochet, knit) appear only *inside* photographed fashion, never as the graphic surface itself, which stays clean flat ink.

### Subjects
Consumer goods and corporate messages of every kind: kitchen appliances, soda bottles, hi-fi stereos and radios, furniture and bedroom suites, clothing and paper patterns, toy and model-kit boxes, compilation album covers, magazine mastheads, department-store ads, printed textiles and towels. Illustrated scenes of youth life — a teenager's poster-plastered bedroom, a custom trike — count too, as do word-only prints where the brand or the sentiment is the product.

### Composition and lighting
Composition is orderly maximalism. Recurring structures: radial symmetry (mandala, sunburst, concentric arcs) organizing the chaos; arched lettering crowning the top edge; a centred product or word as hero inside radiating ornament; flower-and-paisley border frames; collages of circular or rectangular photo insets dropped into flat graphic fields; diagonal logo bands; full-bleed pattern with a stacked word punched out of it; cluttered illustrated interiors. Advertising hierarchy survives everywhere: display type, copy block and lockup keep their legible relationships. Lighting follows the medium — graphic areas have no light source at all, pure flat ink; photographic elements use even, shadowless studio light or soft diffuse daylight, matte and unglamorous. Never dramatic chiaroscuro, never golden-hour romance.

## Mood & Values

The declared values are peace and love, and the mood is those values defanged into décor: cheerful, optimistic, aggressively friendly, a sanitized rebellion that reads as wholesome even when it winks. There is playfulness and mild cheek — a mushroom in the corner, "underground" in the headline — but always within family-safe bounds. The style flatters the buyer as young, hip and inclusive ("unisex") while selling establishment product; its euphoria is commercial, its transgression theatrical. Underneath the grin sits the period's real tension: counterculture symbols emptied of protest and refilled with consumption.

## Era & Origins

The source movements — hippie and psychedelic — emerged on the American West Coast around 1965–1967, promoting anti-war sentiment, peace and love, and recreational drug use through the psychedelic music scene; exploit-the-scene records appeared as early as 1965. With the Summer of Love (1967) the look went mainstream, and corporations rushed to borrow it, spawning Corporate Hippie as a distinct commercial mode from 1967 to 1973. By 1970 the hippie movement's absorption into the mainstream (and the Manson murders' tarnish) drained its exclusivity; by 1973 both the subculture and its corporate mimic had fallen out of favour, replaced in advertising by Earth Tones. The term itself was coined later, by Jack Grimes.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Compose as commerce: include some advertising apparatus — brand lockup, masthead, copy block, product name, or at least a word treated as a brand. Even a textile print carries its slogan.
- Ink, not light: colour arrives as flat printed fields; shading minimal or absent; photographic inserts lit evenly and mattely.
- Let type perform: warp, inflate, arch or stack the display lettering; keep it legible but liquid.
- Fill the frame with orderly abundance: flowers, rays, rings, flourishes; cream resting space is paper, not minimalism.
- Use radial gravity: mandalas, sunbursts and concentric arcs to organize maximal clutter.
- Make love visible: hearts, doves, rainbows, the word LOVE, affectionate couples.
- Wink, don't confess: counterculture signs (mushrooms, peace signs, "underground" slang) as décor only.
- Wear the period body: cream paper, halftone dots, slight misregistration, matte emulsion, the crop logic of a sleeve, page or poster.

**Variation axes** — a correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.
- *Subject*: any product, service, publication, textile, word-print, or illustrated youth scene; geography and language may vary.
- *Composition*: radial mandala; centred-product sunburst; floral border frame; diagonal banner; photo-inset collage; full-bleed pattern with stacked word; cluttered interior scene; single fashion figure over a graphic ground.
- *Lighting*: none (flat ink) through even shadowless studio; occasional soft daylight inside photo inserts only.
- *Colour*: any combination from the saturated band; ground cream, black, or one dominant hue; late-period mustard/olive/brown admixture allowed.
- *Mood*: wholesome-cheerful, cheeky-subversive-lite, romantic-love, playful-kiddie.
- *Medium look*: magazine page, vinyl sleeve, poster, towel or linen print, pattern envelope, catalog card.

**Avoid**
- Digital gradients, glossy 3D renders, lens flare, HDR, photoreal CGI smoothness.
- Underground-poster illegibility; copy and product must stay consumer-readable.
- Minimalist whitespace layouts, neutral corporate grotesques, International Style grids.
- Monochrome earth-tone palettes (that is post-1973 Earth Tones) or beige craft-paper naturalism.
- Tie-dye bleed, macramé and handmade craft roughness as the dominant surface — this style is printed ink, not homespun.
- Neon-on-black chrome, fractals, glitch (Cyberdelic territory).
- Mod Op-art geometry, Union Jacks, Swinging-London iconography.
- Beatnik monochrome: turtlenecks, jazz-club smoky greys, sardonic cool.
- Explicit drug imagery or paraphernalia; the iconography stays subtle.
- Grunge distress, xerox punk collage, torn-paper Dada.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Flower Power** is the genuine counterculture: daisies in hair, handmade craft, sun-faded natural light, documentary candidness. Corporate Hippie keeps the flowers but prints them flat, adds brand lockups, copy blocks and studio polish — commerce wearing the movement's clothes.
- **Psychedelia** is the underground source: extreme perceptual distortion, deliberately illegible liquid type, swirling non-Euclidean space, concert mythos. Corporate Hippie borrows the vocabulary but preserves legibility, advertising hierarchy and orderly maximalism; it is psychedelia watered down for shoppers.
- **Groovival** is the later revival (1990s onward): clean digital vectors, modern brand sensibility, often parody exaggeration. Corporate Hippie carries period print materiality — halftone, cream paper, misregistration, vintage fashion photography, period ad-copy voice.
- **Beatnik** is the preceding decade's cool: monochrome or near-monochrome palettes, coffeehouses, jazz, berets and turtlenecks, existential irony. No saturated colour, no flowers, no love themes.
- **Cool Britannia** swings in mod geometry: Op-art stripes, targets, Union Jacks, minis, a red-white-blue national palette. Corporate Hippie is American-style commercial psychedelia — radial floral maximalism, bubble type, product heroes — sharing only the 1960s optimism.
