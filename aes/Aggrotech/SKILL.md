# Aggrotech

Aggrotech is a music genre whose visual identity lives almost entirely on record sleeves, flyers and stage projection: a harsh offshoot of Electronic Body Music that emerged in the mid-to-late 1990s in Germany, Belgium and North America, and peaked underground in the mid-2000s inside the Cybergoth club scene. Where Cybergoth parties celebrate neon futurism, Aggrotech artwork stages the end of the world: tactical and urban-warfare imagery, heavy machinery, biohazard signage, gas masks, medical horror and stylized violence, all delivered with the loud, distressed graphic-design language of underground club print. Its aggression is performative and apolitical — shock as catharsis, not propaganda — and its core myth is the "technological apocalypse": man fused with machine as a horrifying inevitability rather than a utopia.

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule: hazard signage, not naturalism
Every Aggrotech image sits inside a warning-sign palette: a dominant field of black or bone-white, one hot signal color, and optionally a military mid-tone. Treat color as hazard printing — flat, saturated, posterized — never as observed nature.

- **Black field:** #050505–#1C1C1C, the default ground.
- **Blood red:** #6E0B0B (dried) through #E32219 (vermilion signal red). The most common hot color; used as duotone ink, splatter, flags, stars and type bands.
- **Olive / drab green:** #3E4622–#7A8B4A, plus desaturated sage #9AA896 for overcast military photography and painted gun metal.
- **Acid yellow-green:** #C6D422–#E8FF3C, the text-documented "neon green / acid yellow"; in practice it appears as stencil type, radiation trefoils and sickly tinting rather than rave-bright flood.
- **Bone / clinical white:** #E9E7E1–#F4F4F2, a high-key contrast field for medical-cold variants (masked figures, pictogram signs on white).
- **Rare cold variant:** a single cyan-teal duotone tint over black as an off-key accent.

Acceptable handling ranges from full red/black or green/black duotone, through posterized high-contrast tri-tones, to a white field carrying one red or black accent only.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how strongly the style documents them)
1. **Gas masks and respirators** — the pervasive emblem; rubber facepiece, round filter cartridges, often shown in close-up with the filter stamped by a symbol.
2. **Biohazard and radiation pictograms** — trefoils, biohazard trefoil-blades, warning triangles, emergency-exit and evacuation pictograms; vector signs layered over photography.
3. **Tactical and military gear, urban warfare imagery** — caps, webbing, straps, uniforms, anti-aircraft guns, radar dishes, antenna arrays, ruined city skylines.
4. **Heavy machinery** — matte painted steel, coiled barrels, hydraulics; machine bodies cropped monumental and low-angle.
5. **Body horror and medical horror** — stitched or mutilated mouths, dripping black blood, flesh fused with skeletal prosthetics and cabling, clinical white settings turned cruel.
6. **Stylized violence implements** — blades, razors, hammers, rope; often arranged as a cold flat-lay inventory rather than an action scene.
7. **Anonymous figures** — masked, blindfolded, cropped, or posterized faces; uniformed or capped performers; crowds and flags staged as theater; occasional uncanny innocence (a child, flowers) inserted into ruin for dread.
8. **Aggressive synthesizer hardware** — documented in the style's key motifs but rare on sampled covers; permitted as prop (knobs, patch cables, battered rack gear).

**Typography is a motif, not an afterthought:** military stencil, condensed industrial sans-serif, and blackletter/gothic logotypes, all distressed, eroded or splattered; type set in hard bands or blocks at the top or bottom edge, plus emblem marks (stars, seals). Lettering carries as much identity as the photograph.

### Materials and textures
Halftone print dots and offset misregistration; photocopier grain and xerox contrast; pixel-noise scatter and glitch speckle; scratches, scuffs and distressed edges; blood splatter and drip; matte chipped paint on metal; rubber, canvas webbing and filter paper; occasional uncanny domestic textures (damask upholstery, clinical tile) used as cold contrast. Everything reads as *printed matter*, never as a clean digital render.

### Composition and lighting tendencies
Square album-cover format is the native canvas. Composition defaults to a central emblematic object or figure (mask, gun, machine, kneeling body) framed by type bands; alternatives include grid flat-lays of implements, collage layering of photo + vector pictogram + logo, and high-key negative-space layouts with a small figure and large sign. Lighting ranges: harsh direct flash on figures; flat even scan lighting on object arrays; overcast, desaturated daylight for exterior military and ruin scenes; ember/flame glow for apocalyptic scenes; blown-out white studio light for the clinical variant. Contrast is always pushed — crushed blacks or blown whites, little mid-tone comfort.

## Mood & Values

Misanthropy, apocalypticism, techno-nihilism. The image should feel like a threat assessment posted in a bunker: cold, clinical enumeration of violence (flat-lays, pictograms, stencil captions) colliding with hot, screamed aggression (splatter, distortion, blackletter). Values expressed: provocation over persuasion, catharsis over politics, dread of the man-machine fusion. Nothing is comforting, glossy or aspirational; even the white-field variants feel like hospital paperwork for something cruel.

## Era & Origins

Name coined in 1990 at a San Francisco industrial club; the genre itself crystallized mid-to-late 1990s across Germany, Belgium and North America, with Mexican act Hocico as pioneer, and reached its underground peak in the mid-2000s alongside Cybergoth. It evolves from EBM by adding harsh distortion, fast 4/4 techno/trance beats and power-noise aggression — and visually reacts against both EBM's sparse masculine minimalism and Cybergoth's rave neon by pushing militaristic, biohazardous, body-horror imagery.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
1. Build the image like underground club print: photo layer + distress layer + type/pictogram layer.
2. Restrict the palette to field + one hot signal color (+ optional olive), posterized or duotoned.
3. Make anonymity and protection central: masks, filters, caps, cropped or posterized faces.
4. Prop the scene with military, medical or industrial hardware — painted steel, rubber, webbing — not consumer tech.
5. Set shock theatrically: staged, symbolic, apolitical; dread and catharsis, not reportage or propaganda.
6. Distress everything: grain, halftone, scratches, splatter, eroded type. No clean renders.
7. Let typography and warning pictograms compose the frame as much as the subject does.

**Variation axes.** Subject: mask portrait ↔ machine/weapon monument ↔ implement flat-lay ↔ ruin or overcast field ↔ body-horror figure ↔ uniformed crowd ↔ high-key masked figure with flowers or signage. Composition: central emblem, edge type bands, grid flat-lay, collage stack, negative-space white field. Lighting: flash, flat scan, overcast daylight, ember glow, blown white. Color handling: red/black duotone, olive monochrome with red star accent, acid yellow on black, bone white with single red. Mood: clinical cold ↔ screamed hot ↔ funereal dread. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**
- Cybergoth rave neon: UV pink/cyan floods, cyberlox dreadfalls, goggles-and-PVC club hedonism, smiley positivity.
- Cyberpunk gloss: rain-slick chrome megacities, holograms, sleek interfaces, magenta-cyan night palettes.
- Clean minimal Swiss design, un-distressed vector flatness, glossy commercial finish.
- Warm pastoral earth tones, soft bokeh lifestyle photography, cozy domestic light.
- Utopian or aspirational technology imagery (chrome robots, bright labs, hopeful futures).
- Partisan political propaganda or literal hate symbols — the aggression is theatrical, not agenda-driven.
- Desert-scavenger post-apocalypse in sandy rust browns without the graphic-design/pictogram layer.
- Bare EBM masculinity (crew cuts, bomber jackets, camo) presented cleanly with no horror, distortion or biohazard element.
- Hand-drawn charm: watercolor, storybook illustration, organic brushwork.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates it from Aggrotech |
|---|---|
| **Cybergoth** | Neon is UV pink/cyan/green on black latex with dreads, goggles, platform boots; mood is hedonist club future. Aggrotech keeps neon only as acid hazard accent over red/black/olive, and swaps club gear for masks, webbing and gun metal. |
| **Electronic Body Music** | Sparse, minimalist, masculine realism: boots, bomber jackets, crew cuts, camo, clean type. Aggrotech adds gore, medical horror, heavy distortion and pictogram overload to that base. |
| **Cyberpunk** | Sleek, chrome, holographic, rain-neon megacity; technology as spectacle. Aggrotech's technology is analog, painted, rusting machinery and warning signs, printed with xerox grain. |
| **Industrial** | Grey concrete, occult/esoteric sigils, brutalist xerox minimalism, muted rust. Aggrotech is louder: saturated blood red, dance-club stencil/blackletter lockups, gas masks and biohazard trefoils front and center. |
| **Post-Apocalyptic** | Sandy dust, scavenger cosplay, weathered brown rust, survivor realism. Aggrotech ruin is duotoned, stenciled and sign-posted — a graphic-design statement about collapse, not a costume documentary of it. |

**Quick recognition checklist:** black or bone field + one hazard color; gas mask or radiation trefoil; stencil/blackletter distressed type; halftone or xerox grain; military/medical hardware; anonymous or mutilated figure; theatrical dread. Four or more of these, and the image is Aggrotech.
