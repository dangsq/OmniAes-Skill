# Aliencore

Aliencore (also Alienwave) is the aesthetic of the alien as identity: extraterrestrial iconography borrowed from 1950s sci-fi cinema, 1990s conspiracy culture and Y2K futurism, re-worn as a badge of not belonging. It is speculative rather than realistic — the wiki is explicit that its space is synthetic and artificial, not documentary astronomy — and it oscillates between two registers: a melancholic, apathetic outsider mood (the crying alien, the lone silhouette under planets, "I belong wherever I want to be") and a winkingly kitschy one (plush martians, sticker sheets, neon signs, Europop brightness). Both registers share one material rule: nothing looks naturally lit or organically made. Everything glows, foils, prints flat, or degrades like tape.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The color language has two grounded families plus a metallic accent layer, and any correct image sits in one of them:

| Role | Range |
|---|---|
| Void / ground | near-black `#050508`–`#14141F`, usually seeded with starfield noise; alternatively a pale print ground (`#DDE6EE`, `#E8E4F0`) for two-tone riso-style work |
| Alien skin / emissive green | neon green `#39FF14` through mint `#8FF5C4`, `#98E9B0` |
| Violet family | deep violet `#6A3FD8` through lavender `#B49BE8` and lilac haze |
| Magenta / pink | `#FF3DF2`, hot pink `#FF6EC7`, pastel baby pink `#FFB3D9` |
| Cyan / teal | `#00E5FF`, `#4FE3E3`, turquoise for hair and glass |
| Metallic / holo | silver `#C4CCDA`, iridescent foil gradients shifting pink–green–blue |
| Acid accent | chartreuse / acid yellow-green `#D8F32F`, mostly in fashion |

The rule, distilled: color must behave synthetically. Either it *emits* (neon on black, glow-in-the-dark, screen glow) or it *prints* (flat pastel or single-ink shapes on a pale or black ground). A naturalistic daylight spectrum — warm sun, earth tones, atmospheric perspective — is outside the style regardless of subject.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **The alien body and icon.** The grey-alien head is the style's logo: bulbous cranium, enormous black almond eyes, slit nose and mouth. It appears as portrait, silhouette, cartoon character, plush toy, neon outline and line-art stamp. Crucially, the alien doubles for the self — reflected in a mirror, dreamed through a window, captioned with belonging slogans.
2. **UFOs and flying saucers.** Disc silhouettes with under-glow, hovering crafts, abduction beam cones, and saucer imagery on road signs, warning signs and vintage shirts.
3. **Outer space.** Starfields, nebulas, ringed planets, cosmic landscapes; frequently framed as a rectangle inside a room — window, porthole, mirror, screen — opening onto the void.
4. **Synthetic/digital layer.** Circuit-like patterns, abstract digital shapes, glitch and scanline effects, pixel dithering; the wiki ties these to cyberpunk and early-internet graphics. (In the image sample this layer shows up mostly as VHS degradation and dithered starfields rather than literal circuitry — treat circuit patterns as licensed but optional.)
5. **Glow and blacklight culture.** Neon tube signage (alien heads, saturns, moons), glow-in-the-dark toys, blacklight posters.
6. **Conspiracy ephemera and fashion.** Tinfoil hats, antennae headbands, vintage UFO tees, warning signage, deadpan slogans in the "I want to believe" / "humans aren't real" lineage.
7. **Kitsch merch and graphic design.** Martian plushies, sticker sheets, packaging-style graphics (space-food cartons, Japanese-type kitsch), holographic cosmetics, and square-tile moodboard collages.

### Materials and textures

Holographic and iridescent foil; metallic laminate and silver vinyl; glossy PVC; plush minky fabric; neon glass; flock and blacklight poster grain; risograph/screenprint grain with one or two inks; VHS scanlines, chromatic aberration and tracking noise; starfield film grain. Finishes are shiny, emissive or deliberately lo-fi — never matte-organic.

### Subjects, composition, lighting

Subjects range from alien character portraits and human–alien encounters (mirror reflections, bedroom windows, beam abductions) to fashion looks (holo dresses, alien-print skirts, antennae and tinfoil), merch and packaging graphics, neon sign photography, lone figures before cosmic vistas, slogan typography posters, and collage grids. Composition tends toward icon-like frontality and symmetry (centered alien head, centered disc), or extreme scale contrast (a tiny figure against a vast starfield) to carry the isolation theme; interiors use a rectangular aperture — mirror, window, monitor — as the portal device. Lighting is self-emissive: neon tubes, blacklight, glow-in-the-dark plastic, monitor glow, beam cones, holographic specular rainbows; illustration and print work uses flat, shadowless even light; snapshot fashion uses direct lo-fi flash. There is no golden hour here.

## Mood & Values

The wiki's key values — apathy, exploration, individualism, mystery — surface as a cool, detached melancholy: the alien crying with a cigarette, the girl dreaming of a porthole cosmos, slogans about belonging nowhere and everywhere. Against that sits playful kitsch and conspiracy parody, paranoia worn as jewelry rather than fear. The style values the outsider, the weird girl, the one who doesn't fit; it reclaim(s) UFO imagery as nostalgic yet forward-looking, and treats synthetic artificiality as beauty, not falseness.

## Era & Origins

Infobox origin: the 1990s. The deeper lineage runs from 1950s Cold War anxiety and the California sci-fi boom (*Invasion of the Saucer Men*, *It Came from Outer Space*) through Space Age, googie, Atompunk and Retrofuturism, into Y2K Futurism filtered by late-90s technology and early-internet sci-fi fandom; recognition arrived late 1990s–early 2000s with *The X-Files*, *Invader Zim*, *Roswell* and sticker-sheet merch. A 2020s resurgence on TikTok and Instagram added holographic cosmetics and kitschy green alien motifs. The style reacts against mainstream belonging and against realistic space representation: its cosmos is deliberately speculative, commercial and personal.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Set the ground first: near-black void with star noise, or pale/pastel print ground; then place emissive or flat-printed color on it.
2. Include at least one load-bearing otherworldly signifier — grey-alien head, saucer, beam, starfield portal — plus one synthetic finish (glow, holo, grain, scanline, flat ink).
3. Let the alien function as self or outsider, even when the subject is an object or a room.
4. Choose a mood register deliberately: melancholic isolation, kitschy play, or conspiratorial wink; mixed registers are fine, naturalism is not.
5. Optional short slogans in pixel, hand-drawn or retro display type are idiomatic ("I want to believe" lineage), never long copy.

**Variation axes.** Subject: alien portrait, human–alien encounter, fashion look, merch/packaging graphic, neon sign, cosmic vista, typography poster, collage grid, interior with portal. Medium: flat digital illustration, two-tone riso print, neon photography, holo fashion photo, VHS glitch frame, vector sticker graphic. Color handling: neon-on-black, pastel-on-black, ink-on-pale, silver-lavender holographic, acid-chartreuse fashion. Lighting: neon, blacklight, screen glow, beam cone, flat print light, lo-fi flash. Mood: apathy ↔ kitsch ↔ paranoia-play.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A holographic sneaker product shot, a riso print of an alien waiting at a laundromat, or a neon saucer over an empty highway all read as Aliencore if the palette, finish and outsider mood hold.

**Avoid.** Naturalistic daylight, beige/earth/boho palettes, matte organic materials (linen, wood, rattan, dried flowers); terrestrial folklore cryptids in wooded flash-photo settings; mid-century chrome, tailfins and googie diners without an alien/synthetic layer; pulp rayguns, tin robots and bubble helmets as the sole subject; organic rainbow swirls, mandalas and hand-painted 60s poster lettering; reverent realistic NASA astronaut/rocket photography with no alien or kitsch element; biomechanical body horror and gore; clean corporate minimalism with matte gradients; golden hour and candlelight.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Aliencore |
|---|---|
| Spacecore | Spacecore worships real spaceflight — astronauts, rockets, photographic sublime, often white/blue/orange. Aliencore centers extraterrestrial *life* as persona, with neon-on-black synthetic color and kitsch conspiracy ephemera. |
| Cryptidcore | Cryptidcore is terrestrial folklore (Mothman, Bigfoot) in rural/forest settings with grainy analog flash and earth tones. Aliencore's creatures are extraterrestrial, its settings digital, bedroom or void, its palette neon and holographic. |
| Atompunk | Atompunk is 1950s retrofuturist optimism: chrome, googie, diner signage, cream-orange-teal period ad style. Aliencore borrows the saucer silhouette but filters it through 90s–Y2K digital culture, glitch and alien-as-identity melancholy. |
| Psychedelia | Psychedelia is organic: swirling rainbow gradients, mandalas, hand-lettered 60s posters. Aliencore shares only the blacklight-poster lineage; its geometry is flat cartoon or digital glitch, its subject alien iconography, its finish synthetic. |
| Raygun Gothic | Raygun Gothic is pulp hardware — rayguns, finned rockets, printed magazine texture, primary colors. Aliencore is post-*X-Files* conspiracy and Y2K fashion: alien bodies, holo foil, neon, not tin gadgets. |

When in doubt, apply the two-part test: does the alien (or its saucer/void) stand in for a self or outsider, and does every surface glow, foil, print flat or degrade like tape? If yes to both, it is Aliencore.
