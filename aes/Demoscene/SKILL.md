# Demoscene

The Demoscene is the visual culture of a non-commercial underground of programmer-artists, centred in Europe, who since the 1980s have shipped "demos": self-contained, non-interactive audiovisual programs that render every pixel in real time from mathematical code rather than pre-rendered assets. Its aesthetic inverts the usual artistic question — not "what technology does my concept need?" but "what feat can this hardware, with its hard physical limits, be made to perform?" The look follows from that philosophy: art that flaunts its own computation. Pixel grids, ordered dither, mathematically perfect repeating patterns, chrome group logos and scrolling greeting tickers are not decoration; they are proof of work, the residue of a program performing live under a 64k or 4k file-size budget.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule: saturated RGB-spectrum primaries and secondaries suspended on a dominant black void, accented by metallic bevel greys and hot sunset gradient ramps. Anchor points:

| Role | Range |
|---|---|
| Void background | #000000 – #0A0A14, the default field and negative space |
| Signature pair | electric blue #0030FF – #00BFFF; neon green #00FF41 – #39FF14 |
| Full-RGB accents | magenta #FF00FF, cyan #00FFFF, signal red #C00000 – #FF0000, yellow #FFFF00 |
| Copper / sunset ramp | indigo #200080 through red-orange #FF4000 – #FF6A00 to yellow #FFC400, used in horizon bands and stacked bars |
| Chrome and bevel metal | #F0F0F0 – #585860 stepped gradients for logos and badges |
| Optional flat field colors | desaturated olive #A8B878, dusty teal, muted purple for surreal tableau backdrops — always posterized flat, never gradient-soft |

Palette discipline is part of the style: colors are counted like a budget. Gradients arrive as discrete bands or dither steps, never as smooth interpolation; mid-tones are simulated with checkerboard and Bayer dither patterns.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the culture documents them

1. **Procedural geometry and real-time 3D complexity** — rotating polyhedra, tunnels, parametric curves, grids and fractal-like repetition; shapes that read as generated by formula, not drawn by hand.
2. **Intense color manipulation** — plasma fields, banded gradient ramps, interference and moiré patterns such as concentric "eye" rings, raster line effects.
3. **Constraint-aesthetic surfaces** — low color counts, visible pixel grids, character-cell mosaics (PETSCII blocks, teletext glyph art), dithered pixel-art portraits.
4. **Audiovisual synchronization rhythm** — motifs repeating at beat-like regularity (rows of spheres, pulses, equalizer-style bars), the frozen frame of a music-locked sequence.
5. **Surreal demo short-film tableaux** — low-color-count rendered figures staged with dreamlike literalness: a figure standing on dithered water under a flat sky, flat-shaded low-poly busts, portraits crowned with radiating ray halos.
6. **Demo-screen grammar** — metallic or gothic bevelled group logos and emblems, bottom scrolling text tickers in pixel fonts (greetings, boasts, effect names), status bars and copper bars framing the top edge like a running program.
7. **16-bit era effect vocabulary** — starfields over horizon gradients, checkerboard or grid floors receding in one-point perspective, shaded bouncing spheres with hard specular dots.

### Materials and textures

Ordered dither (Bayer, checkerboard) is the universal mid-tone material. Hard pixel edges; character-cell glyph blocks with visible mosaic seams; metallic bevel gradients on logos; moiré and interference ring patterns; plasma smears rendered in banded steps; flat posterized planes on low-poly or vectorized figures. Faint scanlines or CRT glow are period-plausible but optional. There are no photographic materials here: no paper, canvas, brushstroke, film grain or lens bokeh.

### Recurring subjects

Pure abstract effect fields; geometric structures; portraits and busts (dithered pixel art, PETSCII mosaic, flat-shaded low-poly); surreal figurative tableaux; horizon landscapes of starfield sky over grid sea; text-as-subject character-cell compositions; emblem and logo screens. Human figures appear only as rendered artifacts, never as photographs.

### Composition and lighting

Composition tends toward centered emblems or halos; horizon-split screens with an effect sky above a receding plane; full-bleed effect fields with a focal ring or blob; centered busts with radial bursts; framed demo-screen layouts with ticker bands top and bottom. Symmetry and radial order dominate; perspective, when present, is one-point and mathematical (grid floors, tunnels). Lighting is simulated, never photographic: emissive neon glowing out of black, hard specular highlights on shaded spheres and chrome bevels, flat ambient light on posterized planes, gradient skies acting as backlight. Shadows are hard-edged, dithered, or absent.

## Mood & Values

Competitive virtuosity performed as generosity: boasting text ("greetings", effect labels, crew credits) coexists with a gift-culture non-commercialism. The mood ranges from triumphant and kinetic to surreal and contemplative, but always carries constraint-pride — maximum spectacle from minimum means — and a machine-room wonder of starfields, glowing geometry and live computation. Nostalgia is present but secondary to present-tense technical pride.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in 1980s Europe from the software cracking scene: crack groups attached artistic "intros" to cracked software to claim credit, and the intro grew into an independent art form judged at parties and compos by an international network of sceners. Platforms define the generations: Commodore 64 and Atari ST character-cell and sprite looks, Commodore Amiga copper bars and dithered renders, modern PC shader work. The Constraint Aesthetic — whole audiovisual worlds packed into 64k or 4k executables — is the philosophical core.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

1. Generate, don't draw: every shape should look formula-born — repetition, symmetry, parametric curves, raster-line math.
2. Budget the colors: choose a small palette and reach mid-tones through dither or banding, never smooth interpolation.
3. Keep pixel integrity: either a visible low-res pixel/character grid or crisp flat vector planes; nothing in between.
4. Light from within: black field plus emissive color, or simulated specular and flat shading; no photographic light rigs.
5. Speak screen grammar when useful — logo, ticker, greetings — but a pure abstract effect field with no text is equally valid.
6. Imply synchronization: rhythmically repeated elements so the frame reads as one beat of a music-locked sequence.

Variation axes:

- **Subject**: abstract effect field ↔ geometric structure ↔ dithered portrait ↔ surreal figurative tableau ↔ character-cell text art ↔ horizon landscape.
- **Composition**: full-bleed field, centered emblem or halo, horizon split, one-point tunnel/grid, framed demo screen with bands.
- **Color handling**: pure RGB on black; copper sunset ramp; muted dithered naturalism (olive sky, violet sea); iridescent holographic ray bursts; monochrome-plus-one-accent character cells.
- **Lighting**: emissive neon, hard specular, flat ambient posterization, gradient-sky backlight.
- **Mood**: triumphant, playful-boastful, surreal-dreamlike, cold-mathematical.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A dithered octopus, a chrome logo over a checkerboard sea, and a moiré ring on black are equally demoscene because they share palette discipline, generated geometry and computation-showing surface. Note honestly: sampled evidence skews to the 8-/16-bit era (pixel art, PETSCII, Amiga stills); modern shader-era fractal flights are within the documented range but under-evidenced, so admit them only when paired with demoscene grammar.

Avoid:

- Photorealism: soft global illumination, depth of field, film grain, realistic skin/hair/fabric, subsurface scattering.
- Smooth continuous gradients without banding or dither; airbrushed gloss.
- Corruption as aesthetic: datamoshing, channel-shift accidents, broken compression artifacts, torn seams — demoscene "noise" is calculated pattern, not error.
- Vaporwave props: classical statues, pastel marble, palm-lined malls, Japanese retail typography, faded VHS pastels.
- Handmade surfaces: brushstrokes, paper, canvas, ink lines, print halftone dots (dither is digital checkerboard, not print halftone).
- Corporate modern UI: glassmorphism, flat-design icons, minimalist sans layouts, soft drop shadows.
- Organic psychedelic ornament: tie-dye swirls, mandala flora, melting acid forms.
- Unconstrained maximal detail: if it looks like it required gigabytes of assets, it breaks the constraint aesthetic.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Cyberdelia** shares neon-on-black rave psychedelia, but its forms are organic and hand-psychedelic — melting swirls, mandala flora, acid ornament — while demoscene forms are machine-precise: pixel grids, character cells, periodic raster effects, credit and scroll-text grammar. Cyberdelia never dithers on a budget.
- **Glitch Art** celebrates the accident: corrupted seams, channel shift, compression wreckage. Demoscene patterns may look noisy (plasma, moiré) but are perfectly controlled, periodic and coherent as a running screen; its artifacts are deliberate dither and pixel grids.
- **Net.art** is browser-era, conceptual and deadpan — web-UI appropriation, HTML minimalism, irony. Demoscene is pre-web, offline, executable virtuosity: maximalist effects and competitive credits that flaunt skill where net.art deflects it.
- **Silicon Dreams** airbrushes chrome and glass into smooth idealized futurist gloss; demoscene keeps computation visible — banded gradients, dither, pixel edges, black-dominated RGB primaries — and its chrome is a bevelled pixel logo, not an airbrushed spaceship.
- **Vaporwave** is pastel, faded and consumer-ironic (malls, statues, elevator-muzak mood); demoscene is saturated, high-contrast and earnestly technical. Vaporwave nostalgizes buying; demoscene nostalgizes making.
