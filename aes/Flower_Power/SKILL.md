# Flower Power

Flower Power began as a protest tactic — Allen Ginsberg's 1965 call for marchers to carry "masses of flowers" to hand to police, press and politicians — and hardened into a full youth-culture look by the Summer of Love in 1967. Visually it is psychedelia filtered through the botanical: the liquid color-trips of the poster scene restrained and sweetened into daisies, gardens and pastoral daylight. It lives in two material registers that share one palette and one mood. The **graphic register** is album sleeves, posters and advertisements: flat saturated color fields, hand-painted curvy lettering with paisley and petal infill, portraits collaged as if blooming from stems. The **photographic register** is sun-bleached color film: young people with flowers in their hair, flowered mini-dresses, garlands and beads, shot outdoors in flaring light or posed in soft studio pastels. Both registers treat the flower as an actor, never as background decoration.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

Two families, one rule: every hue must read either as hand-mixed poster paint at full saturation or as sun-faded film stock. The movement's stated key colours are red, yellow and blue; in practice the floral-psychedelic interpretation widens that triad into a garden range.

- **Saturated poster family:** poppy red (#D93A2B–#C22E24), magenta-fuchsia (#C93A9C–#E858B8), marigold and acid yellow (#F2C230–#E8E26A), lime and leaf green (#8DC63F–#B5D334), violet-purple (#5C3A8E–#7B4FA6), tangerine (#E8703A), cobalt-sky blue (#2E6DB4–#4FA3D9). These sit on white, cream or black grounds with no shading and no gradient.
- **Sun-bleached film family:** cream (#F2E8D5), blush pink (#E8C8C0), pale lavender (#D8C8E0), faded gold (#D9B36A), washed sage (#BFD9D2), warm chestnut hair browns (#6B4A32). Highlights blow out to paper white; shadows stay milky, never black.

Green foliage is the constant bridge between the two families. A correct image may lean entirely into either family, or place a saturated graphic element (a painted sign, a printed dress) inside a faded photographic scene.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the movement documents them

1. **The flower as prop and gesture.** Simple-petaled meadow flowers — daisies above all, plus carnations, marigolds, sunflowers, chrysanthemums, wildflowers — held in the hand, pressed to the lips or over the face, tucked behind the ear, offered outward toward a viewer, a camera, or a uniformed soldier.
2. **Flowers worn on the body:** woven daisy crowns, garlands and leis around the neck, sprigs pinned or caught in long hair and fringe bangs.
3. **Lush gardens and pastoral settings:** meadows, leafy dappled backdrops, fields of blooms, domestic rooms brought indoors with vases and potted stems.
4. **Floral psychedelia in graphic form:** hand-lettered words whose letters swell, curl and fill with paisley, petals and spirals; flat pattern fields of blossoms; the conceit of a portrait photograph blooming from a planted stem like a flower head.
5. **Floral fashion:** cotton and chiffon floral-print mini-dresses and maxi-dresses, matching headscarves and turbans, paisley shirts, layered bead-and-coin necklaces, occasional petal-or-star face paint on the cheek.
6. **The peace confrontation:** a single unarmed young person or a flower-bearing crowd facing helmets, bayonets and uniform rows — the flower as the only weapon.
7. **Summer light itself:** flare, sparkle and golden glare standing in for the Summer of Love.

Secondary and image-attested rather than text-defined: painted facial petals, coin pendants, tender domestic interiors stuffed with blooms and soft toys. Period press documentation sometimes appears in black and white, but the style's identity is carried by color; treat monochrome as an archival exception, not the norm.

### Materials and textures

Real stems and petals with visible veins and slightly bruised edges; crowns wired or woven from fresh greens; printed cotton, chiffon and paisley weave; glass beads and stamped metal coins; hand-painted board and screen-printed poster paper with slight misregistration; color film with grain, halation around highlights, and faded, warm-shifted emulsion; the thin printed border of a record sleeve or magazine page.

### Recurring subjects

Young women and men with long hair, center partings or heavy bangs; bands and choruses; fashion models in catalog poses; protesters and, as their opposite, rows of police or soldiers; gardeners and flower-sellers; crowds with raised hands; close crops of a face half-hidden by a bloom.

### Composition and lighting

Graphic register: frontal and flat, white or single-color ground, ornamental lettering as the hero element, portraits inserted as ovals or cutouts, botanical elements arranged symmetrically or as all-over pattern, no cast shadows. Photographic register: intimate close crops in profile or three-quarter view; full-length catalog figures against plain studio backdrops or brick walls; wide crowd shots with flare. Light is natural and unforced — backlit golden hour, dappled leaf shade, overcast pastel softness — with highlights allowed to burn out and focus allowed to soften. Flash-hard lighting and crisp deep focus belong to other styles.

## Mood & Values

Non-violence and utopianism are the declared values, and the look performs them: tenderness, playfulness, wide-eyed sincerity, communal joy, a gentle defiance that answers bayonets with a daisy. There is innocence without naivety-of-parody — the movement was mocked in its own time as commercialized ("flower power is the new way of wrapping garbage"), yet the images never turn cynical. Softness is strength; the garden is the argument.

## Era & Origins

United States, 1965–1969. Ginsberg's essay (November 1965) supplies the tactic; California — San Francisco, Berkeley, Laurel Canyon — supplies the look from 1966; the term "flower child" arrives in 1967 alongside the Haight-Ashbury Summer of Love and the March on the Pentagon. It is the bright early face of the West Coast hippie scene and the direct precursor that commerce later flattened into Corporate Hippie. It reacts against the Vietnam War and against gray postwar conformity.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Make the flower an actor: held, worn, offered, or blooming — never mere wallpaper.
2. Pick one register (flat poster or sun-bleached photograph) and keep its materiality consistent throughout.
3. Obey the palette rule: full-saturation hand-mixed poster hues or faded film pastels, anchored on red/yellow/blue with green foliage as bridge.
4. Let sunlight co-star: flare, blown highlights, dappled shade; or, in the graphic register, shadowless even print light.
5. If type appears, it is hand-painted, curvy, swollen, paisley-filled; no clean geometric sans.
6. Keep the mood earnest and gentle, even in protest scenes.
7. Honor period materiality: film grain, milky fades, slight print misregister, sleeve or page edges.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a portrait, a couple, a band, a crowd, a protest line, a garden still life, a fashion catalog figure, or an invented album sleeve. Composition may be a tight profile, a full-length studio pose, a flat frontal graphic layout, or a wide street scene. Lighting may be golden backlight, leaf-dapple, overcast pastel, or flat print evenness. Color handling may sit anywhere between the saturated and faded families, including rare archival monochrome. Mood may range from tender to jubilant to quietly defiant. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**

- Tie-dye spirals, fringed suede, denim, earth-brown rusticism and festival mud (that is Hippie, not Flower Power).
- Brand logos, airbrushed slickness, vector-clean gradients (Corporate Hippie).
- Modern digital sharpness, HDR, seamless studio gradients, LED or neon-cyber color.
- Tailored dandy suiting, velvet and ruffles without floral content (Peacock Revolution).
- Medieval or folk costume, tapestries, heraldry, lutes (Mid-Century Medieval).
- Crisp contemporary revival production: perfect modern haircuts, digital type, sterile retouching (Groovival).
- Formal floristry: roses in vases, bridal bouquets, tropical monstera or succulents.
- Cynical, ironic or aggressive tone; weapons in the protagonist's hands (weapons only as the confronted opposite).
- Minimalist sans-serif typography and black-dominant gothic palettes.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | How Flower Power differs |
|---|---|
| **Peacock Revolution** | Same years, but Peacock is a menswear dandyism — flamboyant tailoring, velvet, ruffles — with flowers optional; Flower Power is botanically literal, pastoral, and includes women, crowds and poster graphics as core. |
| **Corporate Hippie** | The sold-out offshoot: logos, slick airbrush, brand messaging. Flower Power keeps handmade lettering, real stems, film fade and a peace-movement context; any visible branding pushes an image into Corporate Hippie. |
| **Groovival** | A later revival wearing the costume with modern production values. Flower Power shows period materiality — grain, fade, misregister, period haircuts — where Groovival looks crisply contemporary. |
| **Hippie** | Broader, later, earthier: tie-dye, denim, fringe, mud, tribal layering, darker earth tones. Flower Power is earlier, brighter, cleaner, daisy-specific, with mod silhouettes (A-line minis, headscarves) and saturated poster color. |
| **Mid-Century Medieval** | Shares long hair, flower crowns and pastoral romance, but grounds them in pre-Raphaelite costume, tapestry and heraldry. Flower Power's romance is botanical and psychedelic, never feudal. |

Against its parent **Psychedelia**, the test is daylight and botany: general psychedelia may dissolve into liquid light, fractals and non-floral ornament, while Flower Power always keeps a recognizable flower, a garden, or a hand-painted blossom-lettered word at its center.
