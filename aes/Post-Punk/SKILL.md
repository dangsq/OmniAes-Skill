# Post-Punk

Post-Punk is the visual culture of the rock movement that emerged in the late 1970s in Britain and America directly after — and in deliberate departure from — first-wave punk. Where punk shouted, post-punk withdrew: the aesthetic rejects studs, leather and mohawks for a subdued, androgynous, monochromatic look built from second-hand and army-surplus clothing, described by writer Mimi Haddon as "Eastern Bloc chic," an anti-rockstar "non-image" mirroring Cold War-era working-class attire in Europe and Britain. Its other half is intellectual: British art schools, existentialist literature (Kafka, Sartre), and the avant-garde geometry of German Expressionism, Bauhaus and Dada, which produced the stark, minimal graphic design most famously associated with Peter Saville's work for Factory Records. The result is a style that is simultaneously utilitarian and cerebral — drab overcoats on one side, abstract waveform-on-black sleeve art on the other — and that later seeded the Goth subculture when figures like Siouxsie Sioux and Robert Smith added eyeliner, dark lipstick and teased hair to the austere base.

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule

Build every image on a monochrome spine, then allow at most one drab secondary family and, rarely, a single cold shock of accent:

| Family | Hex range | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Near-black | #0B0B0C–#1C1C1E | Dominant: coats, shirts, jeans, graphic fields |
| Charcoal to smoke grey | #2E2E30–#7A7A7C | Suits, knitwear, concrete, halftone |
| Bone / off-white | #D9D4CA–#EAE6DE | Walls, paper, shirt collars, negative space |
| Sepia / drab brown | #4B3A2B–#8C7050 | Trench cloth, warm monochrome toning, faded prints |
| Grey-blue / olive drab | #46525C–#6E7A84 | Surplus cloth, overcast interiors, muted color shots |
| Pale brick beige | #C7B49E–#D6C6B0 | Background masonry, plaster |
| Oxblood / crimson accent | #6E1A20–#A32328 | Rare: dyed hair, lipstick, a flash-flared artifact |

Saturation stays low everywhere; color images should read like faded analog prints whose dyes have cooled toward grey. Pure black-and-white and warm sepia monochrome are equally valid handlings of the same rule.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how firmly the text documents them)

1. **Monochromatic minimalism** — the governing principle; whole outfits and whole graphics held in one value family.
2. **Trench coats and drab overcoats** — double-breasted, belted, often ill-fitting; the style's signature silhouette.
3. **Button-up shirts, slim or ill-fitting trousers** — plain poplin shirts, narrow dark jeans, simple trousers; tailored blazers and wool jumpers as layering; a skinny tie as the overlap with American new wave.
4. **Practical footwear** — Dr. Martens, desert boots, black Oxford shoes; never decorative.
5. **Abstract geometric design** — album sleeves and promo material of stark lines, plots, grids and diagrams on empty fields; the Bauhaus/Dada/Expressionist lineage, the Factory Records look.
6. **Architectural lines and working-class settings** — brick walls, concrete, terraced streets, plain plaster interiors; the built environment as flat backdrop.
7. **Second-hand / army-surplus layering and DIY construction** — visibly worn, mismatched, anti-commercial clothing.
8. **Androgynous short hair and stark, near-corporate deadpan** — the "anti-rockstar" posture; cropped cuts, neat-ish shags, blank or averted gazes.
9. **Late theatrical variant** — heavy black eyeliner, dark lipstick, voluminous teased hair (the Siouxsie/Smith turn that becomes Goth); an optional register, not the baseline.

### Materials and textures

Wool gabardine and cotton trench cloth with matte creases; coarse-gauge knit jumpers; poplin shirts; dull satin sheen on the occasional bomber or lining; rubber Dr. Martens soles and worn black leather. Surfaces around the body are porous and municipal: painted brick, raw concrete, plaster, asphalt. The image surface itself is analog: 35mm grain, faded print dyes, hard flash falloff, xerox/offset halftone in printed matter, screenprint flatness in flyers and sleeves.

### Recurring subjects

Band group portraits standing in a row; lone candid figures mid-gesture or mid-speech; street and backyard snapshots; fashion editorial pages and diagrammatic fashion plates with clinical annotations; abstract sleeve graphics (line plots, waveforms, geometric fields on black); rehearsal rooms and plain interiors. Subjects are ordinary-looking by intent — clerks, art students, conscripts — not stars.

### Composition and lighting

Composition favors flatness and linearity: horizontal line-ups against a wall, vertical candid crops, diagrammatic grids, or a single small abstract object centered in a large black field with generous negative space and minimal or absent typography. Poses are deadpan, awkward, leaning, hands in pockets; gazes blank, averted, or caught off-guard. Lighting is anti-dramatic: overcast flat daylight, direct on-camera flash throwing a hard shadow on the wall behind, soft diffuse window light indoors, or high-contrast black-and-white and sepia toning in print. No rim light, no golden hour, no glamour key.

## Mood & Values

Introspection, social alienation and existential dread carried with dry intellectual composure. The style values DIY self-sufficiency applied to complex, personal, non-commercial expression; art-school curiosity; restraint as a form of rebellion. Its emotional temperature is cold, wry, and austere — melancholy without self-pity, severity without aggression. The "non-image" is the point: looking like nothing special is the statement.

## Era & Origins

Late 1970s, United Kingdom and United States, in the wake of punk's first wave. It reacts against punk's raw three-chord formula and its perceived commercialization, and against American rock 'n' roll glamour generally, turning instead to European culture: krautrock and kosmische musik (Kraftwerk, Can, Neu!), German Expressionism, Bauhaus, Dada. British art schools supply the critical-theory backbone. By the early 1980s its theatrical dark variant evolves into Goth; its minimalism echoes onward through Minimal Wave, No Wave and, distantly, Normcore.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Monochrome first: choose one value family and hold the whole image in it; add drab earth or grey-blue only as cloth, never as decoration.
- Analog materiality: grain, faded dye, flash falloff, halftone — the image must look chemically produced, pre-1985.
- Anti-rockstar stance: candid, awkward, deadpan; the subject is observed, not performing.
- Utilitarian-intellectual wardrobe: trench coat, blazer, button-up, wool jumper, slim dark trousers, Dr. Martens / desert boots / Oxfords; silhouettes slightly ill-fitting, second-hand.
- Graphic lineage for printed matter: stark geometry, line plots, diagrams, empty fields, small clinical sans-serif type or no type at all.
- Municipal backdrop: brick, concrete, plaster, street furniture; architecture as flat graphic line.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a group portrait, a solo candid, a street scene, an editorial scan, a diagrammatic plate, an abstract sleeve, a flyer, or an interior — a correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. Composition may run from centered line-ups to off-kilter crops to pure abstraction on black. Lighting may be overcast flat, hard flash, soft window light, or high-contrast monochrome conversion. Color handling may be pure black-and-white, sepia, grey-blue muted color, or faded print color. Mood may slide from dry deadpan to austere melancholy to the late theatrical dark register (eyeliner, teased hair) so long as the utilitarian base garments remain.

**Avoid.**
- Punk hardware: studs, spikes, safety pins, mohawks, ripped leather biker jackets, ransom-note xerox collage, bright anarchic color.
- Goth romance as baseline: lace, velvet, corsetry, crucifixes, Victorian frills, candlelit cemeteries, full dramatic makeup on every figure.
- Deathrock horror kitsch: bat makeup, coffin props, shredded fishnet as costume.
- Industrial debris: rust, metal plating, camouflage, machinery, noise-collage texture.
- Doomer digitality: hoodies, smartphones, filter-desaturated panel-block skylines, lone modern figures.
- Rockstar glamour: stage pyro, lens flares, heroic low angles, smiling crowd energy, glossy studio key light.
- Warm or sweet palettes: autumnal boho, pastels, neon, saturated HDR color, pristine modern minimalism (post-punk minimalism is worn and drab, not sleek).

## Discrimination Cues

- **DIY Punk** shares the ethos and the era but not the restraint: punk is studs, rips, mohawks and confrontational color; post-punk is intact second-hand tailoring, monochrome, and deadpan composure.
- **Deathrock** theatricalizes horror (bat makeup, fishnet, kitsch props); post-punk's dark turn stays inside utilitarian garments and never becomes costume.
- **Goth**, its direct descendant, makes the theatrical variant permanent and adds romantic/fetish materials and occult jewelry; post-punk remains daylight, street-level, wool-and-gabardine, with makeup only as a late optional accent and greys/sepias in the palette rather than total black.
- **Doomer** borrows the grey alienation but is a digital-age mood: hoodies, cigarettes, filter-desaturation, no period garments, no analog grain, no art-design lineage.
- **Industrial** pursues transgressive machine aesthetics — rust, steel, camouflage, factory dystopia; post-punk's abstraction is clean geometry on paper and cloth, not debris and metal.
