# 2 Tone

2 Tone (Two-Tone, Ska Revival) was a music genre and subculture born in Coventry, England in the late 1970s, a second-wave revival of 1960s Jamaican ska fused with punk energy and new wave attitude. Its visual identity is one of the most tightly coded in popular culture: an almost exclusively black-and-white graphic world in which the checkerboard pattern functions as a political emblem of racial unity, the suited Rude Boy figure functions as a mascot, and every surface — record sleeve, poster, shirt, skirt, dancehall floor — can be claimed by the check. The look is sharp, clean and working-class: revived Jamaican Rude Boy tailoring crossed with British Mod and skinhead staples, rendered in monochrome print graphics, grainy flash photography and dense block typography. It is protest dress and dance-craze graphics at once: deadpan faces, stomping loafers, and a checkerboard under everything.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is monochrome first and monochrome last. The working palette is:

- Ink black: `#000000` to `#141414`
- Paper white and off-white/cream stock: `#FFFFFF` down to `#F2EFE6`
- Photographic greys and halftone midtones: roughly `#3A3A3A` to `#B9B9B9`, carried by film grain and print dots rather than smooth gradients
- Optional micro-accent: a single small area of saturated red (around `#C8102E`) — a shirt collar, a heel, a hat band. Evidence for red is thin (a couple of poster variants); treat it as a rare, deliberate exception, never a second palette color.

Any correct image lives inside black + white + grey halftone, optionally on cream paper stock, with at most one token accent. Full-color photography, pastel fields, neon and warm sepia toning are outside the style.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the text documents them)

1. **Checkerboard pattern** — the defining motif, repurposed from Mod culture by Jerry Dammers. Equal black and white squares used as borders framing a poster field, as full floors under standing figures, as narrow strips and bands, as garment panels (skirts, shirt yokes, hat bands), as background fields behind illustrated figures. Scale varies freely: fine checks for frames, bold checks for floors.
2. **The Walt Jabsco Rude Boy figure** — the 2 Tone Records logo character (designed after a photograph of Peter Tosh): black suit, white shirt, thin black tie, pork pie hat, often caught mid-skank. Appears as flat illustration, sticker icon, and as the template for how real people dress and pose.
3. **The dress code** — two-tone suits, thin black ties, button-down shirts, pork pie and trilby hats, Fred Perry polo shirts, Dr. Martens boots or loafers; sharp, clean, pressed. Rude Girl equivalents: skirts with checkerboard panels, neat beehive-or-bob hair, hats and sunglasses.
4. **Band logos and block type** — jagged, angular, hand-cut logotype lettering (sharp diagonal cuts, uneven baselines) paired with condensed block sans-serif capitals stacked in gig-listing columns: band names, venues, dates, "ALBUM OUT NOW ON 2 TONE."
5. **Print carriers** — record sleeves, 7-inch single covers, gig posters, sticker sheets and icon grids of skanking silhouettes, hats, sunglasses and check circles.
6. **The dance itself** — skanking poses: bent elbow, punching arm, kicked-back leg, leaning diagonal body; stomping feet on a check floor.

### Materials and textures

Screen-printed ink on paper; photocopier and halftone dot grain; flat vector-like illustration with hard edges and no gradients; grainy black-and-white film (pushed, contrasty); vinyl sleeve card; wool suits, knit cardigans, leather jackets, polished leather loafers; painted dancehall floors laid out in checks. Texture comes from print process and film grain, never from gloss, chrome or digital sheen.

### Recurring subjects

Multiracial bands and crowds (the mixed lineup is the politics made visible); solo Rude Boy or Rude Girl figures, illustrated or photographed; dancers mid-skank; group portraits deadpan in venues, on urban steps, or dancing against a white studio sweep; object still-lifes of the kit (hat, tie, loafers, vinyl, checkerboard swatch); typographic poster compositions with little or no figure at all; stylized illustrated faces in sunglasses and thin ties.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Poster logic dominates: a white or black field enclosed by a checkerboard border frame; a single figure placed on a strong diagonal against empty space; dense stacked type blocks balanced against large voids; icon-collage grids and sticker-sheet arrangements; checkerboard as ground plane receding under a group. Photography is hard and direct: on-camera flash in cramped interiors, deep shadows, blown highlights, visible grain; or even studio light on a seamless white sweep for group dance shots. Illustration is flat, no modeled light at all — silhouette, white shirt shape, thin tie line. There is no soft ambient lighting, no golden hour, no bokeh.

## Mood & Values

Cool defiance and collective joy held together: deadpan, unsmiling faces beside ecstatic dance poses. The core values are anti-racism and racial unity (the checkerboard's meaning), working-class pride, and sharp social commentary on unemployment, urban decay and Thatcher-era Britain. The mood is urgent but disciplined — "stay rough and tough" — smart dress as dignity, monochrome as solidarity, the dance floor as the place where the argument is won.

## Era & Origins

Coventry, United Kingdom, late 1970s; 2 Tone Records founded 1979 by Jerry Dammers of The Specials; label defunct by 1985. A revival of 1960s Jamaican Rude Boy style and ska rhythm, sped up and hardened by punk and new wave, formed in direct opposition to far-right racial politics of the period. Associated acts: The Specials, The Selecter, The Beat, Madness, Bad Manners, The Bodysnatchers.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

1. Build the image in black, white and print-grey only; decide early whether the ground is paper white, ink black, or cream stock.
2. Let the checkerboard do structural work — frame, floor, band, garment panel — not incidental decoration.
3. Dress every figure by the code: suit jacket, button-down, thin tie, pork pie or trilby hat, loafers or boots; keep it pressed and sharp.
4. Set type as image: jagged cut-logotype for the band name, condensed block capitals stacked for information.
5. If photographing, use hard flash or flat studio light in high-contrast monochrome with grain; if illustrating, use flat hard-edged shapes with no gradients.
6. Pose bodies either deadpan-frontal or mid-skank on a diagonal; nothing in between.
7. Make the cast multiracial whenever people appear — the mixed lineup is the message, not a styling choice.
8. Keep the context British working-class: dancehall, terrace street, venue backroom, white studio sweep.

Variation axes (what may freely change): subject matter (band portrait, solo dancer, poster with no figure, sleeve, sticker sheet, object still-life, crowd); composition (framed checkerboard border, full-bleed check floor, asymmetric void with one diagonal figure, dense collage grid, symmetric type stack); lighting (flat graphic no-light, hard flash, even studio sweep); color handling (pure black/white, cream paper, one red micro-accent or none); mood (bleak social commentary, joyous dance craze, cool defiance); illustration vs photography vs photo-plus-type hybrid. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

Avoid:

- Full-color or saturated palettes, pastels, neon, sepia or warm vintage filters.
- Smooth gradients, airbrushing, chrome, gloss, 3D render sheen.
- Ornate serifs, script lettering, ransom-note punk cut-ups used as the main type voice.
- Punk damage: rips, safety pins, mohawks, bleached denim.
- Mod signage as lead motif: target roundels, Union Jacks, parkas, scooters.
- Tropical reggae clichés: palm trees, beaches, dreadlock-centric imagery, red-gold-green color coding.
- Third-wave ska-punk brightness: cartoon mascots, checkered sneakers as the joke, orange or turquoise fills.
- Soft lifestyle photography: shallow depth of field, golden light, candid laughter.
- Checkerboard used as motorsport or diner retro signage rather than as the unity emblem of a monochrome system.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Rude Boy (1960s Jamaica):** the source of the suit, tie and pork pie hat, but without the checkerboard system, without the Walt Jabsco logo, without block-cap poster type; Jamaican street context, rocksteady stillness, often warmer or duotone photography. 2 Tone adds monochrome print graphics, punk tempo energy and British urban setting.
- **Skinhead:** shares boots, sharp working-class dress and occasional check trim, but centers on braces, cropped hair, button-down polos and tonic suits in actual colors (burgundy, blue, green); lacks the checkerboard-as-emblem field, the skanking pose and the suited Rude Boy mascot; its politics are ambiguous where 2 Tone's are explicitly anti-racist and multiracial.
- **Mod:** 1960s origin of the borrowed checkerboard trim, but Mod's own signs are parkas, scooters, target roundels, Union Jacks and color-block pop palettes; Mod composition is cleaner and popper. 2 Tone makes the check the whole world and adds thin ties, pork pie hats and ska dance iconography.
- **Mod Revival:** the parallel late-1970s scene; smart suits and sharp hair, but no checkerboard dominance, no Rude Boy figure, no monochrome poster system, and a guitar-pop rather than ska posture.
- **New Wave:**同期-era graphics favor neon, pastel, chrome, Memphis grids and studio gloss with angular haircuts and skinny ties in color; 2 Tone refuses color and gloss entirely and keeps its type hand-cut and its photography grainy and flash-lit.

If an image shows a pressed suit, a pork pie hat, a hard black-and-white check and a mixed-race cast — in print graphics or flash photography — it is 2 Tone; remove any one of those and it drifts to a neighbor.
