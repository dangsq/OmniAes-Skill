# Plakatstil (Sachplakat / Object Poster)

Plakatstil is advertising distilled to a noun and a name: one product, one brand, one field of colour. Born in Berlin around 1905–1906 when Lucian Bernhard reduced a match advertisement to two burning matches and the word "Priester", the style was a deliberate revolt against the tangled whiplash ornament of Jugendstil (German Art Nouveau). Where Jugendstil narrated and decorated, Plakatstil stated. The commodity itself — a shoe, a tire, a spark plug, a cigarette pack, a bottle — becomes the entire argument, drawn in flat opaque planes and anchored by lettering that behaves as a second image rather than as caption. The movement was codified through the journal *Das Plakat* (1910–1922) and carried into wartime propaganda, cultural posters and fashion advertising by Bernhard, Hans Rudi Erdt, Ludwig Hohlwein and Julius Klinger, before feeding directly into the Bauhaus and, later, the International Typographic Style. Its legacy is the idea that a poster must be legible in one glance from across a street.

## Visual Identity

### Colour: field-first, counted inks

The textual anchor is explicit: palettes of two to four hues, most commonly red, blue, olive and black, set at high contrast. The operative rule is *field-first*: one hue occupies the ground as an unmodulated plane, and the object plus lettering take the remaining contrasting hues. A fourth hue may appear only as a small accent area — a match tip, a glove, a skating cap, a shoe lining. Every plane is a single ink; hues never blend, gradient or shade into one another.

| Dark grounds | ink black `#14100C`, near-black brown `#1B1512`, deep teal `#123A40`, navy `#1F3A5F`, olive-mustard `#A9832C` / `#6E6B22`, muted lilac-grey `#A29AA3` |
| Light grounds | warm paper cream `#EFE6D2`, pale grey-green `#C7D0C6`, off-white `#F4F1E6` |
| Signal hues | vermilion `#C8102E`, crimson `#D22B2B`, orange `#E4671E`, cobalt `#2A4B8D`, teal-green `#3E8E8C`, chrome yellow `#F2C230` |
| Object neutrals | flat white `#F6F3EC`, cool grey `#8C8F92`, warm beige `#D9C6A5`, pale pink `#E5B4B4` |

Dark ground with light object and light ground with dark object are equally valid; so are saturated vermilion fields and dusty grey-lilac fields. What is invariant is the *count* and the *flatness*, not the specific hue. (The wiki names red, blue, olive and black as the common hues; the wider hex ranges above are distilled from surviving posters of the period, and muted fashion-poster grounds sit at the quiet end of the range.)

### Motifs, ranked by how the text documents them

1. **The isolated object.** The hero product, drawn large and alone: matches, a shoe, a pneumatic tire, a spark plug, a typewriter, a cigarette pack or carton, a liqueur bottle, a chandelier, a coffee package with spilled beans. Objects are described with basic shapes and their simplest outlines — recognisable, never abstracted beyond readability.
2. **Bold brand lettering as co-image.** The brand name, and usually nothing else, set large and integrated into the composition's mass balance.
3. **Plain negative space.** The empty field is an active compositional element, not leftover background.
4. **The reduced human figure** (secondary in the text, frequent in the corpus): dandies, waiters, coachmen, speed skaters, helmeted soldiers, motorists. Figures are simplified to silhouette-like planes; faces carry two, at most three, flat tonal steps; clothing becomes flat pattern — pinstripe, check, houndstooth — applied like wallpaper across the silhouette.
5. **Structural rule-lines:** lettering bands at top or bottom, corner blocks, occasional geometric frame borders with squared corner motifs. Structure, never curvilinear ornament.
6. **Absence as motif:** no slogan, no scenery, no decorative detail, no narrative props.

### Typography

Lettering is simplified, clear and direct, meant to carry the message alone; sans-serif forms are favoured for distance readability, but the lettering is *drawn*, not typeset neutrally: chunky geometric capitals, squared display sans, occasionally calligraphic script or blackletter for brand character. Erdt's "textual letter" pushes further, fusing letterform and image so a letter becomes the picture (a U-boat silhouette performing the letter "U"). The brand name may sit in a top or bottom band, lock into a corner block, or overlap and touch the object; a small caps sub-block (address, product line) is the maximum permitted extra copy. Lettering always uses inks already present in the image.

### Material, texture, lighting

Matte opaque lithographic inks on poster paper: slight paper grain, faint ink absorption at plane edges, crisp but hand-drawn contours with a hair of wobble, occasional whisper of misregistration between colour planes. There is no ambient light. Form is explained by discrete tonal steps — a shadow side one value darker, a lit side one value lighter — never by gradient, airbrush or soft shadow. Cast shadows appear only as flat declared shapes; highlights only as flat patches (a white lamp globe, a yellow match tip).

### Composition

One hero, centered or tilted on a diagonal; the object may crop the frame. Figure variants allow dramatic foreshortening and steep perspective (Hohlwein's leaning motorists, crouching skaters) while remaining flat-plane drawings. The lettering mass counterweights the object mass; vertical poster format dominates, horizontal bands exist. Negative space is left deliberately empty — including reserved blank panels framed by rule-lines.

## Mood & Values

Clarity, directness, modernity, readability, impact. The mood is the confidence of the plain statement: the product needs no allegory, no cherubs, no landscape, because the commodity itself is the promise. Fashion and lifestyle variants add dandyish poise and dry elegance; Hohlwein introduces irony and humour (the overcoat-clad motorist, the waiter's deadpan tray); wartime and propaganda variants turn the same directness into urgency and accusation (the helmeted head, the shouted "U Boote heraus!"). Across all registers the shared value is economy as respect for the viewer: one glance, one message.

## Era & Origins

Germany, 1905–1906 onward, Berlin first. Bernhard's Priester match poster (contest entry of 1905) is the founding gesture; the style spread through commercial commission, the journal *Das Plakat* (1910–1922, published by the Verein der Plakatfreunde with Bernhard as director), and the work of Erdt, Hohlwein, Klinger and a wider circle (Gipkens, Scheurich, Leupin, Baumberger and others). It reacted against Jugendstil ornament, absorbed Modernist reduction, served Imperial German WWI propaganda, and later fed the Bauhaus and Swiss graphic design. Its typefaces were eventually banned by the Nazi regime on the false belief that Bernhard was Jewish — a footnote that fixes the style firmly in the Wilhelmine-to-Weimar window. Period voice: commercial stone-and-paper lithography, 1900s–1920s.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. **Field first.** Choose one ground hue and leave it unmodulated edge to edge; the emptiness is the composition's lung.
2. **One hero.** A single product object, or a single reduced figure, or a letterform-as-object. Nothing narrative beside it.
3. **Plane logic.** Build every form from flat opaque shapes; model with at most one or two tonal steps of the same hue; edges crisp, hand-drawn, slightly imperfect.
4. **Count your inks.** Two to four hues plus a paper neutral. A small accent area may spend the fourth hue; nothing else may.
5. **Brand as image.** Set the brand name large, drawn, integrated — band, corner block, or overlapping the object. No slogan, no paragraph, no secondary typeface competing.
6. **Litho body.** Matte ink, paper grain, faint misregistration; no gloss, no gradient, no photographic texture.
7. **Period hand.** Draw as a 1900s–1920s commercial lithographer: descriptive simplification, not contemporary vector iconography.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter: any commodity or message — footwear, beverages, food, machines, tires, textiles, fashion, transport, sport events, cultural programmes, political or wartime calls; even a present-day product is admissible when rendered in this period language, because the style is a rule-set, not a subject list. Composition: object centered; object on a diagonal crossed by a lettering band; figure plus object; letterform fused with object; corner-locked lettering block; framed with geometric rule-lines or frameless. Lighting/modeling: wholly flat, or two-value tonal steps, or dramatic foreshortened perspective in the Hohlwein manner — always flat planes. Colour handling: dark field with light hero or light field with dark hero; saturated vermilion and orange registers or muted lilac-grey and olive registers. Mood: commercial poise, dandyish elegance, dry humour, or urgent proclamation. Format: vertical column poster dominant, horizontal acceptable. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a poster for an object that appears nowhere in the historical corpus is fully correct if it obeys field, plane, ink-count and lettering rules.

**Avoid (style-breakers)**

- Gradients, airbrushing, soft cast shadows, glossy highlights, 3D rendering, photorealism, photomontage, halftone photo reproduction.
- Jugendstil/Art Nouveau ornament: whiplash curves, floral filigree, decorative borders, allegorical figures.
- Complex scenery, crowds, interiors with perspective depth, narrative vignettes; landscape only as a simplified flat band (treeline, skyline strip) if at all.
- Slogans, body copy, paragraphs, multiple competing typefaces; any text beyond brand plus at most a small caps sub-block.
- More than four hues; rainbow or neon digital palettes; hue blending within a plane.
- Drop shadows, bevels, glows, rounded vector-icon crispness of digital flat design.
- Swiss-grid asymmetry with photography; pure geometric abstraction with no product (Bauhaus/Constructivist composition); UI-style iconography.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | Its tell | Plakatstil's counter-tell |
|---|---|---|
| International Typographic Style (Swiss) | Photography or pure typography on white, asymmetric grid, objective neutral sans, no illustrated hero | Hand-drawn isolated product as hero, coloured ground, drawn display lettering fused with the image |
| Minimalism | Contemporary reduction for concept's sake, vector-clean, often brandless | Always advertises something: a named brand, a lithographic object, period print grain |
| Bauhaus | Geometric construction, primary colours, experimental lowercase type, abstract composition | The commodity stays recognisable and central; geometry serves description, not construction |
| Constructivism | Dynamic diagonals, photomontage, red-wedge agitation geometry, political montage | Static or single-figure field, flat litho planes, commercial brand name as the message |
| Flat Design | Digital UI palette, rounded icons, no texture, no historic lettering | Matte litho grain, muted historic hues, hand-wobbled contours, early-20th-century drawn type |

Within Plakatstil itself, three internal variants share the invariants: Bernhard's pure object-plus-name poster; Hohlwein's patterned-figure variant with flat textile checks, vivid fields and foreshortened perspective; and Erdt's letter-fusion variant where typography becomes the image. What never varies across them: the flat unmodulated field, the counted inks, the reductive single message, and the bold brand lettering integrated as image. If a poster keeps the ornament but drops the product, it is Jugendstil; if it keeps the product but adds scenery and slogan, it is merely illustration; if it keeps the planes but removes the brand and the period hand, it is contemporary flat design. Plakatstil is the intersection: object, name, field, and nothing else.
