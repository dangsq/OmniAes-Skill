# Plaguecore

Plaguecore is a costume-and-cabinet-of-curiosities aesthetic that emerged on Tumblr in the late 2010s, Western Europe, by way of the 17th-century Renaissance/Baroque plague doctor uniform re-read through a modern lens. Its community calls itself "doctors," nurses, students and apprentices, and treats the style as a guild of curious amateurs: students of old medicine (bloodletting, herbal remedies), collectors of masks, bones and ethically sourced taxidermy, keepers of journals and sketchbooks. The emotional core is **curiosity and discovery**, not fear — the masked figure is an investigator holding a lantern over a tray of instruments, not a monster. Dark humor runs through it: a woodcut rat wearing the doctor's own beak and hat is exactly the right register.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette is a desaturated value study with two metal families and strictly rationed accents. As a rule: darkness carries the image (near-black through mid-grey occupy most of the frame), light arrives as bone white, candle cream or aged-paper sepia rather than pure white, and any saturation is confined to small objects — a wine fill, an amber tincture, a brass chain.

| Role | Range | Typical carriers |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant darks | #0a0a0a – #2e2e2e | cloaks, robes, leather, shadowed rooms |
| Greys | #3a3a3a – #6b6b6b | linen skirts, studio backdrops, overcast sky, stone |
| Lights / paper | #d8cfc0 – #efe6d5 | bone white, letterpress pages, card stock, candle glow |
| Cold metal | #9aa0a6 – #c8ccd0 | surgical steel, syringe barrels, forceps, enamel tray rims |

| Rationed accents | oxblood #4a1218–#6b1f2a; apothecary amber #7a4a12–#a86a1e | wine in ornate glass, tincture vials; in fashion only, a single pop of vivid yellow or red |

Muted brown sits inside the dark family (leather, corduroy). Pure white, neon, candy pastel and saturated primary color are outside the style.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **The beaked bird mask** and mask collections (medical and decorative) — the style's keystone; round glass eyepieces that catch a glint or glow faintly in dark rooms.
2. **The doctor silhouette**: wide-brim hats (sunhat, fedora, gaucho, bolero, top hat), long shapeless cloaks, coats, robes and cardigans at knee length or longer; gloves (leather, medical, lace); a cane or staff; veils, bonnets, ruffle headbands; collar pins and brooches; chatelaine belts, chains, sashes and lanyards hung with small corked vials, keys and pocket watches.
3. **Books and paper**: bookshelves, leather-bound books, journals, sketchbooks with botanical or anatomical drawings; antique letterpress title pages and broadsides about the plague; woodcut and etching illustrations.
4. **Corvids**: ravens and crows, live or as ethically sourced taxidermy, wings spread, sometimes wearing a chain or cross pendant.
5. **Antiquarian medical apparatus**: bloodletting and surgical instruments (syringes, forceps, scissors, lancets) laid in enamel trays and leather roll cases; leeches; bottles and jars; apothecary vials.
6. **Herbs, houseplants and incense** (lavender typified) — the physician's garden indoors.
7. **Rats, bones, taxidermy** — the plague's companions, treated with dry affection rather than disgust.
8. **Lanterns and candles** as both props and light sources.
9. **Graveyards and abandoned buildings** (asylum corridors especially, per the style's webfiction lineage).
10. **Dark red wine in ornate glasses**; dark humor as a tonal motif throughout.

Note honestly: leeches, rats and wine are documented in the text but appear rarely or only as prints in surviving imagery; treat them as licensed motifs, not required ones.

### Materials and textures

Matte, worn, pre-plastic surfaces: oiled and scuffed leather; heavy wool, linen, corduroy, lace and mesh trims; blown glass with cork stoppers; brass and silver metalwork with patina; enamelware with chipped rims; laid paper with foxing and deckled edges; woodcut line work; feather barbs; bone; candle wax. Polished surgical steel is the one permitted gleam — instruments may shine coldly against matte surroundings.

### Recurring subjects and composition

Three compositional families cover the style: **tabletop still lifes** (instruments in trays, vials on chains, macro close-ups of a chatelaine against a linen skirt); **masked-figure portraits** (bust or full figure, often near-silhouette against a plain ground, a woodland, a stone ledge, a deep corridor, or a muted sky); and **archival flat objects** (scanned title pages, bordered card portraits, etchings of masked rats). Snapshot and polaroid framings with white borders, grainy high-contrast monochrome, and one-lamp corridor shots with the figure small in the distance all belong. Deep shadow should occupy a large share of the frame; when daylight appears it is overcast or flat, never golden.

### Lighting

Single-source, low-key: candle, lantern, wall sconce, or diffuse overcast sky. Chiaroscuro with the mask's round lenses catching the only bright points. Analog grain, vignetting and desaturated grading are characteristic; flat, even, fluorescent or studio-strobe brightness is not.

## Mood & Values

Scholarly macabre, eerie calm, dry wit. Plaguecore values curiosity, discovery and the romance of obsolete knowledge — journals filled, herbs dried, masks collected — while keeping a respectful distance from real medicine and from gore. Death is a colleague: bones and taxidermy are ethically sourced, graveyards are visited quietly, and the humor is gallows-dry (the rat in the doctor's hat) rather than shocking. Community and role-play titles ("doctor," "apprentice") give images a ceremonial, guild-like warmth beneath the black.

## Era & Origins

Late 2010s, Western Europe, born on Tumblr; source costume is the 17th-century Renaissance/Baroque plague doctor uniform. Modern catalysts include the cryptic 2015 viral video 11B-X-1371 (a masked figure in an abandoned asylum, flickering light, hidden ciphers) and Darkest Dungeon's playable Plague Doctor (2016). Adjacent touchstones: Bloodborne, Pathologic, The Seventh Seal, The Masque of the Red Death, Penny Dreadful; fashion brands Killstar, Blackcraft Cult, Restyle. The style layers eras freely: strict historical reenactment and modern streetwear (Doc Martens, cuffed jeans, black nail polish) are both valid.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Costume logic first: any human figure should read as a doctor — beaked mask (glass lenses glinting) or at minimum wide-brim hat plus long, shapeless dark cloak or robe.
2. Palette discipline: black/grey dominance, bone-white light, silver plus brass as the only metals, saturation confined to small accents.
3. Light discipline: one source (candle, lantern, sconce) or overcast daylight; let shadow hold most of the frame.
4. Object storytelling: include at least one antiquarian medical or scholarly object — tray of instruments, corked vial chain, leather journal, letterpress page.
5. Surface age: patina, foxing, grain, wear; nothing factory-new except cold polished steel.
6. Tone: curious, ceremonial, or dryly comic; never splatter-horror.
7. Medium freedom: photograph, polaroid snapshot, woodcut/etching, letterpress scan, painterly portrait card — all legitimate.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a masked portrait, a masked couple or ceremony, a still life, a document, a corvid or rat, a graveyard, an abandoned corridor, or a modern fashion arrangement. Composition may range from macro close-up of a chain and vials to full-figure landscape to flat archival scan. Lighting may range from candlelit interior to overcast noon to a single corridor lamp. Color handling may be pure monochrome, sepia, or muted color with one accent. Mood may be dread, whimsy, scholarly calm or ritual. Era layering may be strict 17th-century or modern wardrobe with historical accessories. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**

- Neon, candy pastel, or saturated modern color grading; glossy plastic; chrome techwear.
- Gas masks, respirators, military gear (that is Avant Apocalypse territory).
- Gears, goggles, brass engines, airships (Steampunk).
- Gore, blood splatter, zombies, slasher horror; the style references bloodletting but does not depict carnage.
- Sterile modern clinics: white chrome, fluorescents, latex sheen.
- Gilded Baroque abundance: cherubs, heavy ornament, saturated oil-paint reds and blues.
- Sunlit collegiate tweed-and-ivory bookishness with no macabre element (Dark Academia).
- Club-goth glamour: fishnets, platform boots, bat jewelry, underlit neon (Goth).
- Corvid-only nature mysticism with no doctor persona (Crowcore).
- Clean minimalism, flat even lighting, digital UI, logos, cars, modern crowds.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Avant Apocalypse** depicts present-day collapse: scavenged techwear, gas masks, dust and ruin as lived emergency. Plaguecore's ruins (asylums, graveyards) are visited historically, and its costume is pre-industrial, scholarly and intact.
- **Baroque** supplies plaguecore's period lineage but inverts its values: Baroque is gilded abundance, swirling religious drama, saturated pigment; plaguecore is matte austerity, clinical still life, desaturated shadow.
- **Crowcore** celebrates the bird itself — feather iridescence, corvid behavior, nature mysticism. In plaguecore the corvid is a companion or prop beside masks, instruments and books; the masked human silhouette is the subject.
- **Dark Academia** shares books and learning but keeps light-filled libraries, tweed, ivory and beige, and romantic classicism; plaguecore adds the beak mask, surgical steel, graves and low-key light.
- **Goth** shares black but is a modern music-subculture glamour — fitted Victorian romance, silver occult jewelry, club lighting. Plaguecore is costume-historical, shapeless in silhouette, medical-antiquarian in props, and leavened by dry humor rather than romance.
- Neighbors worth checking: **Steampunk** (machinery optimism) and **Vulture Culture** (bone collecting without the doctor persona) borrow elements but lack the masked physician's unified scholarly narrative.
