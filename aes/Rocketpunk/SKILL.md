# Rocketpunk

Rocketpunk is the aesthetic of spaceflight imagined with an engineer's eye and a mid-century heart. It covers retro-futuristic visions of space travel from the 1950s — advanced, cheap aerospace technology paired with primitive electronics — as well as modern, semi-realistic or hard science-fiction depictions inspired by real programs and proposals: Apollo, the unbuilt Project Orion nuclear spacecraft, the International Space Station, nuclear shuttle studies, Mars mission concepts. Whether it arrives as gouache pulp illustration or as a clean matte 3D concept render, the style's contract is the same: the machine must look like it was *designed*, with every tank, truss, radiator and engine bell answering a physical need. Alternate names mark its registers — "hard sci-fi" for the realistic end, "NASApunk" for program-inspired realism, "COADEcore" for the joking label given to plausible cylindrical and conical space warships.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette rule: neutral machine tones dominate the hull, one or two warm functional accents appear on panels and radiators, and the environment supplies either void black with a planetary blue-or-ochre arc, or a pulp night sky in deep teal. Saturation stays low on the vehicle and high only in markings, flames and planetary surfaces.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Rocketry itself** — cone-and-cylinder launch vehicles whose silhouette descends from the V-2: pointed nose, straight or curved fins, sometimes fins used as landing legs; checkerboard test-pattern bands on fuselages; bright exhaust at the pad.
2. **Cylinders and spheres as pressure vessels** — clustered propellant tanks, drum habitats, spherical module nodes; the style trusts stable manufactured shapes over sculpted ones.
3. **Solar panels and radiators** — flat rectangular wings, often in rust red, dark blue-gray or ochre, mounted on booms; radiators read as plain matte slabs, not decoration.
4. **Engineering legibility (realism)** — dedicated propulsion sections separated from payload sections; exposed truss spines and lattice booms; rocket engine bells, nuclear thermal cores, small electric thrusters; fuel-cell and nuclear power units.
5. **Rotating centrifuges for artificial gravity** — torus rings and wheel stations on axial spines, the circle-as-horizon motif.
6. **Stations, colonies and interplanetary ships** — orbital assemblies over a planetary limb, Mars transit vehicles, space hotels, settlement infrastructure; a larger human presence in space rather than lone heroics.
7. **Retro-futuristic fuselages and fittings** — cigar-shaped polished alloy bodies; large circular windows; onboard computers of nixie tubes, flashing indicator lights, reel-to-reel cassette decks, analog dials and toggle switches.
8. **Program markings** — small flags and insignia, stenciled serials, painted bands; identification rather than branding.

### Materials and textures

Matte painted hulls in white and light gray; bare aluminum truss lattice and scaffolding; gold or silver foil insulation wraps; dark photovoltaic glass; graphite and steel engine bells; thick glass portholes. Interiors, when shown, are bakelite, painted steel, cathode-ray green and warm incandescent dial light. Surface finish ranges from clean flat shading (concept-render strand) to visible gouache and oil brushwork (pulp strand); wear, when present, reads as functional use — scorch at engine ends, handling marks — never as decay or grime-as-mood.

### Recurring subjects

Launch pads and gantries at night or dawn; ascent with plume; orbital rendezvous and docking; station assemblies with spinning rings; interplanetary transit over a planetary arc; nuclear shuttle and Mars mission concepts; asteroid or lunar operations; crew figures in pressure suits as scale-givers in the pulp register; cockpit and control-room vignettes of analog instrumentation.

### Composition and lighting

Composition favors the whole vehicle: three-quarter portraits of a craft held in negative space, silhouettes readable as diagrams of purpose. Long truss diagonals cross the frame; ring centrifuges supply circles against void; planetary limbs arc across a corner or edge as the only scenery. Formations of several craft — station, shuttle, tug — establish scale and traffic. Lighting follows vacuum physics: a single hard sun, crisp terminators, black sky even beside blazing hulls, cool planetary albedo bouncing onto undersides; stars sparse or absent. The pulp and poster register substitutes dusk or night: floodlit pads, searchlight beams, starfields, long shadows, flame as the warm key light. Depth comes from occlusion and scale contrast — a tiny winged ferry beside a massive tank cluster — not from atmosphere or haze.

## Mood & Values

The core value is human expansion into space: settlement, infrastructure, programs, crews. The tone ranges from optimistic but not without challenges — missions with risk, checklists, and consequence — to dystopian variants where the same hardware serves colder purposes. The feeling is competent awe: the sublime located in engineering rather than in mystery. Even at its most dramatic, Rocketpunk is earnest and documentary-minded; it asks the viewer to believe the vehicle could fly, and invites them to read how.

## Era & Origins

The style emerges with the Space Age of the 1950s, crystallized by Sputnik in 1957, and takes its rocket silhouette from the postwar V-2. Its prototypes reach back further — Jules Verne's gun-launched moonship, Buck Rogers and interwar pulp — and its traditional look overlaps 1950s–1960s pulp sci-fi illustration, including the period's social conventions in crew depictions. A second, modern lineage draws directly on real space programs and studies: Apollo, Project Orion, nuclear shuttle concepts, Mars mission architectures, the ISS — carried forward by hard science fiction in film, television, games and simulation, from 2001's wheel station to contemporary program-realistic dramas and flight-simulator visuals. Rocketpunk therefore exists in two honest registers, retro-futurism and near-future realism, united by respect for engineering.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Engineer before styling: assemble every vehicle from functional sections — propulsion block with bells and tanks, truss spine, payload or habitat module, power wings, thermal radiators, optional centrifuge ring. If a part cannot be named, it should not be drawn.
- Honor vacuum light: one hard key source, black sky, crisp shadow lines, planetary bounce as fill; or, in the pad register, floodlights and flame against night.
- Keep the palette rule: neutral hulls, one or two warm functional accents, environment limited to void black plus a planetary blue, cyan limb, or ochre surface.
- Preserve legibility over drama: the silhouette should explain the machine's job at a glance; use negative space and scale contrast instead of motion blur or spectacle.
- Choose one register and stay coherent in it: pulp gouache with period crew and polished cigar fuselages; clean matte concept render with CAD clarity; or cinematic photo-real realism with modern program markings. Electronics, surface finish and figure styling must match the chosen register.
- Mark like a program, not a brand: small flags, stenciled numbers, checkerboard test bands, painted safety stripes.

**Variation axes**

- Subject: launch pad, ascent, orbital assembly, docking, station with ring gravity, interplanetary transit, nuclear shuttle, Mars or asteroid operations, space hotel, plausible warship, control-room interior.
- Composition: wide void portrait, close docking detail, diagrammatic side elevation, formation cluster, pad scene with gantry and tiny figures.
- Lighting: hard sunlit void, eclipse rim-light, night pad floodlighting, dawn ascent.
- Color handling: accent choice may swing ochre, rust, signal red or bare metal; pulp register may add teal night and cream poster fields.
- Mood: confident optimism, procedural tension, cold dystopian utility.
- Medium: gouache/oil illustration, flat matte render, cinematic realism.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A hotel station in ochre and white over a cyan limb and a rust-radiated freighter in pure black void are equally valid Rocketpunk.

**Avoid**

- Sleek seamless organic hulls, biomechanical curves, glossy featureless monoliths; sculpted "sci-fi smoothness" with no visible joints.
- Neon cyberpunk palettes, holograms, glowing energy fields, force shields, magic drives; engine effects limited to plausible plumes.
- Chrome tailfin cars, diners, boomerang patterns and domestic atomic kitsch.
- Googie architecture: swooping roofs, starburst signage, coffee-shop futurism as the subject.
- Mushroom clouds and radiation trefoils used as decorative iconography.
- Bubble-helmet heroes firing ray guns as the melodramatic focus; crew may appear, but hardware stays the protagonist.
- Steampunk brass and wood, or corrosion grime as an aesthetic focus; wear must read as use, not decay.
- Lens-flare spectacle, atmospheric haze in vacuum, star-dense skies doing the compositional work.
- Space-opera ornament: alien sculpting, heraldic hull paint, seamless glass bubble habitats standing in for engineered pressure vessels.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Atompunk** shares the 1950s–60s retro-future but lives on Earth: tailfin automobiles, diners, bulbous robots, domestic nuclear fantasy, chrome and boomerang graphics. Rocketpunk leaves the ground; its subject is flight hardware and orbital infrastructure, and its kitsch level is near zero.
- **Raygun Gothic** is the finned-rocket, bubble-helmet, zap-gun adventure pulp. Rocketpunk overlaps its earliest register but insists on engineering plausibility — radiators, trusses, tank clusters, dedicated sections — where Raygun Gothic is content with ornament and heroics.
- **Space Age** is the broader cultural design language: populuxe furniture, chrome satellite motifs, graphic design and architecture of the Sputnik era. Rocketpunk is the spacecraft-and-mission subset of that world, hardware-first rather than lifestyle-first.
- **Atomic Age** centers nuclear iconography — mushroom clouds, atom symbols, radiation motifs — across any medium. Rocketpunk may include nuclear thermal rockets and Orion-style pulse units, but as engineering components with shielding and pusher plates, never as emblem.
- **Googie** is ground-level commercial architecture: sweeping roofs, starbursts, signage. If the frame's subject is a building expressing roadside optimism, it is Googie; if it is a machine expressing orbital mechanics, it is Rocketpunk.

Within Rocketpunk itself, recognize the registers: retro-futurism (polished cigar fuselages, checkerboard, nixie tubes), NASApunk program realism (study-accurate stages, insignia, photo-real light), and COADEcore warship realism (cylindrical and conical combat vessels built from the same functional grammar). All three keep the same promise: the future, built from parts.
