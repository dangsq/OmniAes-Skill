# Tecktonik

Tecktonik is a dance-first youth subculture, not a music genre: a vertical, arm-driven choreographic style plus a tightly coded fashion uniform that grew out of the Métropolis nightclub in Rungis, outside Paris, and detonated worldwide through YouTube and French web forums between 2006 and 2008. Its visual world is the body in motion wearing its own brand: gel-sculpted punk crests, skinny denim, striped fingerless gloves, glowing belts, and a grey heraldic eagle split by a diagonal wordmark with a pink star, printed on everything from T-shirts to shampoo. The photographic record is deliberately cheap and immediate — on-camera flash at night, overcast daylight on concrete, grainy web-video stills — while its graphic record is glossy Y2K vector: sunbursts, stars, paint splashes, bubble letters, checkerboard, duotone posters and hand-scrawled marker tags reading "Tecktonik Killer." Energy, individuality and club belonging are the values on display; the image always feels like a fad burning at maximum brightness.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is a binary base pierced by fluorescent accents. The ground state is strict black and white — jet black tees, belts and backgrounds against stark white tees, jeans and gloves — plus the trademark's neutral mid-grey used for the eagle emblem. Into that monochrome base go one to three neon punches: hot magenta-pink, neon green, electric cyan, acid yellow, vivid orange or violet. A secondary color move is rainbow color-blocking: gradient wings on cover-art mascots, custom-painted sneakers with panels of pink, yellow, green and blue. Reproduction treatments extend the palette logically: pink-or-green duotone posters, black-and-white photos with a single selective pink accent, and full negative/inverted color effects. Muted, earthy or faded tones do not exist in this style except as accidental web-compression artifacts.

- Base blacks: #0A0A0A–#1A1A1A; base whites: #F2F2F2–#FFFFFF; trademark greys: #4D4D4D–#8C8C8C
- Neon pink/magenta: #FF007F–#FF33CC; neon green: #39FF14–#00FF66; cyan: #00E5FF–#00FFFF
- Acid yellow: #CCFF00–#FFE600; violet: #7F00FF–#9933FF; signal orange: #FF6600–#FF9900

### Signature motifs and objects, ranked by how strongly the culture documents them

1. **Punk-crest hair**: mohawks and mullets, heavy gel spikes, fluorescent hair extensions, bleached crests with dyed pink tips, shaved side panels with razor patterns. Hair is the loudest single signal.
2. **The eagle-and-star trademark**: a grey heraldic eagle with spread wings, cut by a diagonal band carrying the wordmark and a pink star; reproduced on T-shirts, flyers, posters and merchandise in varying colorways. Stars appear independently as makeup, prints and vector graphics.
3. **Fingerless gloves and arm warmers**, characteristically black-and-white striped, worn on one or both arms to accentuate elbow movement.
4. **The skinny silhouette**: slim jeans and tight pants, often white, black or bright blue, rolled or cropped at the ankle.
5. **Belts as light sources**: black belts studded with rhinestones or fitted with LED panels that glow pink or green.
6. **Pattern trio**: skulls, stripes and checkerboard, on socks, slip-on sneakers, scarves and layout backgrounds.
7. **Dark makeup with a star**, classically placed at the right eye.
8. **Eye-catching sneakers**, including checkerboard canvas slip-ons and custom color-blocked pairs; striped knee socks beneath them.
9. **Sunglasses**, worn indoors and at night.
10. **Club apparatus**: colored LED light rigs, neon club signage, and — documented for the early club nights specifically — fire effects and boxing rings for dance battles.
11. **Futuristic Y2K graphics**: sunburst rays behind emblems, vector stars and paint splashes, beveled bubble letters, gradient mascots, scrawled marker tag lettering, diagonal bands across layouts.
12. **The dance pose itself**: upright torso, feet on the ground, arms snapped into angular positions with elbows turned outward — the choreography never goes to the floor.

### Materials and textures

Cotton jersey T-shirts with vinyl screen-printed logos; thin denim stretched over the leg; rhinestone studs and cheap plastic LED housings on belts; gel-hardened, lacquered hair; rubber cupsoles and canvas checkerboard on sneakers; polyester scarves and bandanas at the neck; skin glossed by flash and club heat. The graphic layer is glossy Illustrator-era vector: smooth gradients, hard-edged stars, halftone dots. The photographic layer is the opposite: low-megapixel sensor noise, motion blur from web video, flash blowout, compression blocks — the texture of 2006–2008 internet fame.

### Recurring subjects

Solo dancers caught mid-move in streets, schoolyards, club entrances and concrete parking structures; group lineups of friends in matching logo tees in different colorways; cropped studies of legs, socks and sneakers; close-ups of crests and shaved head patterns; night crowds and glowing club signs; flyer and cover-art collages built from snapshot grids; merchandise stills of belts, gloves and branded objects; outdoor mass gatherings and pride-parade crowds. The human subject is teenage or young-adult, posed with bravado or lost in arm movement.

### Composition and lighting

Framing favors the vertical body: full-length or three-quarter shots that keep feet planted and arms inside or cutting across the frame edge; knee-down crops for footwear; tight head crops for hair. Graphic layouts run on diagonals — the saltire band of the logo repeated as slanted wordmarks across posters — and on centered sunburst rays behind an emblem. Collage thinking is constant: grids of four or more snapshots, overlapping stickers, splashes and stars filling negative space. Lighting splits into four regimes: harsh direct on-camera flash against night blackness; club neon and LED washes in magenta and green; flat overcast or midday light on grey concrete; and completely flat vector color in designed pieces. Selective colorization, duotone and negative inversion are accepted post-treatments.

## Mood & Values

Tecktonik images radiate kinetic confidence and teenage ownership: dance as self-expression, club culture as home turf, individuality performed through a shared uniform. There is battle bravado in the poses — the culture called its dancers "Killers" and "Killeuses" — and a communal, inclusive streak visible in pride-parade appearances and global schoolyard imitation. Everything feels urgent and ephemeral, a fad at peak intensity, simultaneously DIY (web videos, forum culture, hand-scrawled tags) and hyper-commercial (trademarked eagles on energy drinks and Nintendo DS games). Playfulness coexists with seriousness about style: the star at the eye and the crest on the head are declarations, not costumes.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in the early 2000s in the Parisian club circuit, named in 2002 by Alexandre Barousdin and Cyril Blanc after the French "tectonique," and built through "Tecktonik Killer" events at the Métropolis nightclub that imported Dutch and Belgian jumpstyle and hardstyle into France. The dance fused rave, hip-hop and voguing into upright, elbow-spinning choreography born in club dance battles alongside Melbourne shuffle and breakdancing. Peak visibility arrived 2006–2008 via YouTube and Skyrock forums, exporting the look to teenagers across Europe, Africa, Asia and the Americas before over-trademarking collapsed the scene from within. Visually, it reacts against both baggy nineties rave dress and rock-oriented emo by choosing a skinny, sporty, logo-heavy silhouette lit by club technology.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

- Build every image on a black-and-white foundation, then pierce it with one to three fluorescent accents; never let muted color carry the frame.
- Keep the body vertical and arm-led: planted feet, upright torso, elbows snapped into angular geometry. No floorwork.
- Sculpt the hair into a crest, mohawk or mullet with gel shine; optionally tip it with fluorescent color or shave a pattern into the sides.
- Dress the subject in the code: skinny jeans, logo or eagle-print tee, striped fingerless glove, studded or LED belt, checkerboard or color-blocked sneakers, star at one eye.
- Light with club technology or cheap flash: neon and LED washes at night, direct flash blowout, or flat daylight on raw concrete.
- In graphic pieces, think Y2K vector: sunburst rays, heraldic eagle, pink stars, paint splashes, checkerboard fields, diagonal wordmarks, gradient mascots, marker-scrawled tags.
- Keep the medium period-honest: early digital snapshot, grainy web-video still, forum-era page layout, or glossy vector flyer — nothing that reads as modern computational photography.

Variation axes. Subject matter may range from a single dancer to a group lineup, a crowd, a footwear or hair close-up, a merchandise still, a club façade, or a pure graphic flyer with no people at all. Composition may range from full-body vertical framing to knee crops, head crops, snapshot grids and diagonal-band layouts. Lighting may range from night flash to neon wash to overcast concrete daylight to flat poster color. Color handling may range from strict monochrome with a single pink accent, through full neon color-blocking, to duotone, selective color and negative inversion. Mood may range from euphoric dance motion to confrontational posed bravado to playful group camaraderie to pure graphic loudness. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

Avoid:

- Floorwork of any kind: lying down, somersaults, headspins, breakdance freezes — the dance is vertical by definition.
- Baggy or wide-leg silhouettes, cargo pants, phat pants, boho layering, draped fabric.
- Muted, earthy, pastel or vintage-faded palettes; golden-hour warmth; soft window-light editorial fashion; beige interiors; minimalist design.
- Nineties rave signifiers as the dominant code: smiley acid-house faces, pacifiers and whistles, PLUR kandi beads, fog-and-laser warehouses with mass jumping crowds.
- Emo/scene signifiers as the dominant code: rock band T-shirts, fringes covering both eyes, layered wristbands replacing the glove-and-belt code.
- Glossy 3D chrome renders, lens-flare trance typography and arena-euphoria crowd art.
- Modern anachronisms: smartphones, selfie sticks, contemporary app interfaces, post-2010 sneaker silhouettes.
- Organic, artisanal or heritage textures: linen, leather craft, wood grain, film-grain seventies warmth.

Note on evidence: the surviving visual record is inherently low-resolution web-era material, and some text-documented elements — fire effects, boxing rings, sneakers "resembling slippers" — appear rarely or not at all in surviving imagery; treat them as period-accurate optional details rather than core signals.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Bloghouse** shares the same Parisian electro years but images itself through type and irony: rough neon sans-serif, cartoonish vector mascots, indie-sleaze party blur, DJ booths and blog graphics, with no uniform dress code. Tecktonik always centers a costumed, choreographed dancing body and its eagle-and-star merchandise.
- **Hands Up** is a Central European eurodance genre whose art is glossy 3D render, chrome lettering, lens flares and euphoric arena crowds. Tecktonik is snapshot-real, fashion-coded and vector-collage, grounded in concrete streets and flash-lit clubs rather than rendered trance space.
- **Krocha**, the Austrian cousin, overlaps in bright hair and skinny pants but layers scene/emo accessories — band hoodies, checkered shirts, studded belts without LEDs — and poses for portraits rather than dancing. It lacks the eagle-star trademark, the striped fingerless glove and the vertical arm choreography that define Tecktonik.
- **Partille-Johnny** is a Swedish gel-haired mall style: popped collars, vests, faux tan and preppy partywear in restrained colors. Shared hair gel aside, it has no neon club gear, no logo merch and no dance pose.
- **Rave** of the nineties means baggy silhouettes, smiley icons, UV body paint, whistles, fog and laser beams over jumping masses. Tecktonik inverts the silhouette to skinny, replaces anonymous crowd euphoria with branded individual dancers, and swaps laser fog for flash photography and daylight concrete.
- Adjacent looks such as Emo, Scene, Electroclash, Indie Sleaze and Truzzo may borrow single elements (dark makeup, neon, gel hair), but none combines the vertical elbow-driven pose, the eagle-and-star trademark culture and the black-white-plus-neon palette in one frame; when all three coexist, the style is Tecktonik.
