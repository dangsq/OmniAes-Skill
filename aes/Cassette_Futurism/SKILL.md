# Cassette Futurism

Cassette Futurism (also called Formicapunk) is the retrofuturism of the moment when digital technology first appeared inside an analog world: roughly the early 1970s through the mid-1990s as depicted era, with the aesthetic itself named later by the TVtropes editor fruitpork. It is a transitional style — nostalgic for the period after Mid-Century Atomic Age and Space Age optimism cooled and before Y2K Futurism's seamless gloss arrived — in which the microprocessor is new, raw, and housed in heavy beige plastic. It absorbs 1970s–80s sci-fi film language and interior design (Supergraphic Ultramodern stripes, Earth Tones rooms, Laser Grid graphics), early virtual reality, and primitive computer animation, and it shares structural DNA with Cyberpunk, Neon Noir and the 1990s Cypherpunk / Early Cyber movement while refusing their shine. Its worldview matches Mundane Dogmatic science fiction: advanced hardware juxtaposed with legacy devices, big files moved by hand on tape rather than by network.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette.** The rule is casing neutrals in dominance, with color arriving as small functional points or as restrained analogous schemes. Beige, cream and grey fill the frame — matte shell tones from warm beige `#D9CDB4` through cream `#F1ECDF` to warm grey `#A9A294` and medium institutional grey `#6E6C68`. Darker shells run charcoal `#3A3A3C` to near-black `#141416`, especially on later consumer hardware. Against this, light emits rather than paints: red, green or blue LEDs (`#C22B2B`, `#2FA84F`, `#2B4BC2`), green phosphor CRT text (`#35D06A`–`#8CF09A`), olive passive LCDs (`#9BA84A`–`#B4BC64`), occasional amber phosphor (`#E8A33D`). Accent stripes and silkscreened marks may add signal orange `#C96A2A` or brick red. Translucent smoke plastic — maroon-grey `#4A2A2E` or grey `#55555A` — covers tape wells and cartridge slots. Scenes are frequently near-monochrome from the overuse of grey and beige; when several hues coexist they tend to be analogous. Brushed aluminium trim `#B9BCBE` and paper labels `#EDE8DC` with near-black type complete the set.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.**
1. **Compact cassettes and magnetic tape** — the defining object; tape as storage, as ROM cartridges, as console add-ons.
2. **Boxy CRT displays** — curved glass, deep chassis, chunky beige cases; sometimes evolved into larger, more complicated machines that keep the curved screen and gain whole panels of blinking lights and buttons.
3. **Early microcomputers and third-generation computer systems** in beige box cases; mainframe consoles and terminal rooms.
4. **The analog media family**: floppy disks, reel-to-reel tape drives, VHS or Beta videotape, dot matrix printers, dial-up modems, standalone hi-fi systems.
5. **Small LCD or monochrome green CRT readouts** with cryptic abbreviated labels.
6. **Microfiche readers** (key motif in the style's definition).
7. **Hybrid interfaces**: rotary dials and switchboards beside keyboards, joysticks serving as mice on low-resolution GUIs, older and newer interfaces welded into one desk.
8. **Poor graphics**: 2D visuals, simple 3D of basic geometry and stock textures — the look of limited future graphics.
9. **Geometric shapes and greebling**, concentrated on lab walls and spaceship hulls rather than scattered decoratively.
10. **Physical media exchange** as the network: CDs may appear, DVDs may not.

**Materials and textures.** Matte, lightly textured ABS plastic in beige, grey, charcoal or black; gloss-black plastic on later consumer decks; smoke-tinted translucent slot lids; rubber dome keys and membrane pads; painted steel panels on institutional machines; brushed aluminium trim strips; curved CRT glass with soft glare, faint scanlines and phosphor bloom; coiled and bundled cables; paper labels with small sans-serif or serif type, edges yellowing. Surfaces show use: dust in vents, fingerprint sheen on dark plastic, worn keycaps, faded silkscreen.

**Recurring subjects.** Game consoles and handhelds with landscape screens and round directional pads; desktop microcomputers and terminal stations; control rooms and spaceship bridges lined with console walls; laboratory and archive rooms of tape drives and reel cabinets; hi-fi shelving systems with flat panels and small colored buttons; wedge-shaped concept vehicles in overcast light; arcade cabinets with striped marquies; operator portraits seated at built-in console furniture.

**Composition and lighting.** The text prescribes little here, so treat these as observed tendencies, not laws: frontal and three-quarter object views with product-shot objectivity; wide vistas of console walls and machine rooms layered panel upon panel; tight crops across button fields, tape wells and port clusters; over-the-shoulder operator framings; isometric or turntable object renders; flat poster-like layouts with small typographic blocks. Lighting ranges from diffuse studio light with soft contact shadows, to flat fluorescent institutional light, to dim interiors lit mainly by phosphor glow and single LED points, to grey overcast daylight on exteriors. Light is always functional — fixtures, screens, indicators — never decorative neon.

## Mood & Values

Utilitarian wonder and institutional calm. Cassette Futurism values mass, tactility and repairability: every function has a button, every file has a shell you can hold, every machine hums, clicks and waits. It carries nostalgia for a future that stayed physical — bureaucracy of buttons, night-shift operators alone with glowing screens, labs where the advanced and the obsolete share a desk. The mood can be serene, eerie, or mundane, but it is never slick: competence expressed through hardware rather than through style.

## Era & Origins

Depicted era: early 1970s to mid-1990s, spanning late-Cold War technology, 1970s–80s sci-fi cinema and television, early microcomputing and early virtual reality. The term emerged from online aesthetic taxonomy (coined by fruitpork), and the style positions itself as the bridge between Space Age optimism and Y2K Futurism, reacting against both atomic-age chrome fantasy and today's seamless touchscreen present. Foundational touchstones include *2001: A Space Odyssey*, *Alien*, *Blade Runner*, *Tron*, *Akira*, *Ghost in the Shell*, and games from *Doom* and *Fallout* through *Control*, *SIGNALIS* and *Alien: Isolation*.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Data has a body: tape, cartridge, floppy, reel, microfiche — never cloud, never stream.
2. Machines have mass: deep boxy volumes, visible seams, vents, screws, handles; screens curve inward inside thick bezels.
3. Interfaces are many small decisions: arrays of buttons, toggles, rotary dials, sliders, labeled ports, cryptic abbreviations.
4. Graphics are poor: blocky pixels, 2D or primitive 3D, stock textures, monochrome phosphor or olive LCD.
5. Palette discipline: neutrals dominate; color arrives as LEDs, one button, one stripe, or an analogous scheme.
6. Hybrid chronology: new hardware bolted onto legacy dials, switchboards and patch cords.
7. Surface honesty: matte plastic, dust, yellowing, label wear — nothing seamless or candy-translucent.
8. Light is functional: fluorescents, phosphor bloom, indicator points; greebling belongs on lab walls and hulls.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may range across consoles, handhelds, mainframe halls, spaceship bridges, domestic computing corners, hi-fi stacks, vehicles, arcades, offices, labs. Composition may range from isolated object studies to crowded console-wall vistas to intimate panel close-ups. Lighting may range from bright studio diffuse to flat fluorescent to phosphor-lit gloom to overcast daylight. Color handling may range from strict grey-beige monochrome with one LED accent to analogous multi-hue schemes to a single saturated shell color on an otherwise utilitarian form. Mood may range from clinical calm to lonely dread to playful retro-optimism. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Touchscreens, smartphones, tablets, thin flat panels, wall-mounted LCDs; holograms, HUD overlays, hexagon grids, wireframe VR, chrome gradients, lens flares; seamless minimalist glass-and-aluminium design; DVDs, Blu-ray, cloud or network iconography, wireless earbuds; rain-slicked neon streets and noir chrome; vaporwave pastel grids, palms, marble busts; atomic-age chrome fins and bulbous raygun robots; steampunk brass; crisp anti-aliased modern GUIs; candy-translucent Y2K plastic; environments whose only content is wall-scale supergraphic print with no machines.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Supergraphic Ultramodern** shares the 1970s interior language and stripe graphics, but its hero is environmental graphic design — color-blocked walls, chevrons, bold flat shapes on architecture and print. Cassette Futurism's hero is working hardware and media; its color stays in casing neutrals with tiny functional accents. A room full of bold wall graphics and no machines is Supergraphic Ultramodern.
- **Cyberpunk** shares 1980s sci-fi lineage and structural DNA, but lives at night in rain, neon signage, chrome and wet streets, with sleek black-glass interfaces and body modification. Cassette Futurism is fluorescent or phosphor lit, beige and grey, tape-based, bureaucratically dystopian rather than noir.
- **Early Cyber** (1990s cypherpunk/VR culture) embraces the virtual: chrome gradients, wireframe space, glossy digital optimism. Cassette Futurism refuses the virtual — information sits on shelves as physical media, and screens are small, curved and poor.
- **Earth Tones** shares 1970s beige and brown but is domestic and organic: harvest gold, avocado, wood paneling, shag textiles, plants. Add machines, grey plastic and phosphor glow and it becomes Cassette Futurism; remove the hardware and it is Earth Tones.
- **Hexatron** is defined by glowing hexagonal grids, dark HUD fields and holographic interfaces. Cassette Futurism's graphics are deliberately primitive and its interfaces entirely physical — buttons, not hexagons.
