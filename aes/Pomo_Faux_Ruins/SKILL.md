# Pomo Faux Ruins

## Overview

Pomo Faux Ruins (Postmodern Faux Ruins) is an architectural and interior-design aesthetic that flourished from the late 1970s through the 1990s. Its defining act is the insertion of deliberately fragmented, distressed, or crumbling classical architecture — a column that stops in mid-air, a pediment cracked down the middle, a capital detached from its shaft — into overtly commercial, artificial environments. The decay is a prop, not a condition: it subverts Modernism's pristine strictness by turning structural failure into a playful, educational, often ironic storytelling device. Where a genuine ruin accumulates history, a Pomo Faux Ruin manufactures it overnight in fiberglass and drywall, then lights it like a stage.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)

Pigment stays in a sterile neutral triad; saturation arrives from light, not from dyed surfaces.

| Role | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sterile plaster white / cream | `#F7F3EA` – `#E6DFD2` | Columns, fragments, cast statues, drywall shells |
| Warm brown / wood / terracotta | `#6B4A32` – `#A9764F`, tans to `#C9A87C` | Wood veneer, coffered ceilings, brick infill, buffet counters |
| Grounding black / charcoal | `#12100E` – `#262220` | Steel stair rails, chair frames, column shafts, ceiling grids |
| Theatrical accent (emitted light) | sunset orange-red `#D9542B` – `#F2A65A`; neon pink/magenta `#E88FA5` – `#F4B8C4`; cool chrome `#B9BCBF` | Up-lights, neon tubing, airbrushed sky murals, chrome plating |

The rule: surfaces are white, brown, black, or a pale pastel; any intense color in the frame should read as *lighting* — an up-light wash, a neon tube, an airbrushed gradient horizon — or as a pastel-painted modern element (pink scaffold pipe, mint trim) under the style's "pastel drywall" clause. A room can be almost entirely cream-and-black with one red gradient wall and still be correct.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Deconstructed classical elements** — the core motif. Columns that stop mid-air, cracked pediments, detached capitals, broken arches, fluted drums lying on their sides. Always ornamental, never load-bearing; the break is the point.
2. **Statues and busts** — classical figures and heads, often fragmentary (headless torsos, armless busts), used as props in still-life groupings or on plinths within installations.
3. **Rocks** — listed as a key motif but thinly described in the text; in practice sterile boulder-like or geometrically faceted stone forms, smooth and moss-free, placed like set dressing.
4. **Theatrical lighting devices** — saturated up-lighting grazing column flutes, track spots, neon highlights, and simulated open-sky ceilings or airbrushed twilight murals standing in for a horizon.
5. **Commercial-classical trim** (image-supported, secondary to the text) — Greek-key meander friezes, checkered or coffered ceiling grids, black steel railings and stair towers that frame the fragments inside working commercial rooms.

### Materials and textures

Material dissonance is doctrinal: "ancient" shapes in distinctly cheap or modern substances. Fiberglass casts, pastel-painted drywall, smooth poured concrete, chrome plating, neon tubing, plaster of Paris, glossy laminate, painted MDF, black powder-coated steel. Surfaces are matte-smooth or geometrically fluted/wavy; fracture edges are crisp, like broken ceramic or cut foam board. There is no patina anywhere: no moss, no lichen, no water staining, no crumbling mortar, no erosion powder. The decay is clean enough to touch.

### Recurring subjects

Text-documented settings, in order: corporate atriums and office plazas with massive non-functional column fragments as sculptural focal points; hotel lobbies; cafes and nightclubs populated with freestanding pillars and crumbled archways for a cinematic, edgy, dystopian nightlife atmosphere; 1980s–90s clothing boutiques, shopping centers and flagship stores using fragments to structure immersive retail environments. The photographic record extends this to outdoor plaza and lawn follies (a temple portico shard on grass, a tilted pediment on paving) and to gallery or studio installations where white fragments are wired together with pastel scaffold pipe against plywood walls. These outdoor and installation variants are legitimate extensions of the same rules.

### Composition and lighting

Spaces are staged like theatre sets: wide, eye-level interior vistas with a fragment or fragment-cluster holding the focal point in an open floor; symmetrical grids (ceiling coffers, colonnade rhythms) deliberately interrupted by asymmetrical breaks; proscenium-like layering of counter, stair, and mural behind the ruins. Lighting is the style's second voice: saturated up-lights from floor coves, track spots on black rails, glowing cove lines, neon outlines, and simulated skies — coffered luminous ceilings or airbrushed sunset gradients — that dissolve the boundary between room and set. Flat, even, shadow-soft light is acceptable for installation- and studio-type views, where the plywood-and-concrete backstage is part of the statement.

## Mood & Values

Ironic, theatrical, commercially optimistic. The style values wit over reverence: decay as pedagogy and play, artificiality as an honest virtue, irony as a design material. It carries a cinematic edge — sometimes edgy or faintly dystopian in nightclub contexts — but never melancholy; the ruin is a joke told beautifully, a nostalgia performed rather than felt. Emptiness is fine: an unpopulated cafe or plaza reads as a set between shows, which is exactly the intended feeling.

## Era & Origins

Emerged in the late 1970s, prevalent through the 1990s; the term is credited to the CARI Institute. It is a postmodern reaction against Modernism's pristine, strict rules — borrowing the classical vocabulary that Neoclassical Pomo used earnestly, then breaking it on purpose. Its natural habitats are the commercial building booms of the 1980s: mall courts, hotel atriums, flagship retail, nightlife venues.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Break the classical, never build it: every column, pediment, arch or capital appears truncated, cracked, detached or tilted; nothing antique-looking supports anything real.
2. Decay without dirt: crisp fracture planes, pristine surfaces, zero moss, grime, staining or erosion.
3. Let materials lie: ancient silhouettes in fiberglass, pastel drywall, smooth concrete, chrome, neon; visible modern joints and scaffold elements are welcome.
4. Light it like a stage: saturated up-lighting, track spots, neon lines, simulated open-sky ceilings or airbrushed gradient horizons.
5. Keep it commercial or civic: cafes, clubs, malls, lobbies, plazas, installations — the ruin is set dressing for commerce and leisure, never an archaeological site.
6. Hold the palette rule: white/brown/black pigment plus pastel accents; strong color enters as emitted light.

**Variation axes**

- *Subject*: any interior or site within the range above — cafe, nightclub, boutique, mall court, hotel atrium, office plaza, lawn folly, paved plaza sculpture, studio installation, or a still life of busts and sterile rocks.
- *Composition*: wide interior vista, plaza vignette, close fragment study, or installation view; symmetrical or deliberately off-balance.
- *Lighting*: warm track-and-uplight theatricality, cool neon nightlife, luminous simulated sky, twilight mural backdrop, or flat studio light.
- *Color handling*: neutral-dominant with a single saturated light accent, or accent-rich neon schemes; pastel structural accents allowed.
- *Mood*: playful irony, sterile corporate monumentality, or edgy dystopian nightlife.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A plaza folly in daylight with no neon, or a cream cafe with one cracked pediment and a gradient mural, are both fully in style even if neither resembles a given exemplar.

**Avoid**

- Genuine weathering: moss, ivy, lichen, water stains, crumbling mortar, patina, soil, archaeological stratigraphy.
- Earnest intact classicism: complete colonnades, whole pediments, symmetrical load-bearing orders (that is Neoclassical Pomo).
- Romantic overgrown ruins, jungle temples, Gothic or medieval vocabulary (pointed arches, gargoyles, tracery).
- Graffiti, trash, rust, cracked asphalt, documentary neglect-melancholy (that is Austurbane).
- Handcrafted wabi-sabi warmth, artisanal patina, cozy residential clutter.
- Pure Minimalist or International-Style spaces with no classical quotation at all.
- Saturation as pigment: candy-colored walls or props instead of light-born color.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Against Neoclassical Pomo**: same classical vocabulary, opposite intent. Neoclassical Pomo quotes antiquity whole and earnestly — complete columns, full pediments, symmetrical facades, stone-toned precast reading as permanent monument. Pomo Faux Ruins fractures the same elements, floats them off structure, strips them to sterile modern materials, and stages them with theatrical light and commercial program. If the columns carry real load and nothing is broken, it is not this style.
- **Against Austurbane**: Austurbane documents real urban austerity and decay — weathered concrete, grime, rust, vegetation reclaiming infrastructure, flat documentary light, melancholy of neglect. Pomo Faux Ruins simulates decay as a pristine prop: crisp breaks, sterile surfaces, saturated stage lighting, occupied commercial settings, irony instead of loss. The test is the surface: if the ruin looks touchable-clean and lit like a set, it is faux; if it looks forgotten and eaten by weather, it is not.
