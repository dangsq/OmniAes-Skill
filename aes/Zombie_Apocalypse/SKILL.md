# Zombie Apocalypse (Zombiecore)

Zombie Apocalypse is a trope of horror, action and thriller fiction — survivors holding on while hordes of infectious, flesh-consuming undead collapse civilization — that hardened into a free-standing visual aesthetic, also called Zombiecore, in which people roleplay the outbreak, curate its imagery, and sometimes weave it into conspiracy folklore. Its pictures are not battle scenes so much as evidence scenes: a notice zip-tied to a fence, a street of abandoned cars under a smoke-grey sky, pale hands crowding a dark window opening. The horror is administrative and ambient; the dread lives in dull color, dead grass, and hand-scrawled instructions about what to do with a body.

## Visual Identity

### Palette: a two-layer rule

The color system is a desaturated field punctured by small, loud accents. The **field layer** is darker earthy color: black, charcoal, ash gray, tan, olive and moss green, brown — frequently pushed to near-monochrome, or washed with a sickly grade. The **accent layer** is brighter, unsettling color used sparingly and with a narrative excuse: arterial red (blood, spatter, marker ink), rust orange, warning yellow (fuel cans, signage). A second, more stylized family — the infobox's key colors — admits green, teal, bruised purple and cadaverous pink as overall tints, most at home in colored posters, outfits and graded photography rather than in the documentary core, which skews gray-green. Note honestly: sampled imagery leans earthy/monochrome/green, so treat purple, teal and pink as legitimate but secondary grade options documented by the style's key-color list.

The governing rule: **saturation is information.** The duller the field, the louder a small accent reads; never let bright color occupy more than a few percent of the frame unless the image is a stylized poster or outfit study.

### Signature motifs and objects, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Empty cities and post-apocalyptic scenery** — named first among key motifs: vacant intersections, parked-and-abandoned cars, overgrown pavement, streets with no living traffic.
2. **Warning signs and hand-made notices** — the Visuals section leads with them; stenciled public-safety boards, marker-lettered cardboard ("dead inside / do not enter"), quarantine instructions tied with string or wire to fences and gates.
3. **Old buildings and derelict civic architecture** — sites of importance, especially government buildings and domes, standing hazed and unguarded to assert the horde's complete takeover of populous areas.
4. **Weapons, especially firearms** — rifles and shotguns carried low by survivors, plus improvised clubs and tools.
5. **Torn clothes** — ripped fabric on survivors and undead alike, signaling struggle and decay.
6. **Meat** — the consumed body: blood spatter, bitten remains, gore handled as trace on surfaces more than as spectacle.
7. **The undead horde** — introduced as "hordes of zombies"; visually usually partial: reaching hands and arms, silhouettes pressed against mesh or frosted glass, shapes in dark doorways, a face half-lost in a black window.
8. **Scarcity objects** — inferred from the key values (fatigue, hunger, survival): canned food, fuel cans, backpacks, barricade materials, scavenged gear.

### Materials and textures

Corrugated iron, chain-link fence, barbed wire, weathered clapboard, peeling paint, plywood and cardboard signage, twine and zip ties, rust, dust, dry waist-high grass, smoke haze, cracked asphalt. Medium memory matters: film grain, scratched emulsion, crushed blacks, edge vignette, the flat softness of an overcast day.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Sourceless ambient light dominates: flat overcast daylight, dusk blue-gray, or dim interiors lit by one practical source. High-contrast monochrome is a common alternate treatment, especially for silhouettes against translucent barriers. Recurrent structures: the frontal centered document (sign as portrait); one-point perspective down an emptied street; a small lone figure dwarfed by environment; hands radiating outward from a dark rectangular opening; tight crops on spatter, fabric or fence. No glam key light, no hero angles — the camera behaves like a witness or a found photograph.

## Mood & Values

The style's declared values are **fatigue, hunger, survival**, and its imagery obeys them: exhaustion over adrenaline, vigilance over action, scarcity over abundance. There is bureaucratic dread in notices that plan for dead bodies, and loneliness in architecture built for crowds and now holding none. A deadpan, bleak-comedy register is native to the lineage (spoof films and hand-made signage share the same straight face), as is a thin thread of tenderness — survivors keeping each other alive.

## Era & Origins

The word "zombie" comes from Haitian folklore and Vodou; the apocalypse genre itself begins in the 1960s with *Night of the Living Dead* (1968), whose creatures audiences recognized as zombies and renamed. From there the vocabulary spread through film sequels and contagion thrillers, video games from *Resident Evil* (1996) onward, prestige TV from 2010, and survival fiction, before condensing into the internet-curated aesthetic "Zombiecore" on Pinterest boards and roleplay communities. Culturally it reacts first to cold-war-era anxieties about mass consumerism and social collapse, and later to viral-pandemic and quarantine fears — collapse by infection, not by bomb.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Desaturate first; build the frame in earthy neutrals, then admit one or two accent hues only where the story puts them (blood, fuel, marker ink, flare).
2. Show collapse through the mundane — signage, fences, parked cars, unmown lawns — not through explosions or rubble piles.
3. Let the undead be partial or absent: hands, silhouettes, a darkened window, a stain. Absence outranks presence.
4. All typography is hand-made: marker or stencil on cardboard, plywood, metal; uneven strokes and misspellings welcome.
5. Light is ambient and unglamorous: overcast, dusk, or a single dim practical; add grain and vignette as medium memory.
6. Keep the recent past intact and empty: late-20th/early-21st-century cars, suburbia, civic domes — standing, silent, unoccupied.

**Variation axes.** Subject: sign close-ups, street vistas, barricaded interiors, survivor portraits with gear, horde fragments, still-lifes of scavenged objects, civic landmarks, treeline edges. Composition: centered document, one-point empty street, small figure in wide frame, tight texture crop, collage of found fragments. Lighting: flat overcast, stark monochrome, dusk, single-source interior, flashlight night. Color handling: full earthy desaturation, near-monochrome, sickly green/teal grade, bruised purple/pink stylization for posters and outfits, minimal accent red. Medium: grainy photo, film still, found-footage frame, scanned print, poster art. Mood: dread, exhaustion, deadpan humor, quiet tenderness.

**A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — palette logic, material vocabulary, ambient light, hand-made communication, the trace of the horde — not any reference image.**

**Avoid**

- Saturated commercial color, glossy retouching, clean studio lighting, golden-hour warmth, lens-flare heroics.
- Neon or cyberpunk palette, chrome, holograms, futuristic tech, digital screens as focal points.
- Pristine architecture, manicured greenery, crowds of living civilians going about daily life.
- Clean vector fonts, branded signage, professionally printed graphics.
- Desert dunes, war rigs, leather scavenger armor, nuclear crater fields — generic Post-Apocalyptic machinery.
- Bats, skulls, coffins, rockabilly cartoon graphics, black-plus-neon-red band-poster styling — Horror Punk emblems.
- Cozy slacker domesticity and flannel fashion editorial with no survival narrative — Grunge.
- Cute or chibi zombies and bright cartoon palettes (parody is allowed only if the earthy field and deadpan tone hold).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Horror Punk:** Horror Punk is graphic and emblematic — stylized skulls, bats, high-contrast black with hot red or neon, illustrative band-merch energy. Zombie Apocalypse is photographic and procedural: horror via quarantine notices, fences and absence, in earthy dull color, with no gothic ornament.
- **Vs Grunge:** Grunge's decay is personal and economic — flannel, analog warmth, messy domestic interiors, 90s slacker intimacy. It lacks the undead, the signage, the weapons and the civilizational narrative; its fade is nostalgic, where Zombiecore's fade is sickly.
- **Vs Post-Apocalyptic:** the broader neighbor spans desert rust, vehicular culture and nuclear ruin in warm oranges and browns. Zombie Apocalypse is its temperate, urban, infection-logic subset: damp green-gray grades, overgrown grass rather than dunes, intact-but-empty recent infrastructure, signs about bodies, and hands at the window. If the collapse has no contagion and no horde-trace, it is merely Post-Apocalyptic; if the ruin is dressed in bats and eyeliner, it is Horror Punk; if nothing threatens anyone, it is Grunge.
