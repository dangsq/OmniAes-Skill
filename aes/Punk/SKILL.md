# Punk

Punk is a subculture whose visual side is a declaration of non-conformity: clothing bought second-hand or handed down, then torn, defaced, repainted and re-pinned by its wearer, and a graphic art that deliberately rejects professional polish in favor of Dada- and Situationist-descended collage. The organizing principle is the DIY ethos — the look must appear made, not bought. Every canonical garment carries evidence of labor: a rip closed with safety pins, a patch sewn by hand, a slogan brushed on in uneven letters. Punk emerged from working-class anger and boredom in the mid-1970s as a rejection of both commercial rock and consumer society, and its aesthetic has persisted across decades as a street style of revolt.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is a black base, natural material tones, and red used as a weaponized accent.

- **Black** (#0b0b0d–#26262a): leather jackets, denim, hair, print ink — the ground color.
- **Naturals**: faded indigo denim, tan and brown leather (#6b4a2f–#8a6a45), drab khaki, off-white newsprint and bleached cotton (#e6e1d6).
- **Shock red** (#b3121b–#d40000): slogans, defacement bars, tartan ground, dyed hair — never a background wash, always an interruption.
- **Metal**: stud silver and gunmetal (#9ea2a8), the glint of spikes, pins and chains.
- **Tartan interrupter**: red-ground tartan with black, white and yellow lines (occasionally green or blue), on trousers, skirts, or strips tied to arms.
- **Hair-dye pinpoints**: peroxide blonde, magenta, acid green — saturated color is allowed only in small organic areas (hair) or as red accents.

Full monochrome is a legitimate alternative palette: high-contrast black-and-white, or Xerox threshold where midtones drop out entirely. Color photography reads as cheap film snapshot — slightly desaturated, casual color casts, never glossy commercial saturation.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Deliberate destruction and repair**: rips, tears, exposed seams, holes held closed with safety pins; garments defaced with paint, bleach and scrawled words.
2. **The battle jacket / vest**: denim or leather converted into a wearable billboard — sewn patches, pins, studs, spikes, hand-painted band names and political phrases in collage density.
3. **Provocative slogans and hand-lettering**: painted or printed text on shirts, jackets and placards; in print, ransom-note letters cut from newspapers.
4. **Studs, spikes and safety pins** as hardware, ornament and jewelry.
5. **Mohawks and spiky hair**: liberty spikes, shaved sides, bleached or dyed crests.
6. **Leather jackets, band T-shirts, tartan** as the base uniform.
7. **Combat boots (Doc Martens)**, ladder-laced; colored-lace code signals are text-documented but rarely legible in images — treat as optional detail.
8. **Anarchy symbols**: the circled A spray-painted on shutters and walls, patched and stenciled onto cloth.
9. **Fetish-wear accents**: bondage straps, harnesses, fishnet, chokers and cuffs from the Westwood SEX/Seditionaries vocabulary.
10. **Print matter**: zines, flyposted flyers, photocopied posters, album sleeves defacing official imagery — crowns, flags, state portraits barred with cut letters.

### Materials and textures

Cracked and creased leather; distressed denim with frayed white warp threads at every rip; wool tartan; rubber boot soles with yellow welt stitching; cone and pyramid studs; coarse hand-stitching and uneven patch edges; fishnet; photocopier grain, halftone dots and newsprint fiber; spray-paint bloom on brick; tape and staple marks on flyers. Surfaces should look handled, sun-faded, scuffed and reworked.

### Recurring subjects

Solo or group punks, unposed or staring the camera down; crowds at gigs, protests and street gatherings; mundane public stages — transit cars, street corners, squat yards, poster walls. Object subjects: battle jackets on hangers or in flat lay, boots, zines, flyers, pins. Space subjects: graffiti-tagged shutters, flyposter-cluttered walls, club basements. Print subjects: album covers, posters, zine spreads.

### Composition and lighting

Snapshot grammar: eye-level or slightly low, centered or carelessly off-kilter, limbs cropped at frame edges, bodies clustered and overlapping, backgrounds kept legible as evidence of place (storefronts, train interiors, brick walls). Graphic grammar: cut-and-paste grids, tilted clippings, type bars laid across faces, repeated Xerox generations. Lighting: direct on-camera flash with hard shadows and hot highlights; flat overcast daylight; dim interiors lifted by flash; grainy high-ISO film; for print matter, flat scanner light with photocopy contrast. Depth of field stays deep — clutter is information, never bokeh.

## Mood & Values

Defiance and confrontation leavened by sarcasm, self-derision and crew camaraderie; boredom converted into anger; pride in making-do. Belonging is expressed through individually modified uniform — everyone in the same vocabulary, no two pieces alike. Anti-consumerism is the core value: anything that looks newly bought, professionally made or corporate-approved contradicts the style. Visible hand-labor is the mark of authenticity.

## Era & Origins

Proto-punk roots in early-1970s Detroit and New York; the style coalesced 1974–1976 between New York (Richard Hell's torn clothes, leather jackets, spiky hair) and London (Malcolm McLaren and Vivienne Westwood's SEX/Seditionaries boutique, the Sex Pistols, deliberate moral panic against the Monarchy). Working-class roots set it against the preceding middle-class hippie culture. It radiated into 1980s hardcore, anarcho- and crust punk and 1990s Riot Grrrl, and survives as a trans-decadal street style. Default visual grammar is 1976–1986, but any decade since is legitimate.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Show the hand: every hero garment carries at least one modification trace — rip, pin, patch, painted word, stud row.
2. Keep palette discipline: black + naturals + red accent; tartan and hair dye as controlled interrupts; monochrome always acceptable.
3. Treat text as physical material: slogans and cut letters have edges, glue marks, misspellings and uneven kerning; never clean overlay type.
4. Photograph as evidence, not advertisement: flash snapshot or flat daylight, grain, cluttered real environments, unposed bodies.
5. Age everything: film grain, photocopy generation loss, faded fabric, scuffed hardware.
6. Confront without hate: deface establishment imagery (crowns, flags, currency, official portraits) with bars, slogans and the anarchy symbol. Historical punk borrowed fascist iconography purely for shock; do not reproduce hate symbols — channel provocation through anarchist and anti-establishment signage instead.

**Variation axes.** Subject: portrait, group, crowd, protest, object flat lay, print spread, interior or street space. Composition: centered snapshot, tilted frame, cropped limbs, collage grid, top-down flat lay, wall-filling frame. Lighting: night flash, overcast day, club flash, flat scan light, high-contrast monochrome conversion. Color: full black-and-white, desaturated snapshot color, red-accented monochrome, tartan pop. Mood: rage, boredom, laughter and crew warmth, sarcastic play. Era: 1974 to present. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a color snapshot of friends laughing on a train in studded jackets is as punk as a monochrome defaced-poster collage.

**Avoid**

- Pristine garments, visible luxury branding, clean tailoring, unworn leather.
- Polished typography: kerned sans-serifs, gradients, vector crispness, drop shadows.
- Studio softboxes, seamless backdrops, golden-hour bokeh, HDR gloss, retouched skin.
- Pastel or jewel-tone dominance, rainbow gradients, candy-colored cartoon spray murals.
- Neighbor confusion: flannel-slouch unmodified thrift (grunge), side-swept fringe and clean skinny denim (emo), batcave glam and teased deathhawk (deathrock), chrome-and-latex futurism (industrial).
- Fascist or hate iconography.
- Symmetrical, manicured, editorial-posed composition; digital noise-free perfection.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared surface | What separates Punk |
|---|---|---|
| Deathrock | Studs, fishnet, black | Deathrock is horror-glam: teased deathhawk hair, white face makeup, occult/bat motifs, club theatrics. Punk keeps daylight street realism, tartan, slogans, thrift-store mess. |
| Emo | Band tees, black denim | Emo is confessional and inward: long side-swept fringe, clean skinny silhouette, emotional softness. Punk hair stands up (mohawk, spikes) and its text addresses the viewer confrontationally. |
| Graffiti Pop | Street-born mark-making | Graffiti Pop celebrates spray virtuosity: saturated candy color, clean outlines, cartoon characters at wall scale. Punk's street mark is the quick stencil, sprayed circled-A and pasted flyer — amateur by principle, monochrome-plus-red. |
| Grunge | Thrifted clothing | Grunge leaves thrift unmodified: flannel, worn jeans as fatigue not statement, slouched apathy, muted earth tones. Punk displays modification labor — patches, pins, paint — and confronts. |
| Industrial | Fetishwear accents, black leather | Industrial refines fetish gear into engineered uniform: chrome, latex, machinery, cold blue light, dystopian order. Punk's bondage straps are torn, pinned, handmade, shot on warm analog grain. |

Honest gap note: the lace code, horror-punk and psychobilly imagery are documented in text but thin or absent in visual evidence here; treat them as optional garnish, not core grammar.
