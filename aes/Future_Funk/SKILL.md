# Future Funk

Future Funk, also called Vaporboogie, is an internet-born microgenre that coalesced around 2012–2013 on SoundCloud, Bandcamp and YouTube as the energetic, dance-floor derivative of Vaporwave. Where Vaporwave deconstructs the past through irony, slowness and decay, Future Funk reconstructs it into a hyper-idealized, euphoric celebration of Japan's 1980s "Bubble Economy" (roughly 1986–1991): perpetual nightlife, romance and cosmopolitan glamour. Musically it speeds 1970s disco and 1980s City Pop samples up to 115–130 BPM under French-house drums; visually it does the equivalent acceleration, recontextualizing vintage anime loops through high-saturation color grading, rhythmic editing and neon typography. The visual language was standardized by curators pairing tracks with perfectly looped GIFs of Golden-Age anime characters, so the still image grammar of the style is essentially "album cover, flyer or loop frame for a party that never ends."

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule
Every Future Funk image sits inside a neon triangle of magenta, cyan and violet, saturated near maximum, usually blended as gradient washes or duotone/tritone grades rather than local color. A working range: magenta-pink `#FF2E97–#FF8FD3`, cyan `#00E5FF–#8FFBFF`, violet `#6A2FBF–#C77DFF`, night grounds of saturated indigo `#150F45–#2E2A89`, and spark accents of lemon yellow `#FFE45E–#FFFB96`, with occasional warm orange `#FF7A45` in sunbursts, jackets and signage. Shadows are colored — violet, blue, magenta — never neutral gray; highlights bloom toward white-pink like phosphor. Pastels are allowed only as candy accents *inside* a saturated field (blush cheeks, doodle-collage backgrounds, sticker shapes); an overall muted pastel haze is Vaporwave, not Future Funk.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them
1. **The anime loop figure.** The genre's most recognizable motif: cel-shaded characters from the Golden Age of anime (circa 1978–1995), curated for themes of romance, dancing and cosmopolitan life, caught in a repeatable gesture — a dance step, a wink, a hair flip, a transformation sequence. The green-haired bikini idol archetype (the genre's unofficial mascot), magical-girl heroines and 80s teen romance casts are the lineage. In still images this reads as a character frozen mid-gesture, often doubled, mirrored or tiled to imply the loop.
2. **Neon typography.** 1980s serif and script faces, chrome and metallic gradients, glowing outlines, and Japanese kanji/katakana (full-width characters welcome), arranged motion-graphic style: arched titles, starburst badges, diagonal pulsing lockups, track-and-artist credits treated as design elements.
3. **Analog music objects.** Spinning vinyl, cassette tapes held aloft or stacked, headphones, boomboxes, keyboards and synths, floating musical notes as graphic confetti.
4. **Disco and stage light.** Mirror-ball flecks, light beams, starbursts, bokeh circles, horizon grids — the lighting kit of late-night television and disco culture.
5. **Bubble-economy settings.** Arcades lined glowing cabinets, neon-signed city streets, club stages, bedrooms centered on a CRT television, resort-leisure glamour; romance and nightlife as subject matter.
6. **VHS/CRT surface.** Soft bloom, fine tape grain, gentle chromatic fringing, a hint of scanlines — explicitly *without* tracking errors, tearing or corruption.
7. **Emblem shapes and cover glue.** Stars, sparkles, sunbursts, stripes, halftone dots; denser corpus flourishes include doodle collages of fruit, dolphins and gear. These are optional seasoning, not canon.

### Materials and textures
Hand-painted cel aesthetic: clean ink outlines, flat saturated fills, one or two hard shadow steps, airbrushed gradient skies and backgrounds. Glossy plastic on cassettes, arcade buttons and headphones; chrome and holographic foil on type; flat paper-flyer printing; a fine VHS grain overlay and CRT phosphor bloom across everything. Absent by definition: marble, stone, rust, grunge, paper decay.

### Recurring subjects
Idols singing into stage microphones; dancers and magical girls mid-transformation; teens flirting, dining, shopping, driving; arcade kids at cabinets; city night crowds; still lifes of tapes and records; type-only gradient covers. Anatomy and face style stay in the 80s/90s cel register — adult or teen proportions, large but era-correct eyes.

### Composition and lighting
The style inherits album-cover, flyer and loop-frame grammar, and the corpus ranges widely: centered waist-up portrait with a type lockup; diagonal dynamic poster layouts; mirrored symmetry and repeated figures; full-bleed illustrated interiors with an even neon wash; a single figure against a maximal doodle collage; or a minimal gradient-and-type cover. Lighting is emissive first: neon signs, screen glow, stage beams and mirror-ball sparkle supply the key light, with soft bloom halos; night grounds stay saturated indigo-violet rather than black. Candy-bright high-key "daylight" variants exist but keep the same neon saturation.

## Mood & Values

Hedonism, escapism, nostalgia, retrofuturism, energy. Future Funk is an uncritical embrace of consumer capitalism's pleasures — a retrofuturist party that never ends, reconstructing a memory of a boom-era Japan the audience never lived. Romance, glamour and optimism are mandatory emotional content; the image should feel like it runs at 120 BPM. Melancholy, irony and decay are foreign bodies.

## Era & Origins

Emergent on SoundCloud and Bandcamp circa 2012–2013, codified by producers such as Saint Pepsi (*Hit Vibes*, 2013), Macross 82-99 (City Pop sampling plus Sailor Moon imagery) and Yung Bae, with the visual language standardized by YouTube curation that paired tracks with looped anime GIFs in the "Rumic World" line; later figures include Night Tempo, Moe Shop (the bridge toward Kawaii Future Bass), Desired and Flamingosis. The style reacts against Vaporwave by refusing its glitch art, marble statues, Windows-95 UI and druggy slowdown, and it borrows its source imagery from 1970s disco culture and 1980s Japanese City Pop album art while refusing their naturalism.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Grade like a neon tube: saturation near maximum, magenta/cyan/violet dominance, colored shadows, blooming highlights.
2. Draw like a 1980s cel: ink outlines, flat fills, hard shadow steps, airbrush gradients.
3. Light like a club: emissive sources — signage, screens, beams, mirror-ball flecks.
4. Set type like a motion graphic: chrome script or 80s serif plus kanji/katakana, starburst badges, arched or diagonal lockups.
5. Degrade like a *good* VHS, not a broken one: bloom and grain, never glitch.
6. Pose like a loop: mid-gesture, repeatable, optionally mirrored or tiled.
7. Feel like a party: optimism, romance, glamour, kinetic joy.

**Variation axes.** Subject: idol stage, arcade interior, neon street, CRT bedroom, transformation sequence, romantic couple, tape/vinyl still life, abstract type poster, doodle-collage portrait, mascot character. Composition: centered cover, diagonal flyer, full-bleed scene, tiled pattern, minimal gradient. Lighting: neon night, stage beams, screen glow, candy high-key. Color handling: full-spectrum neon, magenta/cyan duotone, violet nocturne, pastel collage with neon accents. Mood: euphoric party, dreamy romantic nostalgia, cool nocturne glamour. Medium: cel-anime frame, poster, album cover, event flyer, deskmat pattern. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — palette, cel lineage, emissive light, type attitude, analog warmth, party optimism — not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Glitch slices, datamoshing, tracking errors, corrupted pixels, Windows-95 UI, marble statues, decayed empty malls; overall muted pastel haze, irony, melancholy, dystopia; photorealism, 3D renders, and post-2000s digital-anime rendering with soft airbrushed skin gradients and thin anti-aliased lines; earth tones (brown, olive, terracotta) as dominant palette; grunge textures, tears, heavy scratches; cyberpunk noir grit — rain-slick despair, augmentation, oppressive megacity (neon signage yes, misery no); Memphis postmodern geometry as the primary language; chibi sticker-kawaii as the main register; static, solemn, chopped-and-screwed stillness; American glitch-hop EDM stage iconography, which shares the name but not the aesthetic.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Decisive separation |
|---|---|
| City Pop | The source, not the remix: painterly airbrush realism, plausible daylight and resort palettes, paper-print texture, adult illustrative proportions, no neon duotone wash, no chrome/kanji type overload. Future Funk keeps City Pop's glamour but re-grades it neon and re-draws it as cel loop. |
| Disco | Photographic human dancers, warm amber/gold/sequin interiors, real mirror balls; no anime figures, no magenta-cyan grading. |
| Funk | 1970s earth tones — orange, brown, gold — photographic band grit and print texture; Future Funk contains zero earth tones and zero photographic grit. |
| Kawaii | Pastel-dominant, chibi rounding, cute sans-serif, sticker logic, no retro-cel lineage, no chrome type, no VHS/CRT surface, no nightlife. Future Funk's candy variants still keep 80s cel anatomy, neon saturation, chrome/kanji type and analog music objects. |
| Memphis Design | Matte laminate pattern language: squiggles, terrazzo, primary-plus-black grids, postmodern furniture context; no glow, no anime, no type lockups. Future Funk's density is glowing doodles of music gear around a cel figure, not structural geometry. |

Also separate from its parent: Vaporwave brings decay, irony, muted pastels, glitch and classical statues; Synthwave brings dark horizon grids, chrome sans-serif and laser sunsets without anime cel figures or disco playfulness. Future Funk is the only member of the family that pairs vintage anime line art with undamaged analog warmth and an unironic, never-ending party.
