# Yandere

Yandere (ヤンデレ, from *yanderu* "to be mentally ill" + *deredere* "lovey-dovey") is the visual language of love as a weapon: a character archetype whose devotion curdles into obsession, jealousy and violence, and the edit culture that grew around it. The aesthetic is defined by a single structural law — **the juxtaposition of cute and disturbing imagery** — expressed through pastel pink love-iconography (hearts, cursive notes, blush, ribbons) contaminated by blades, stylized blood, shadowed eyes and possessive, threatening text. It descends from Japanese anime, manga and visual-novel culture (the term went mainstream around 2005 with *School Days* and *Shuffle!*) and overlaps the Japanese *eroguro* (erotic grotesque) tradition and its gurokawa offshoot, but its organizing subject is always **romantic obsession directed at a "darling,"** never gore for its own sake.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette is a four-note chord: **pink, red, white, black**. Treat pink as the ambient world, red as the wound, black as the shadow, white as the paper.

- **Pink (dominant, ambient):** pale blossom through bubblegum to hot magenta — roughly `#FADCE6` → `#F6A9C9` → `#E0559F`. Entire frames may be flooded in a single flat pink, or a manga scan may be tinted monochrome pink.
- **Red (accent, violence):** cherry to dried crimson — `#D22B3A` → `#7A0C1E`. Used for blood splatter, dithered red fields, glowing text on black, and the "hate" half of split emblems.
- **Black (ground, shadow):** near-black `#0A0A0A` for backgrounds behind red text, for the vertical shadow cast over a character's eyes ("darkening"), and for the dark half of love/hate diptychs.
- **White / paper (`#FFF6F9`):** notebook pages, speech bubbles, pale hands, screentone highlights.

Saturation may slide from candy-soft pastel to saturated dithered crimson, but the pink–red–black–white family holds; earth tones, olive grunge, mint/lavender pastel-rainbow and warm golden palettes are outside the style.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how firmly the style documents them)

1. **Knives and blades** — the core object motif; canonically *aestheticized*: pink handles, heart-shaped pommels or blades, scissors and box cutters as substitutes. Blades appear held, offered, pointed at the viewer, or laid as flat graphic icons on pink grounds.
2. **The "yandere face"** — shadowed or vertically shaded eyes, an unsettling small smile, and a deep blush; or its variants: wide unblinking stare, heart-shaped pupils, blank deadpan behind an outstretched hand. The Japanese fan term is "darkening" / "blackening" for the shadow over the face.
3. **Obsession as text** — possessive or threatening first-person phrases ("I love you just a little too much," "you're mine," "love me only," "I'd rip their heads off if they tried"), fake text-message overlays, and phrases repeated line after line until they become wallpaper. Handwritten pen scrawl, pixel/bitmap fonts, and Japanese vertical type all count.
4. **Hearts** — as motifs, filters, stickers, split emblems (half red "I love you" / half black pierced by a knife), and heart-shaped frames or blades.
5. **Stylized blood** — flat splatters, spatter dots, smears and drips in palette reds; never photographic gore.
6. **Anime screencap edits with pastel filters** — frames lifted from anime or visual-novel grammar, tinted pink, often with the character reaching a hand toward the lens.
7. **Manga panels and speech bubbles** — scanned pages tinted monochrome pink or left black-and-white, with obsessive dialogue in the bubbles.
8. **The reaching / grasping hand** — a hand extended toward the viewer in stop-or-grab gesture, or two pale hands reaching from opposite corners of an empty pink field; the POV of being claimed.
9. **School uniforms and student dress** — sailor collars, blazers, ribbons — inherited from the archetype's school-setting origins; present as character costume, not as fashion catalog.
10. **Minor extensions:** syringes, pills and candy-as-weapon graphics, camcorder timestamps and dithered lo-fi overlays borrowed from neighboring edit cultures, collage grids of repeated pink tiles.

### Materials and textures

Flat vector fills and sticker graphics; cel-shaded anime screencap surfaces; manga screentone and scan grain; notebook paper with ballpoint or gel-pen handwriting; Bayer-dithered pixel fields and compressed lo-fi bitmap text; soft-focus bloom and blurred pink washes; grungy paper with smeared red ink. Textures stay *printed or screen-born* — this is an edit-culture aesthetic, not a photographic one.

### Recurring subjects

Anime-style girls and boys (original or archetype-evoking) in the darkened state; intimate or confrontational dyads — lover held, rival faced down, two students close enough to threaten; object still lifes of blade-and-heart; text-only graphics where the sentence is the subject; manga page fragments; handwritten devotion notes and diary pages.

### Composition and lighting

Composition tends toward: centered portrait busts addressing the viewer; first-person POV confrontations (hand or blade toward lens); tight two-figure proximity; flat poster layouts with text as pattern; manga panel grids; minimal empty pink fields with a single gesture or object. Lighting ranges from completely flat graphic light, to muted overcast cel shading, to chiaroscuro where a vertical shadow band blacks out the eyes while the blush stays lit, to black grounds with red glow, to soft pink bloom. Lo-fi compression, dither and scan artifacts are acceptable period flavor.

## Mood & Values

The feeling is **sweetness with a blade under it**: devotion so total it becomes surveillance, possession, threat. The style celebrates intensity — fans love "the extraordinary degree of obsession" — while encoding the instability behind it in the darkened eyes and the too-wide smile. Duality is the moral grammar: love/hate split hearts, "I love you (I hate you)," saccharine color carrying violent sentences. Culturally the aesthetic is contested — criticized for romanticizing abuse and fetishizing mental illness, yet also reclaimed by some mentally ill fans as a coping language and a way to find like-minded others — which is why it insists on being distinct from wholesome Lovecore even where the palettes touch. Menace must read as *relational*: directed at a darling, a rival, or the viewer-as-beloved.

## Era & Origins

The archetype predates its name: proto-yandere characters appear in early-1990s adult visual novels (e.g. 1992), the term was used commercially around 2000, and it went mainstream in Japan in 2005 with *School Days* and *Shuffle!*, spreading to overseas anime communities in the late 2000s. The *visual aesthetic* as documented here is largely a 2010s–2020s internet edit culture — screencap edits, tinted manga scans, Tumblr/TikTok-style graphics, vocaloid-esque music-video typography, and game-derived characters (*Yandere Simulator*, *Doki Doki Literature Club!*) — layered over 2000s visual-novel and anime source grammar. It reacts against wholesome moe romance and Lovecore sweetness by dragging the character's "inside to the outside," as one producer put it: the illness made visible as shadow, blush and blood.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. **Duality law:** every image must couple at least one sweet signifier (pastel pink, hearts, cursive, blush, ribbon, candy) with at least one threat signifier (blade, splatter, shadowed eyes, possessive text, grasping hand). Neither half alone is yandere.
2. **Palette law:** keep pink ambient, red as wound, black as shadow, white as paper. Weighting may shift (pastel-dominant or crimson-dominant) but the family holds.
3. **Face law:** if a face appears, it carries the darkening — shadow band over the eyes, unblinking wide or heart-shaped pupils, heavy blush, small off-key smile — or a blank stare behind a reaching hand.
4. **Text law:** text is diegetic obsession, first-person and possessive; repetition into pattern is canonical; handwritten pen, pixel bitmap fonts, or Japanese vertical type.
5. **Stylized-violence law:** blood is flat splatter, spatter and smear in palette reds; blades are props with cute finishes. Gore is graphic-design, never visceral.
6. **Medium law:** pick one register and keep its texture coherent — anime screencap edit, pink-tinted manga scan, flat vector poster/sticker, handwritten-note photo, dithered pixel graphic.
7. **Gaze law:** the subject addresses the viewer or the darling directly — reaching, grasping, holding, offering a blade. Intimacy is confrontation.

**Variation axes (what may change)**

- *Subject:* character portrait, dyad, object still life, text-only graphic, manga fragment, empty pink field with hands.
- *Palette weighting:* pale pastel flood ↔ saturated magenta ↔ dithered crimson-and-black ↔ monochrome pink scan.
- *Gore level:* zero blood (blade implied) → spatter dots → full stylized splatter.
- *Text density:* none → single caption → all-over repeating wallpaper.
- *Medium/period flavor:* 2000s visual-novel screencap ↔ 2010s Tumblr edit ↔ pixel web graphic ↔ scanned notebook.
- *Composition:* centered bust, POV reach, tight dyad, poster grid, panel grid, minimal field.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A still life of a heart-shaped pink pair of scissors on a pale pink ground with one line of pen scrawl is as valid as a darkened schoolgirl portrait.

**Avoid**

- Wholesome reciprocal romance with no menace (slides into Lovecore).
- Photographic or visceral gore: open wounds, viscera, realistic blood texture.
- Earth-tone or olive-brown grunge, desaturated palettes without pink/red.
- Erotic framing, lingerie, seductive pose — menace here is not seduction.
- Uniforms presented as pure fashion catalog with no obsessive narrative.
- Western live-action fandom characters or franchise logo moodboards without anime grammar.
- Devouring imagery: teeth, biting, flesh, eating-as-gore.
- Clean pastel sweetness with no threat signifier; rainbow fairy-kei pastels.
- Action-shonen dynamism, mecha, sports energy; bright heroic lighting.
- Gloom-cute melancholy with no love object (that drifts to gurokawa/yami kawaii).

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | How it differs from Yandere |
|---|---|
| **Nanchatte Seifuku** | Uniform-as-fashion: real-world sailor suits and blazers photographed or styled for charm, no blades, no blood, no possessive text, no darkened eyes. Yandere may wear the uniform, but only as costume for obsession; remove the menace and you leave yandere. |
| **2010s Dark Fandom** | Fandom-anchored edits of western film/TV/game properties, dark moodboards, often desaturated or black-white with franchise sigils and ship typography. Yandere is anime/manga-grammared, pink-red dominated, and its darkness is specifically love-obsession, not franchise gloom. |
| **Animecore** | Broad anime nostalgia: screencaps, VHS haze, bright or wistful palettes, everyday scenes. Yandere borrows the screencap surface but always inserts the threat signifier — shadowed eyes, blade, splatter, possessive text — that Animecore lacks. |
| **Cannibalcore** | Violence organized around consumption: teeth, biting, devouring flesh, visceral earthy gore. Yandere's violence is instrumental to love (eliminate rivals, keep the darling), rendered as flat pink-red splatter and cute-finished blades, never eating. |
| **Erokawa** | Erotic-cute: sensual allure, seductive pose and styling, "ero-kawaii" fashion and photography. Yandere shares the cute-plus-transgression structure and an eroguro ancestor, but its transgression is obsession and threat, not sexuality; the gaze claims rather than invites. |

Two further border notes: **Lovecore** uses the same hearts-and-pink vocabulary with zero menace — the blade or threat-text is the tell — and **Gurokawa / Yami Kawaii** share gloom-cute and medical motifs but lack yandere's defining directed obsession: a darling, a rival, a "you" being loved too much.
