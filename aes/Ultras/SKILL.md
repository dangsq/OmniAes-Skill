# Ultras

Ultras are organised, fanatical football supporter groups whose visual language is a deliberately planned war-theatre of devotion: whole stands converted into mosaics and painted cloth, air filled with tinted smoke, faces erased into a single anonymous body. The style began in 1960s Italy and spread worldwide, absorbing the wardrobe of 1970s political street militancy (parkas, camouflage, balaclavas) and the brand codes of terrace sportswear (Adidas, Stone Island, Fred Perry, The North Face, C.P. Company). Its imagery oscillates between two registers: the ecstatic spectacle — flares, tifos, chanting walls of bodies — and the cold waiting room of the away day — empty metal bleachers, feet dangling over concrete, crews standing in silence. Both registers share one rule: the individual disappears into the group's colours.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The palette is two-layered. **Layer one is a monochrome field:** black hoodies and balaclavas (#0b0b0d–#26262b), concrete and painted-steel greys (#6e6e73–#9a9a9f), smoke white (#d8d8d8–#f2f2f2). Entire images may be rendered as high-contrast black-and-white or a desaturated cold grade, and this is fully canonical. **Layer two is saturated intrusion:** colour enters only as light or pigment, never as wardrobe pattern — flare orange-red (#d21f1f–#ff4a1f), smoke-bomb columns in club hues (signal red, magenta-pink #d6336c–#e8579a, ultramarine #1f3fae, terrace green #0f7a3d), and banner paint primaries including sun yellow (#f2c200). At most one or two accents dominate a monochrome scene. The single exception is the painted banner or flag as an *object*, which may carry the full primary set on a black ground with checkered or diagonal-striped borders, because it is heraldry, not atmosphere.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the culture documents them)

1. **Tifos** — choreographed stand-wide displays: mosaic panels, card stunts, enormous hand-painted cloths stretching across entire tiers, sometimes layered or moving.
2. **Banners and flags** — group names in bold, slightly uneven hand-painted condensed capitals with outlines; snarling mascots (bulldogs, birds of prey); club symbols; founding years; checkered or striped cloth borders; visible creases and paint cracks.
3. **Pyrotechnics** — hand-held flares spitting white-gold sparks, smoke bombs vomiting dense tinted columns that backlit floodlights turn into architecture.
4. **Face coverings** — balaclavas, scarves pulled over the nose, neckerchiefs, ski goggles; anonymity as uniform and as intimidation.
5. **The terrace body** — figures straddling railings and barriers, arms spread or fists raised in unison, drummers beating atop rails, crowds compressed into a single waving mass.
6. **Stadium vernacular** — curved concrete tiers, flaking painted railings, floodlight masts, empty aluminium bleachers, tunnel mouths, chain-link and netting.
7. **Graffiti and murals** of group names and symbols on urban walls (well documented in the culture's own accounts; thinner in surviving imagery, so treat as a legitimate but secondary motif).
8. **The dress code** — monochrome technical wear: hooded tops, green parkas and camouflage combat jackets inherited from 1970s street politics, Adidas three stripes on sleeves and trainers, small chest badges (Stone Island, Fred Perry laurel, C.P. Company), Alpha Industries MA-1s, The North Face puffers. Understated signals, never logo saturation.
9. **Slogan lettering** — "Against Modern Football", "never on your knees", group mottos painted or stencilled onto cloth and walls.

### Materials and textures

Creased painted canvas, polyester flag sheen, wool scarf knit, matte nylon and waxed cotton, concrete dust and rain-stained steps, flaking painted steel, cardboard shoeboxes and paper fanzines in the leisure register, adhesive sticker stacks in collage layouts. Smoke grain over everything.

### Recurring subjects

A masked sentinel alone on a railing with a burning flare; a whole curved stand holding a tifo; first-person views of own sneakers and jeans dangling over a barrier above a packed tier; a circle of trainers photographed top-down as a crew arrives; hooded figures waiting on empty away-day bleachers; a banner photographed flat as a pure object; drummers and capos mid-chant; cut-out fanzine collages mixing sneakers, badges, masked portraits and painted lettering.

### Composition and lighting

Low-angle hero shots against a smoke-filled sky; first-person terrace POV looking down or at one's own feet; wide arcs of the curved stand; tight crops on cloth, lettering and footwear; tilted horizons and edge-crowded snapshot framing; collage grids with torn paper edges. Lighting is night floodlight cutting through haze, flare-as-key-light casting red rims on black cloth, hard on-camera flash in collage sources, or flat overcast daylight for the waiting and travel register. High ISO grain, motion blur and high-contrast black-and-white documentary treatment are all canonical finishes.

## Mood & Values

Fanatical loyalty performed as collective ritual: depersonalisation that produces belonging, intimidation staged as love for the club. Flares burn like votive candles; chants and drums give the images an implied roar. Underneath runs defiance — anti-commercialisation ("Against Modern Football"), nonconformism, territorial pride, the insistence of never being on your knees. The mood spans euphoric communion, mournful vigil, cold menace and plain boredom; all four are authentically Ultra.

## Era & Origins

Coined in Italy around 1969 (Sampdoria's and Torino's first "Ultras" groups), the movement took its militant wardrobe from the 1970s years of street political turmoil — parkas, camouflage, covered faces borrowed from urban guerrilla dress — and its brand codes from terrace sportswear lineage (Mod, skinhead, casuals ancestry). It globalised into Casuals in the UK, Barra Bravas in Hispanic America, Torcidas Organizadas in Brazil, and scenes from Poland to Egypt to Thailand, with descendant brands like Weekend Offender and Ma.strum confirming the aesthetic's continuing life.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. The group is the protagonist: even a lone figure must imply a collective behind them (smoke, banner edge, crowd hands at frame).
2. Erase the face: cover, crop or turn it; identity lives in cloth, colour and lettering.
3. Monochrome ground, saturated intrusion: keep clothing and architecture in black-grey-white; let colour arrive only as smoke, flare glow or painted cloth.
4. Heraldry must look hand-painted — uneven edges, condensed outlined capitals, aggressive mascots, checkered or striped borders on creased fabric.
5. Smoke is structural: volumetric columns organise the frame and catch backlight.
6. Stage everything on terrace geometry: railings, concrete steps, curved tiers, floodlight masts.
7. Keep the documentary flaw: grain, blur, tilted horizon, flash hotspot, or committed black-and-white. Never advertising polish.
8. Brand codes whisper: three stripes on a sleeve, a small chest badge — never all-over logomania or gold bling.

**Variation axes.** Subject: tifo wide shot, flare sentinel, feet-over-railing POV, trainer circle, banner-as-object, graffiti wall, drummer, away-day bleacher waiting, travel scene, fanzine collage. Composition: low-angle hero, first-person, wide mosaic, tight crop, collage grid. Lighting: floodlit night haze, flare-only key, overcast daylight, high-contrast monochrome. Colour: pure black-and-white; monochrome plus a single smoke hue; a saturated smoke field swallowing the figure; full-primary painted cloth as object. Mood: euphoria, vigil, standoff, boredom. Era and place: 1970s parka-and-camo Italy through contemporary technical jackets anywhere in the world; women ultras with covered faces are equally canonical. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Unmasked smiling faces posed to camera; clean broadcast graphics or commercial stadium marketing; balanced multi-hue or pastel palettes; luxury logomania, gold chains, bling; vector-crisp digital typography on banners; symmetrical CGI-perfect smoke; sunny family crowds, hi-vis stewards, picnic terraces; pub-and-replica-shirt nostalgia; ribbons, skirts and coquette femininity; squatting-on-estate slapstick; neon rave glow without club heraldry; and any literal extremist or hate symbols — political charge is conveyed through slogans, militancy and covered faces, never through prohibited iconography.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | Their tells | Ultras difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Casuals** | Garment is the hero: designer sportswear, badge close-ups, trainer circles, visible faces, street/club/pub settings | Same brands, but subordinated to masked collective spectacle, pyro, banners and stadium architecture; faces hidden |
| **Blokecore** | Vintage replica shirts, dad jeans, pints, ironic daylight nostalgia, faces shown | No masks, no pyro, no choreography in blokecore; ultras is organised, anonymous, smoke-lit |
| **Blokette** | Football shirts fused with skirts, ribbons, bows, pastel coquette playfulness | Ultras is militant monochrome and depersonalised; femininity appears only inside the masked terrace code |
| **Chav** | Individual peacocking: bling, caps, head-to-toe check, tracksuits, estate-street swagger | Ultras is collective, understated, technical, stadium-bound; status signalled discreetly, not shouted |
| **Dresiarz** | Polish slab-block backdrop, full tracksuit sets, squatting on haunches, comedic small-crew lethargy | Ultras adds organised spectacle, painted heraldry, ideology and smoke; dread of the estate is not the subject |

Adjacent energies to keep distinct: rave shares night smoke and collective euphoria but runs neon and lacks club heraldry; skinhead and Mod ancestry shows in brand lineage (Ben Sherman, Fred Perry) but not in stadium spectacle. Where the leisure register of ultras imagery (trainer walls, shoebox stacks, collages) blurs toward Casuals, the deciding cue remains: anonymity, cloth heraldry and the threat or memory of smoke.
