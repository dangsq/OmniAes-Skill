# Minet

Minets — also called the Minets du Drugstore, the Bande du Drugstore or Blousons Dorés — were a short-lived subculture of wealthy Parisian teenagers of the early 1960s, named in 1965 after their habit of congregating at the Drugstore Publicis on the Champs-Élysées, a hybrid pharmacy-newsstand-record shop-restaurant open until 2 a.m. They rejected French cultural values, especially the chirpy yé-yé pop mainstream, and styled themselves instead on English schoolboy and provincial country dress filtered through Mod sharpness: clothes bought second-hand at the St-Ouen flea market because anything new was *déclassé*. Contemporaries summed them up as "fake beatniks, dandies who hate everything French." The scene died with the assimilation and politicization that followed May 1968, leaving behind a photographic record of street reportage, party snapshots, and a later pop-graphic afterlife.

## Visual Identity

### Palette: two documented registers

The style lives in two color worlds, and a faithful image commits to one or deliberately bridges them.

- **Documentary monochrome (the period record).** Nearly all period photography of Minets is black-and-white or faintly sepia: silver-gelatin greys from near-black `#1A1A1A` through mid grey `#7A7A7A` to paper white `#E9E7E2`, with visible grain; warm snapshot fades toward `#D9C7A7`. Overcast Paris daylight flattens contrast; night interiors go hard-flash black-and-white with blown highlights.
- **Wardrobe and poster color (the style's own accents).** Color enters through cloth and graphics, never through environment. Shetland sweaters in canary yellow `#F2D21F`–`#FFE135`, candy pink `#F4A7B9`–`#FFB6C1`, pale blue `#AEC6CF`–`#BDD7EE`; oxford white `#F4F2EC`; tweed browns and houndstooth greys `#6B5B45`, `#8A8578`; gabardine beige `#C3B091`; corduroy tan, olive and rust `#A9793F`, `#6E6B3E`, `#8C4A2F`; velvet suits in deep burgundy, bottle green or navy `#4A2C2A`, `#2E3B2E`, `#1F2A44`; brass-gold buttons `#C9A227`. The pop poster register uses a flat canary field with black silhouetted figures and an orange-red logotype `#E84A1F`.

Rule: saturation is rationed. In color images, vivid hue belongs to a sweater, a poster field or a small emblem; everything structural — coats, trousers, street, skin — stays in cloth neutrals or monochrome. Honest caveat: the color record is thin; period hues are known mainly from written garment descriptions and one modern poster interpretation, so treat the pastel trio plus cloth neutrals as the sanctioned color space rather than a reproduced swatch.

### Signature motifs and objects, ranked by how firmly the text documents them

1. **The drugstore itself.** Lit lettered signage spelling DRUGSTORE over a wide storefront, glowing at dusk or night; interiors with magazine racks, record counters, late-night restaurant tables. The single most identifying setting.
2. **The uniform code.** Oxford shirts with button-down collars; blazers with gold buttons and embroidered "British" crests; tweed jackets in houndstooth or flannel; fitted gabardine trench coats; tailored velvet suits; five-pocket corduroy trousers worn deliberately short; footwear shown off — Clarks desert boots, Carvil zip boots, JM Weston loafers; round-neck, often short-sleeved Shetland sweaters in the pastel trio.
3. **Coiffed, androgynous youth.** Voluminous combed hair, side partings or brushed-forward fringes reaching the ears, on slim, pale, slightly androgynous teenagers of both sexes; the look is groomed, never scruffy.
4. **The Parisian stage.** Champs-Élysées vistas, the Arc de Triomphe as backdrop, Haussmannian stone façades, wrought-iron railings, news kiosks (presse), café terraces, period sedans parked along the curb.
5. **Social ritual.** Crowded party rooms ("boums", rallyes) in dark suits and white shirts; gangs leaning and lounging in doorways and against shopfronts; couples pairing off on steps; the posture of being seen.
6. **Anglophile emblems.** A small Union Jack device used as a logo or badge; British music magazines such as *Melody Maker*; pirate-radio dials; record-shop bins.
7. **Pop poster grammar (modern reinterpretation).** Flat canary ground, black silhouette cut-outs of the gang, condensed orange-red logotype, inset object details.

### Materials and textures

Cloth carries the meaning: napped tweed and flannel, the diagonal rib of gabardine, the wale of corduroy, crushed velvet pile, fuzzy Shetland knit, soft-washed oxford cotton with rolled collars, scuffed polished leather at the shoe. Everything must read *worn*: faded elbows, creased knees, softened shoulders, flea-market patina. Crispness is a category error — newness was déclassé. Photographic texture is part of the style: film grain, halftone newsprint dot, sepia fade, flash flare, or the flat matte ink of silkscreen poster work.

### Recurring subjects

Groups of three to eight teenagers in loose clusters; a lone full-length figure presented like a fashion plate; couples at a party; youths framed by a shopfront or sign; magazine-reading and record-browsing at counters; street tableaux with traffic and pedestrians as ambient life.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Candid reportage framing: waist- or eye-level, subjects caught mid-glance, often looking back over the shoulder at the camera; wide cinematic crops of street crowds; full-body isolation on a plain white ground with inset detail objects and a vertical column of type (advertising-plate grammar); signage used as a horizontal band anchoring the top of the frame; dense party tableaux packed edge to edge. Light is available and unforced: flat overcast daylight outdoors, neon and storefront glow at dusk, direct on-camera flash in dark interiors, high-key even light for plate-style figures. Depth of field stays documentary — everything legible, nothing dreamily blurred.

## Mood & Values

Cool, aloof, faintly sneering insouciance — the pursuit of *branché* status. The values are contrarian aristocracy: moneyed kids playing at second-hand shabbiness to insult the bourgeoisie they come from; Anglophilia as rebellion against Frenchness; androgyny as distinction from other Parisian trends; gang loyalty and gatekept sociability (exchanged addresses, exclusive parties); mischief with a straight face (the prank of sending strangers to a "fantastic party" at the Paris Morgue); nocturnality — the scene lives after dark, until 2 a.m. Beneath the pose is genuine cultural allegiance: British Invasion rock and American rhythm and blues, pirate radio tuned against French stations, contempt for yé-yé.

## Era & Origins

Paris, early 1960s; the term coined 1965; decline after May 1968, when members drifted into student activism or back into bourgeois life. The style reacts against three things at once: French bourgeois conformity, the yé-yé pop mainstream, and the idea of new clothes as status. It borrows from Mod London and from the American drugstore concept transplanted to the Champs-Élysées in 1958, and it sits genealogically between Teddy Boy dandyism and later European pop-youth styles such as Paninaro. Period lock: roughly 1960–1968, with 1963–1967 as the core.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Fix time and place first: 1960s Paris,Haussmannian stone, French storefront lettering, period cars as background props only.
2. Choose one register and hold it: grainy monochrome street reportage; warm sepia snapshot; hard-flash night interior; high-key white fashion plate; or flat pop poster on canary yellow.
3. Dress every figure by the code, and age every garment: crest blazer, tweed, trench, velvet, corduroy cropped at the ankle, leather loafers or zip boots, pastel Shetland knit. At least one figure should show the shortened-trouser-over-shoe detail.
4. Stage the social: clusters, lounging, glances back at camera, party density, or the lone plate figure. Solitude without pose is off-key; the Minet is always aware of being looked at.
5. Let the drugstore logic appear even when the Drugstore does not: lit signage, news kiosks, magazine racks, record bins, late-night restaurant windows.
6. Ration color per the palette rule; keep hue in knitwear, emblems and poster fields.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be street, storefront, party, shop interior, portrait plate, or poster graphic — a record counter, a rallye in a private apartment, a kiss on stone steps, a gang silhouetted on a yellow field are all valid. Composition may range from wide cinematic crowd crops to tight two-figure over-shoulder frames to isolated full-body plates. Lighting may be overcast flat, dusk neon, hard flash, or studio high-key. Color handling may be full monochrome, sepia, or pastel-accented. Mood may slide from aloof cool to mischievous to nocturnal glamour. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — no specific reference composition is canonical.

**Avoid.**
- Denim jeans, leather rocker jackets, greaser or hippie hair; flares and psychedelia (post-1968 drift).
- Crisp, brand-new preppy clothes, American Ivy brightness, chinos-and-polo cheer; anything that reads Nantucket rather than St-Ouen flea market.
- Yé-yé pop staging: matching girl-group dresses, twirling, primary-color television sets, sunny optimism.
- Beatnik signs: black turtleneck plus beret plus sunglasses, Left Bank cellar jazz, deliberate bohemian poverty.
- Mod hardware: scooters, parkas, target roundels, all-over Union Jack graphics (the Minet Jack is a small emblem, not a wallpaper).
- Dandy peacockry: cravats, canes, top hats, brocade, overt ornament.
- Britpop nineties: track tops, bucket hats, sportswear logos, oversized silhouettes, lad culture.
- May 1968 protest iconography (barricades, paste-up posters) as the subject; that is the scene's ending, not its image.
- Modern digital tells: HDR saturation, razor-sharp noise-free render, contemporary props, anachronistic signage typefaces.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Minet |
|---|---|
| **Beatnik** | Beatniks wear black, berets and sunglasses in Left Bank cellars and project intellectual poverty; Minets wear pastel knits, crests and corduroy on the Right Bank's commercial avenues and project moneyed disdain. Monod's "fake beatniks" is the diagnostic: same aloofness, opposite wardrobe and tax bracket. |
| **Mod** | Mods are British, scooter- and parka-bound, with target roundels and sharp Italian suits; Minets are Parisian imitators who swap scooters for storefronts, Italian suits for flea-market English ones, and add cropped corduroy, trench coats and velvet — and they appear against Haussmannian stone and DRUGSTORE lettering, not London terraces. |
| **French New Wave** | New Wave is a cinematic grammar — handheld spontaneity, mixed ages, cigarettes, newspapers, existential cafés — while Minet is a youth-consumer scene: teenage gangs, uniform codes, lit commercial signage, party rooms. Overlap is monochrome Paris; the difference is uniformity of dress and the consumer backdrop. |
| **Dandy** | Dandies ornament (cravat, cane, peacock color) across any era; Minets subtract — schoolboy and country English pieces, worn down, androgynous rather than florid. The dandy attitude is present, the dandy costume is not. |
| **Britpop** | Britpop revives Union Jack irony in 1990s casualwear and lad posture; Minet's Anglophilia is sincere 1960s consumption of British imports, expressed in tailoring and knitwear, photographed in grainy monochrome, never in sportswear. |
| **Preppy / Yé-yé (honorable mentions)** | Preppy demands newness and American campus brightness — exactly what Minets called déclassé; yé-yé is the rejected mainstream, all sunshine and matching outfits. If the clothes look freshly bought or the mood looks eager, it is not Minet. |

In one sentence: look for worn English tailoring on coiffed Parisian teenagers, pastel knit against grey stone, lit commercial signage at night, and a pose of practiced cool — affluent, Anglophile, anti-yé-yé, and gone by 1968.
