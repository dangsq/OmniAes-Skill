# Purism

Purism is the painted doctrine of order that emerged in Paris immediately after the First World War. In *Après le cubisme* (1918), Amédée Ozenfant and Le Corbusier attacked late Cubism for having decayed into decoration, personality and irrationality, and proposed instead an art of clear thinking: pure primary forms, standardized subjects, and the functional elegance of the machine as moral model. Propagated through the journal *L'Esprit Nouveau* and practiced by Ozenfant, Le Corbusier, Fernand Léger and circle associates such as Serge Charchoune and Nikos Engonopoulos, the movement lasted only until about 1925 in painting, but its logic of standardized types, flat planes and rational composition flowed directly into Le Corbusier's architecture and, later, the International Typographic Style. A Purist picture behaves like a well-made object: anonymous, balanced, exact, and quietly monumental.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)

Purist color is subdued, flat and limited. The working range is an earthy, plaster-and-machine family: bone and off-white, warm and cool grays, ochre and sand, umber and dark brown, olive and gray-green, with muted blue-gray, dusty rose or pale lavender as cool accents. Within this subdued field, a single plane — usually the ground or a table mass — may carry one saturated note: oxide red, deep bottle green, or near-black. That saturated plane stays solitary; it never multiplies into polychromy. Value steps are close and calm; contrast comes from plane-against-plane, not from light-against-dark. Gradation, when present, is a smooth tonal sweep across a curved surface (cylinder, cone, sphere), reading like turned metal or molded plaster, never like atmospheric chiaroscuro.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual authority

1. **Standardized vessels**: bottles, carafes, glasses and pipes — the movement's first-named subjects, drawn as invariant types rather than individual things.
2. **The guitar or stringed-instrument silhouette**, inherited from Cubism but restored to a whole, readable outline.
3. **Pitchers, jugs and bowls** — domestic vessel types documented among the movement's still lifes.
4. **Machine elements**: wheels, handles, levers, funnels, stacked cylinders and bolted plates — the "machine aesthetic" made literal, often cropped close by the frame edge.
5. **Architectonic armature**: the table treated as a plinth or horizontal band, background as flat shaped fields, and occasional fluted or scalloped edges on forms, echoing turned metal or classical fluting. Secondary but recurrent.

Objects are monumentalized: a bottle can occupy the height of the canvas, dignified like a column.

### Materials, surface, texture

Matte oil or gouache-like paint on canvas or board, smoothed until brushwork disappears. Edges are hard and drafted, as if ruled and compassed; surfaces read as planes of even color with at most a faint tooth of the support. No impasto, no glaze shine, no gestural trace. Where forms curve, modeling is a fine, almost airbrushed gradation — the look of a machined surface, not of paint handled by a temperament.

### Composition

Frontal and shallow. Depth is produced by overlapping silhouettes and by stacking planes, never by perspective recession. Objects align to verticals and horizontals; the table edge or a background seam supplies a stabilizing band. Compositions cluster centrally or stack vertically like masonry, with weights carefully balanced; cropping at the frame edge is accepted, especially for machine subjects. Negative space is itself a shaped plane, planned with the same care as the objects.

### Lighting

Diffuse, sourceless, even. There is no raking light, no theatrical highlight, and cast shadows — if present at all — appear as flat, dark shaped planes beneath or behind objects rather than as projected illusions. The only "light" is the internal tonal logic of gradated cylinders and the quiet value contrast between planes.

## Mood & Values

Order, rationality, precision, universality, functionalism. Purism is art as clear thinking: "create faithfully, precisely, squarely." Its calm is almost civic — a post-war assertion that clarity and standardization are moral goods, that the anonymous type outranks the personal gesture, and that ornament is a lie. The mood is solemn, contemplative, machine-serene; emotion is present only as the satisfaction of resolved proportion.

## Era & Origins

Paris, c. 1918–1925. Founded by Ozenfant and Le Corbusier in *Après le cubisme* (1918) as a corrective to decorative late Cubism; theorized in *L'Esprit Nouveau*; associated with Léger's machine imagery. Short-lived as painting, foundational as theory: its principles seed Le Corbusier's architecture and, downstream, the grid-and-type rationalism of the International Typographic Style.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Choose a standardized, everyday object type and distill it to its invariant silhouette; keep the outline whole and clean.
2. Build the picture from primary forms — cylinder, cone, cube, circle, flat rectilinear plane — arranged architectonically.
3. Keep color flat, matte and subdued; allow at most one saturated plane.
4. Model curved surfaces with smooth lathe-like gradation; suppress cast shadows and directional light.
5. Organize space frontally: overlap and stack planes instead of using perspective.
6. Purge ornament, narrative, personality and brush gesture; the hand should not be visible.
7. Treat negative space as a planned shape, and let the frame crop forms confidently when the subject is mechanical.

**Variation axes.** A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.
- *Subject*: any vessel or domestic type (bottle, carafe, glass, pipe, pitcher, bowl, guitar) or machine element (wheel, lever, funnel, bolted plate, cylinder stack); assemblages of such objects are equally valid. The text documents still life and machine themes only; figure and landscape subjects lie outside the evidenced range.
- *Composition*: centered cluster, vertical stack, horizontal frieze, or close-cropped mechanical fragment; symmetrical or carefully weighted asymmetry.
- *Lighting*: uniformly diffuse; gradation modeling optional in strength, from nearly flat to softly turned.
- *Color handling*: full earthy range; overall key may be pale (bone, pastel gray-blue, rose) or somber (umber, olive, gray); the single saturated ground plane is optional.
- *Mood*: from serene and luminous to grave and monumental, always controlled.

**Avoid.**
- Cubist fragmentation: faceted planes, multiple viewpoints, shuffled space.
- Ornament, pattern for its own sake, decorative richness of any kind.
- Expressive brushwork, impasto, drips, visible gesture, gloss.
- Deep perspectival space, strong directional light, dramatic cast shadows.
- Many-hued saturated polychromy; primary-color diagrammatic grids.
- Dynamic diagonals, speed, movement, agitprop energy.
- Collage elements: pasted paper, wood-grain simulacra, cubist lettering.
- Narrative, portraiture, emotional expression, anecdote.
- Photorealistic rendering or digital glow gradients.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Decisive tell |
|---|---|
| Precisionism | American factories and machines depicted in situ with cooler metallic gleam and more illusionistic depth; Purism stays in the studio with vessel types, earthy matte planes and shallow stacked space. |
| Constructivism | Non-objective or material-based abstraction, dynamic diagonals, industrial materials, red-black agitprop energy; Purism remains representational, static, frontal, muted. |
| Cubism | Fragmented, multi-viewpoint objects, busy shuffled space, papier collé and ornamental accumulation; Purism restores whole silhouettes, single frontal view, ordered placement — it was founded as a critique of exactly this. |
| De Stijl | Pure abstraction with primary red-yellow-blue plus black and white on an orthogonal line grid; Purism depicts curved object silhouettes in subdued earthy color. |
| International Typographic Style | Graphic design: sans-serif type, photography, asymmetric grid on white; Purism is a painted image without typography — the relation is genealogical, not visual. |

Borderline note: works at the movement's edge (particularly Charchoune's cubist-titled compositions) retain facetting and collage-like warmth; treat persistent faceting as drift toward Cubism, not as core Purism. Conversely, Léger-adjacent tubular machine forms are fully within the style so long as outlines stay whole and color stays subdued.
