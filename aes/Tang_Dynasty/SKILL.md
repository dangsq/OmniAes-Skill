# Tang Dynasty (Tang China, 618–907)

The Tang aesthetic is the visual language of a confident, cosmopolitan empire at peak prosperity — especially the Kaiyuan era — where Central Plains grace absorbed Hu (Central Asian) customs, Silk Road goods and Buddhist iconography without losing its own order. It values plumpness as beauty, gorgeousness as virtue and openness as strength: full-bodied court ladies, glossy tri-color glazed camels, gold bowls heavy with lotus petals, and brocades whose medallions bloom in gold on teal. Everything is abundant, but nothing is chaotic; richness is always held inside symmetry, smooth contour and a warm, even light.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

Tang images speak in one of four coordinated registers; a single image may blend two, but the warm mid-value ground is constant. Never pure white, never black-dominated, never neon.

| Register | Ground / body | Accents | Typical hex range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silk-mineral painting | aged silk tan, ochre | vermilion, malachite green, azurite blue, lead white, ink line | ground `#C9AE86–#B08D5F`; reds `#A63A2B–#C44536`; greens `#3E7A5E–#57906B`; blues `#2F5D8A–#3C6E9F`; white `#F2EDE2`; line `#2A2622` |
| Sancai glaze | cream/beige clay | lead green, amber-yellow, rare cobalt, occasional black glaze | cream `#EADFC6–#F2E9D5`; green `#5C7A3A–#3E7C6F`; amber `#C98A2D–#E3B04B`; cobalt `#274B8F`; black `#221D18` |

Rule of thumb: saturation is mineral, not electric; gold behaves as pattern-metal (rosettes, rims, chased bands), never as an ambient glow; greens lean jade-to-teal rather than emerald.

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Baoxiang rosette** — the composite peony/lotus medallion in radial symmetry, on brocade, sancai pillows and dish centers; the style's calling card.
2. **Tang sancai glaze manner** — dripping, pooling lead glazes in green/amber/cream over pale clay bodies.
3. **Plump court ladies** — round faces, small clustered features, full soft bodies, high chignons, layered trailing robes.
4. **Pearl roundels (lianzhu wen)** — beaded rings enclosing paired beasts, birds or hunters, inherited from Sassanid silk trade.
5. **Scroll grass and honeysuckle vines** — undulating tendrils that bind surfaces together.
6. **Grape-and-sea-beast relief** — the dense chased background of bronze mirror backs.
7. **Camels and foreign (Hu) figures** — bearded musicians, grooms, envoys in翻-collared Hu clothing; the cosmopolitan signature.
8. **Hunting scenes** — galloping riders, bows, fleeing prey inside roundels or scroll fields.
9. **Fine horses** — arched necks, round rumps, thin legs, ornate saddlery.
10. **"Wu's flowing drapery" line** — smooth, rounded, ribbon-like contour that carries the figure before color does.
11. **Architectural grammar** — huge bracket sets roughly half the column height, gently sloped roofs with far-reaching eaves, no spandrel brackets, straight-bar lattice windows, lotus-carved column bases; grand massing with modest ornament.
12. **Lobed metalware forms** — petal-sided bowls, octagonal cups, repoussé parrots, mandarin ducks and auspicious beasts on gold and silver.

### Materials and textures

Silk weave with age-toned patina; matte mineral pigment sitting flat on cloth; glossy lead glaze with visible runs, drips and pooled rims; hammered and chased gold/silver with crisp repoussé relief; cast bronze mirror backs in soft silvery patina; twill brocade with floated weft patterns; matte timber, plaster and clay tile; mural plaster with oxidized, darkened flesh tones (the Dunhuang overlap). Texture contrast is deliberate: slick glaze against dry silk, bright gold against dull patina.

### Recurring subjects

Court ladies at leisure (preparing silk, spring outings, games, fans, cranes); horses with grooms; camel-borne musician troupes; hunting parties; foreign envoys and groomed tribute animals; Buddhist figures and flying apsaras (overlap zone); temple halls and towered palace compounds; vessels, mirrors, pillows and textile swatches as heroes in their own right.

### Composition and lighting

Line first, shade second: contour defines form, color fills inside it, and modeling is minimal. Figure painting prefers the horizontal handscroll logic — processional groups spaced as islands on an empty warm ground, breathing room as important as figures — or a vertical field for a single dignified figure. Textiles, mirrors and dish centers use radial or mirrored-pair symmetry. Architecture is axial and symmetrical, viewed frontally or in slightly elevated three-quarter. Objects sit frontally, centered, at rest. Lighting is diffuse and shadowless: no single source, no cast shadows, no chiaroscuro; specular highlights appear only as glaze gloss and metal glint.

## Mood & Values

Prosperity made visible: plump vitality, gorgeous ornament, ceremonial calm. The style expresses openness and inclusiveness — foreign faces, fabrics and beasts are welcomed as prestige, not exoticized as other — and an ordered grandeur where abundance never tips into clutter. Confidence without aggression; elegance without fragility.

## Era & Origins

Emerges with the dynasty in 618 CE in China, flowering in the Kaiyuan period (8th century) around the capital Chang'an, a Silk Road terminus. It builds on Six Dynasties and Sui foundations but reacts against insularity: Hu fashion, imported metalwork and Buddhist art are absorbed into Central Plains grammar. Modern revival runs through the Hanfu movement, cultural-creative design and film (e.g. *Chang'an*, 2023), which re-popularized the plump-lady silhouette and sancai palette. Note honestly: the wiki's jade and statue coverage is thin — evidence amounts to pale celadon-white lobed cups and bowls with honeysuckle carving — so treat jade as a minor register.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Pick a material register first (silk painting, sancai object, brocade/metal pattern, architecture, mural) and keep its physics consistent throughout the image.
2. Draw with smooth rounded contour; let drapery flow in ribbon-like folds; model with line and flat mineral fills, not shading.
3. Honor the plump canon: full faces and bodies, small features, poised posture.
4. Where pattern appears, make it symmetrical — radial rosettes, beaded roundel rings, mirrored beast pairs.
5. Keep the ground warm and mid-value: aged silk tan, cream clay, teal silk, plaster white — never studio pure white or void black.
6. Light everything diffusely; reserve gloss for glaze and metal.
7. Feel free to cast the cosmopolitan world: Hu musicians, camels, envoys, tribute horses alongside Central Plains courtiers.
8. Compose for abundance-with-order: rich surface, clear axis or rhythm.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any court, trade, ritual, equestrian, architectural or vessel subject of the era — a polo match, a market street, a sancai incense burner, a woven band, none of which need appear in any reference. Composition: horizontal scroll strip, vertical hanging field, frontal object study, radial medallion, axial architectural elevation. Lighting: uniform silk light, soft studio object light, matte mural light. Color handling: silk-mineral, sancai triad, gold-on-teal brocade, gold/silver metal, vermilion-and-grey architecture. Mood: festive, ceremonial, serene, triumphal.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Neon or electric saturation and cool industrial greys; modern flat-vector poster language with bold typography and pop gradients (Guochao); anachronisms — firearms, eyeglasses, machines, airships, glass-and-steel sheen; later-dynasty markers — Ming blue-and-white porcelain, Qing qipao/magua and frontal five-claw dragon robes, bound feet, small decorative bracket sets with steeply upturned eaves; Song-style ink-wash minimalism as the dominant mode; heavy chiaroscuro, rim lighting, HDR photographic contrast; emaciated figures and sharp angular faces; defaulting every scene to a Buddhist cave wall with halos (that is the Dunhuang overlap, not the core).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Dunhuang Feng:** shares mineral palette and Tang-era caves, but is defined by the cave-mural surface — flying apsaras, halos, caisson ceilings, oxidized blackened flesh, plaster crust, continuous narrative wall fields. Tang court style is secular and object-proud: silk grounds, sancai glaze, chased metal. If it reads as cave wall first, it is Dunhuang.
- **Guochao:** the modern commercial revival — flat vector shapes, saturated pop colors, graphic type, sneaker-and-neon mashups. Tang keeps historical materiality, diffuse light and hand-drawn line; no design-system graphics.
- **Neo-Chinese Style:** minimalist modern reinterpretation — sparse ink-grey compositions, conceptual negative space, contemporary interiors. Tang is pattern-dense, warm, plump and opulent; emptiness in Tang is silk breathing room, not zen minimalism.
- **Silkpunk:** speculative silk-tech fantasy — kite gliders, bamboo machinery, engineered anachronisms. Tang cosmopolitanism is historical costume and trade goods only; the moment invented technology appears, the style has left Tang.

**Quick check:** warm silk or cream ground + plump figures in flowing line, or dripping green-amber-cream glaze, or gold rosettes on teal, plus symmetrical abundance and shadowless light → Tang Dynasty.
