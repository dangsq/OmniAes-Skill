# Riot Grrrl

Riot Grrrl is the visual language of an underground feminist punk movement that coalesced in 1991 around Olympia, Washington and Washington D.C. It treats paper as a site of agitation rather than decoration: photocopied zines, flyers, cassette J-cards and poster pages assembled from handwriting, typewriter blocks, cut-out magazine fragments and scissor lines, with every smudge, strip of tape and misregistered copy left in place or exaggerated. Its photographic strand is equally unpolished — flash-blown snapshots of female-fronted bands in basement halls, and high-contrast xeroxed portraits reduced to black shapes on white. The movement's DIY ethos, anti-consumerism and third-wave feminist politics are not background context but the actual design brief: imperfection is authorship, and the page is a battlefield where corporate and patriarchal imagery is sliced up and forced to carry hostile new messages.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The field is monochrome paper — photocopy black, xerox grey and ivory white — struck by one hot accent at a time, never a full spectrum.

- **Photocopy black:** #0a0a0a–#1c1c1c, the dominant ink mass.
- **Xerox degradation greys:** #4a4a4a–#8a8a8a, appearing as grain, halftone dots and muddy midtones where the copier ate the detail.
- **Paper white / ivory:** #f2efe6–#fbf9f4, the substrate; warm office-paper ivory rather than clinical white.
- **Hot pink / magenta:** #e5006d–#ff2d95, used as flat cut-paper tiles, duotone washes over black-and-white photos, marker strokes and lipstick.
- **Protest red:** #c1121f–#e03131, marker and ink for slogans, underlining, defacement.
- **Peripheral earth accent:** a muted ochre/gold (#a8874f–#c2a36b) appears only in hand-drawn independent-label logo work; treat it as a minor variant, not core.

Rule: black + ivory carries the structure; pink or red carries the shout. Two accents maximum, applied flat and opaque, never blended.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the written record documents them

1. **Photocopied zine and flyer collage.** Layered hand-written text, cut-out images, blocky typewritten passages, abrupt scissor lines, visible tape, smudges, misaligned copies. This is the style's core object.
2. **Agitational typography.** Cramped confessional paragraphs colliding with sudden huge block letters or all-caps commands; dense text-heavy spreads; abrupt shifts in scale and orientation; asymmetrical margins that refuse easy scanning.
3. **Détournement of mass media.** Magazine and advert imagery re-captioned or defaced: faces scratched over, mouths blacked out, headlines replaced with accusatory slogans.
4. **Reclaimed slurs and body-writing.** Words like "slut" or feminist slogans written on skin with marker or lipstick; handwritten slogans, band names and political messages on thrifted clothing; slogans such as "girl power" and "girls to the front" before mainstream co-option.
5. **Thrift-store DIY fashion.** Babydoll dresses paired against combat boots or ripped stockings; band t-shirts; cardigans; sneakers; garments customized by hand. The look blends perceived innocence with defiance and rejects polish.
6. **Smudged makeup and unkempt hair.** Messy smeared eyeliner, bright or smeared lipstick, deliberately unkempt hair, sometimes bleached or dyed in patches.
7. **Female-fronted live performance.** Standard rock instrumentation — guitar, bass, drum kit — shouted confrontational vocals at a gripped microphone, small halls and community spaces, crowds pressed to the stage.
8. **Cassette and label ephemera.** J-cards, liner notes, hand-drawn naive ink illustration and hand-lettered independent-label logos (the Kill Rock Stars / K Records orbit). Hand-drawn doodles — hearts, stars, Venus symbols, scrawled band names — ride along the edges of collage pages. This illustrative strand is real but thinner in the record than the xerox strand; weight it accordingly.

### Materials and textures

Photocopier grain and halftone dot screens; office-paper and newsprint fiber; staple and punch holes; translucent tape; crooked scissor-cut edges with white halos around cut-out figures; ballpoint and felt-marker stroke texture; typewriter type with uneven ink pressure; cut-paper ransom tiles, each letter on its own tilted rectangle; cassette shell and J-card cardstock; flash-flare glare and motion blur on film snapshots.

### Recurring subjects

Women and girls as authors and protagonists: zine makers at cut-and-paste desks, bands mid-shout, groups of friends piled into frame, single defaced media figures turned into critique, hand-drawn local landmarks on label art, manifestos and workshop flyers. Male figures, if present, are crowd or background, never the organizing subject.

### Composition and layout

Asymmetry as principle. Collage layers stack without hierarchy; elements rotate off-axis; scale jumps from tiny confessional type to wall-shouting block caps within one page; margins are ignored or invaded. Cut-out silhouettes sit isolated on blank paper grounds; ransom-letter strips run diagonally across images; text blocks collide and overlap instead of aligning. In the snapshot strand, composition is crowd-level and accidental: mic stands cutting the frame, drum kits shoved to the edge, heads cropped at the bottom.

### Lighting and photographic treatment

Two native lightings only. For paper objects: flat, even scanner or copier light, no shadows beyond the paper's own wrinkles, maximum contrast. For photographs: harsh direct on-camera flash in dark rooms, or dim club light with colored gels (red, blue) bleeding over walls; grainy film, slight blur, blown highlights. Photocopied portraits collapse into thresholded black-and-white shapes with no midtone modeling. Studio softboxes, golden hour and bokeh do not exist here.

## Mood & Values

Confrontational urgency fused with confessional intimacy and communal warmth. Anger is structural, not personal: pages name patriarchy, racism, homophobia, ageism, ableism, rape, abuse and body policing, while first-person testimony about domestic violence, eating disorders and homophobia builds solidarity between readers. Humor and defiance leak through — doodles, inside jokes, crooked hearts beside furious all-caps. Above all: authenticity over perfection, participation over consumption, anyone-can-start-a-band invitation. The mood is a room full of girls talking back, reproduced at thirty copies per month.

## Era & Origins

Early 1990s United States, primarily Olympia, Washington and Washington D.C., fading from peak intensity after the mid-1990s. The term grew out of Bratmobile conversations — "girl riot" — with the "grrrl" spelling contributing a growl to replace the passive "girl." Momentum crystallized at the International Pop Underground Convention's "Girl Night" in Olympia in August 1991 and the first Riot Grrrl Convention in Washington D.C. in 1992; a self-declared media blackout from 1992 onward pushed representation entirely into self-published zines, liner notes and small distribution networks. The style reacts against a scene dominated by grunge and male hardcore that left women little room beyond the audience, and inherits punk DIY zine traditions of the 1970s–80s plus earlier feminist politics. Related media include the documentary *The Punk Singer* (2013) and the book *Girls to the Front* (2010); associated bands include Bikini Kill, Bratmobile, Heavens to Betsy, Huggy Bear, Sleater-Kinney and Team Dresch.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Paper first: whatever the subject, the image should feel staplable — grainy, contrasty, materially analog.
2. Imperfection is authorship: keep the smudge, the tape, the crooked cut, the misregistration.
3. Text is image: in the graphic strand, lettering (hand, typewriter, cut-out) carries compositional weight equal to pictures; write slogans in first person or imperative.
4. Monochrome field, one hot accent: black and ivory structure, pink or red shout.
5. Cut, don't blend: hard scissor edges and flat opaque color; no soft shadows, no gradient merges.
6. Bodies as billboards: when figures appear, marker or lipstick writing on skin and customized thrift clothing state the politics.
7. Snapshot ethics: crowd-level angles, flash flare, blur, cluttered real rooms for any photographic subject.
8. Deface, don't decorate: appropriated imagery must read as attacked — scratched, blacked out, re-captioned.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across zine spreads, flyers, posters, cassette J-cards, sticker sheets, manifesto pages, naive hand-drawn label illustration, live show snapshots, group portraits, single defaced-media cutouts, or body-writing close-ups. Composition may range from full-bleed dense collage to one isolated cut-out figure on blank paper to a diagonal ransom strip to an accidental stage snapshot. Lighting may range from flat scanner light to flash snapshot to gel-lit club dark to thresholded xerox contrast. Color handling may range from pure black-and-white photocopy, to black-and-white with pink duotone, to black-and-white with red marker strikes, to full-color flash snapshot whose pink lives in clothing, to ink line work with a single muted earth accent. Mood may range from fury to tenderness to confessional quiet to gallows humor to communal joy.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A poster of a fictional band, a zine page about rent, a hand-drawn map of a neighborhood, or a flash photo of a friend's basement show all qualify if they obey the palette, material and text rules above.

**Avoid.**
- Glossy digital finish: vector gradients, soft drop shadows, airbrushed skin, lens bokeh, 3D render sheen.
- Clean corporate grids, Swiss minimalism, centered serif elegance, UI sans-serif type, emoji, meme layouts.
- Full-spectrum, neon-cyber or pastel cute-core palettes; more than one hot accent at a time.
- Sepia or warm-faded Instagram vintage filters — the native analog is xerox grain and flash film, not nostalgic fade.
- Fashion-editorial staging: studio softboxes, styled perfection, posed model gazes, male-gaze framing.
- Sexualized infantilization presented as costume alone, with no paper, text or protest layer (that is Kinderwhore).
- Stencil-only monochrome crust-punk iconography with no handwriting, no pink, no personal voice (that is anarcho-punk).
- Pristine logos, barcodes, commercial packaging, brand sheen.
- Wordless layouts in the zine/poster strand; textlessness is native only to the snapshot and naive-illustration strands.
- Slurs directed *at* women rather than reclaimed *by* women — the direction of the ink is the politics.

## Discrimination Cues

**Kinderwhore** shares wardrobe atoms — babydoll dresses, torn tights, smeared makeup — but operates as costume logic: dollish infantilization played for subversive erotic tension, usually photographed as persona or fashion, with no zines, no slogans, no collage, no collective politics. Tell: if the meaning lives entirely in the outfit and no ink touches paper or skin, it is Kinderwhore; Riot Grrrl always leaves a written shout somewhere.

**Grunge** shares the thrift store and the Pacific Northwest but not the message. Grunge visuals run flannel, faded denim, mud-brown and olive tones, slacker melancholy, predominantly male bodies, and no agitational text. Tell: warm earthy desaturation and silence; Riot Grrrl answers with ivory paper, hot pink and all-caps.

**Queercore** is a sibling zine culture with overlapping personnel, so judge by organizing desire: queercore centers queer sexuality and gender transgression — drag, leather, SM signifiers, mixed-gender and trans casts, explicit erotic content. Riot Grrrl centers girl and woman solidarity, feminist reclamation, Venus symbols and "girls to the front." Where both appear, the slogans on the page decide.

**Anarcho-punk** is the older DIY ancestor: austere black stencil, crust patches, anarchy sigils, anti-state iconography, collective political voice. It lacks the confessional first-person handwriting, the pink and red, the reclaimed feminine, the personal-narrative testimony. Tell: if the page sounds like a communiqué it is anarcho-punk; if it sounds like a diary entry shouted through a megaphone, it is Riot Grrrl.

**Incelcore** borrows edgy punk graphics for a modern internet misogynist reaction aesthetic. Tells: digital meme formats instead of photocopy materiality, male-grievance slogans, slurs aimed at women rather than reclaimed by them, absence of female authorship and community imagery. Same visual violence, opposite politics — the direction of the reclaimed word is the decisive cue.
