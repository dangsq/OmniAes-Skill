# Teslapunk

Teslapunk is a speculative-fiction microgenre and visual style closely related to steampunk but principally organized around **electricity** rather than steam. Named after Nikola Tesla, it draws on the electrical pioneers and devices of the 18th, 19th and early 20th centuries, and imagines an alternate history in which widely available, cheap or free, clean and highly portable electrical energy replaced wood, coal, oil and the steam engines that burned them — and was never itself superseded by diesel or nuclear power. A recurring narrative undercurrent holds that such free-energy technology was suppressed and kept secret by governments or organizations to control the masses, which gives the style a second register of fog-bound conspiracy beside its luminous utopianism. As a named aesthetic it crystallized in the 2000s; as a concept it lives in the Victorian and Edwardian age of coils, currents and wireless transmission.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette is a two-pole system: **cold discharge light** against **warm conductive metal**, optionally mediated by a third pole of incandescent amber or fog grey.

- **Electric blues and cyans** (the style's key colour "light blue"): arc cores near-white `#EFFFFC`, discharge cyan `#7FE3FF` to `#4FC3E8`, violet-blue halo `#3D2E7C`, deep indigo and violet-black for shadowed chambers and night skies `#241A4F`, `#150E33`.
- **Bronze, brass, copper** (the key colour "bronze"): polished `#C98A3D`, mid bronze `#B08D57`, dark oxidized copper `#7A4E24`; occasional verdigris patina as a minor note.
- **Warm incandescent amber** for bulbs, hall lighting and neon-tube dressing: `#FFC46B`, `#E8933A`.
- **Fog and slate greys** for misty exteriors: `#9AA3AD`, `#5A6068`.
- **Minority accents**, used sparingly: phosphor green instrument glow `#3FE08A`, warning-signal red `#E23B2E`.

Rule of thumb: an image reads as teslapunk when bronze-age metal is lit by cold electrical glow. Either pole may dominate — a near-black indigo chamber with one blazing core, or a pale daylight cityscape where copper coils and faint arc-cyan carry the signature — but the pairing should remain legible.

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Visible electricity as protagonist**: jagged lightning arcs, corona discharge, plasma globes, wireless transmission beams and rings of force. Electricity is never implied; it is shown.
2. **Tesla coils and coil apparatus**: copper windings, toroid and spherical terminals, stacked ceramic or glass insulators, layered coil drums.
3. **Victorian and Edwardian dress**: top hats, waistcoats, crinolines, long coats, cravats; goggles as the ubiquitous accessory, often tinted by arc-glow.
4. **Brass and exposed hardware**: screws, rivets, threaded rods, pipework — fasteners worn as ornament.
5. **Electromechanical devices and weapons**: tesla rifles and coil gauntlets, arc blades, electrified airships, coil-turreted tanks, trams and hover craft.
6. **Electric "magic" powers**: characters channeling arcs from hands, eyes or body, discharge crawling over skin and clothing.
7. **Monumental power architecture**: generator halls and "power cathedrals" — vast turbine-and-coil interiors, ringed control rooms, pylons arcing across nave-like spaces. (The source text lists "nuclear centrals" among its visuals; in practice this reads as monumental plant interiors rather than atomic-age iconography, and the evidence is thin — treat it as scale, not as radiation symbolism.)
8. **Steam engines as legacy**: present in the motif list but philosophically superseded; they appear as retrofitted, hybridized or abandoned machinery whose boilers now house electric cores. Steam is residual wisps, never the power source.

### Materials and textures

Polished and patinated brass, copper and bronze; cast iron and riveted steel plate; glass and ceramic insulators; oiled wood for workbenches and console housings; leather straps, belts and gloves; wool, tweed and tailored cloth; rubber-sheathed cables. Surfaces catch cold rim-light from arcs and warm speculars from bulbs. Fog, mist and haze act as light diffusers — atmosphere, not coal soot.

### Recurring subjects

Inventor and experimenter portraits (moustachioed figures cradling glowing orbs or coil rods); uniformed soldiers and wardens with coil weapons; adventuring women in goggles and corsetry wielding arc gauntlets; electrified beasts wreathed in discharge; airships and craft docked in colossal hangars; instrument-ringed control rooms; cityscapes strung with arc beams between towers; flat-lay displays of coil weaponry; poster and logo graphics pairing ornate lettering with a coil orb.

### Composition and lighting

- **Radial symmetry around a glowing core**: concentric rings, gantries or coil windings orbiting a central plasma sphere.
- **One-point perspective** down machine-lined halls, corridors and generator naves.
- **Scale contrast**: tiny silhouetted figures dwarfed by colossal apparatus.
- **Dielectric practical lighting**: the coil, orb or arc is the key light, often raking from below or the side, throwing cold rims onto brass and warm bounce onto wood.
- **Arcs as line-work**: lightning paths curve through the frame to bracket subjects and lead the eye.
- **Chiaroscuro and bloom**: deep indigo shadows, corona halos, fog scattering the glow; alternatively, bright cream-and-gold daylight interiors where device-glow supplies the cool counterpoint.
- **Display arrangements**: orderly flat-lays or racks for devices and weapons.

## Mood & Values

Teslapunk consciously advocates abundant, clean, portable electricity for all — its machines glow with optimism rather than belch soot. The mood ranges from utopian wonder and scientific spectacle (the inventor as hero, science as public marvel) to clandestine intrigue (suppressed free-energy secrets, fog-shrouded operatives) and battlefield tension (crackling weapons in grey mist). Underlying values: democratized power, craftsmanship in metal and wire, curiosity, and abundance without pollution.

## Era & Origins

Conceptually anchored in the 18th to early 20th centuries, above all the Victorian/Edwardian epoch of Tesla, the War of Currents and wireless transmission experiments. As a named microgenre it emerged in the 2000s from the steampunk family, reacting against steampunk's coal-and-steam premise by substituting electromagnetism as the prime mover, and sitting beside atompunk, decopunk and dieselpunk as a sibling "punk" retrofuturism.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Make electricity diegetic and visible: at least one discharge phenomenon (arc, corona, plasma globe, transmission beam) must exist in the scene as light source or subject.
2. Hold the two-pole palette: cold cyan/violet discharge plus warm bronze/brass/copper; amber incandescence and fog grey as mediators.
3. Build electromechanical, never digital: copper windings, toroids, glass insulators, analog dials, levers, exposed screws.
4. Dress and build from the period: waistcoats, crinolines, top hats, goggles; iron-and-glass halls, masonry arches, riveted gantries.
5. Light from within the frame: coils and orbs as key lights, fog bloom, strong chiaroscuro.
6. Honor the clean-energy creed: luminous purposeful machines; if the mood is conspiratorial, convey secrecy with shadow and mist, not grime.
7. Let lightning compose: use arc paths as framing curves and directional lines.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may span portraits, soldiers, creatures, vehicles, interiors, cityscapes, weapon displays, posters and abstract coil chambers. Composition may be radial-symmetric, corridor perspective, intimate portrait with practical glow, flat-lay, or sweeping vista. Lighting may run from a single core in near-black indigo to pale daylight with subtle arc accents, or warm amber halls punctured by cool device glow. Color handling may be muted painterly or saturated vivid, so long as the blue-plus-bronze pairing survives. Medium is most often painterly digital concept art with soft blending and glow bloom, but flat vector poster styles and graphic logo work are acceptable. Mood may be wonder, intrigue, combat tension or scholarly calm. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**

- Digital computation: microchips, screens with UI, holograms, LEDs, fiber optics, smartphones, AI iconography.
- Cyberpunk neon-sign density, rain-slick megacities, techwear (a single vintage tube sign as set dressing is fine; a neon grid is not).
- Atomic-age signage: mushroom clouds, radiation trefoils, chrome tailfins, 1950s pastel diners.
- Art Deco chrome streamlining, ziggurat ornament, gold-and-black lacquer glamour.
- Diesel soot, exhaust stacks, fuel grime, wartime utilitarian steel.
- Coal-smoke dominance and clockwork gears as the power source (gears may appear as hardware; coils must dominate).
- Generic fantasy magic: glow without jagged discharge paths, sparkles, runes.
- Clean white minimalist sci-fi interiors, plastics, carbon fiber.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Era anchor | Power signifier | Palette | Tell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steampunk | Victorian, coal | boilers, clockwork gears, steam plumes | sepia, soot, brass | Teslapunk swaps soot for glow: arcs and plasma replace steam; clean-energy creed |
| Atompunk | 1950s | reactors, radiation, rockets | chrome, pastels, orange | Teslapunk is a century earlier: copper coils, not chrome fins; no trefoils |
| Cyberpunk | near-future | computers, neon, networks | magenta/cyan neon on black | Teslapunk's cyan is arc discharge, not signage; no screens, no digital layer |
| Decopunk | 1920s–30s | streamlined chrome, deco glamour | gold, black, lacquer | Teslapunk keeps exposed coils and screws: inventor's workshop, not deco salon |
| Dieselpunk | 1930s–40s | internal combustion, steel, grime | olive, gunmetal, smoke | Teslapunk machines hum with coil turrets and arcs; no exhaust |
| Retrofuturism | any past-future | googie, chrome rockets, white plastic | white, red, chrome | Teslapunk is fixed to Victorian/Edwardian material culture and electromagnetism |

The fastest single test: if the light in the image comes from **electrical discharge crawling over brass-age hardware worn by people in Victorian dress**, it is teslapunk; change any one of those three terms and it drifts into a neighboring punk.
