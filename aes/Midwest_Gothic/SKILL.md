# Midwest Gothic

Midwest Gothic portrays the common facets of Midwestern American life — the farmstead, the county road, the rail siding, the small-town grid — in a macabre light. It emerged in the mid-2010s on Tumblr as one of the Regional Gothic family, flourishing as much in microfiction and literary work (the *Midwestern Gothic* journal, Scott Thomas, Donald Ray Pollock, Thomas Ligotti; kinship with *Something Wicked This Way Comes*, *American Gods*, *Scary Stories to Tell in the Dark*) as in images, and crossing into film and television (*Fargo*, *Children of the Corn*, *Nebraska*, *I'm Thinking of Ending Things*, *Stranger Things*). Visually it is an aesthetic of the settled but abandoned rural landscape: not wilderness, not city, but the worked land after the work has left. Its four keynote motifs, in the order the tradition states them, are alienation, emptiness, ruralness, and nature — nature understood as indifferent and mysterious rather than nurturing. Bad weather, depressing lives, local folklore, and exaggerated Midwestern traditions (the sale barn, the fairground, the church social, the harvest) complete the theme set. The aesthetic sits adjacent to cottagecore and liminal space photography, but where cottagecore warmly curates rural life, Midwest Gothic lets it stand cold, empty, and slightly wrong.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The color language is desaturation itself. Think of a histogram pulled toward gray: bone-white and ash-gray skies, taupe and mud-brown ground, dead-grass olive, silvered wood, charcoal asphalt and rail stock, with winter variants bleaching everything toward pale snow-white and night variants collapsing toward near-black punctured by small flash-lit or headlight-lit patches. Within this muted field, one restrained accent is permitted and characteristic: rust red, faded barn red, oxidized orange, or the dull brick of autumn leaves — a pickup truck's paint, a ghost sign's lettering, a row of tank cars' corrosion. As a working range: skies and snow from `#E6E4DE` through `#D8D5CE` to `#8E8C86`; ground, mud and gravel from `#6B6257` to `#4A443C`; dead grass and olive brush `#7A7B5A` to `#5C5F45`; weathered wood siding `#9A968C`; asphalt, tank cars and night shadow `#2E2C29` down to `#0D0C0B`; the single accent in the region of `#8A3B2E` to `#A0522D`, never bright. Saturation stays low everywhere; if a hue reads as "colorful," it is out of style. The palette may tilt cooler (snow, frost, blue-gray overcast) or muddier (thaw, late autumn, wet gravel) — both are inside the range.

### Light and weather

Weather is a subject, not a backdrop. The signature light is a flat, shadowless overcast: a thick uniform cloud deck that often occupies half to two-thirds of the frame and deletes all directional shadows, leaving textures to carry the image. Snow is explicitly documented as a recurring condition — extensive, flat, glare-less snow fields under white sky, the *Fargo* / *Nebraska* register. Night appears as true rural darkness: weak single sources (headlights on a gravel lot, a flash thrown at fur or brush, one lit window in a dark house), never ambient glow. Dusk and fogged winter haze are acceptable intermediates. What never appears: golden hour, warm raking sun, blue sky with sculpted cumulus, neon or sodium-vapor commercial night.

### Signature motifs and subjects (ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them)

1. **Emptiness and ruralness as subject:** open fields, gravel lots, county roads, rail sidings, the flat horizon of worked land; the frame is organized around absence.
2. **Indifferent, mysterious nature:** bare or storm-stripped trees standing alone, vast cloud decks, snow cover, corn and bean fields, windbreak tree lines at the horizon — nature as something that watches rather than comforts.
3. **Vernacular buildings in ordinary decay:** abandoned farmhouses with broken windows and leaning frames, sale barns and grain sheds with peeling painted signage, mobile homes, small churches, silos and grain bins, rusted farm implements left in the grass.
4. **Isolation markers:** a single house in a field, one parked or approaching vehicle with lights on, utility poles and wires crossing the sky, barbed-wire fence lines, fresh tire tracks or puddles as the only trace of people.
5. **Bad weather and depressing lives:** mud season, puddled gravel, thaw, gray thaw-light on shabby domestic infrastructure.
6. **Local folklore and the uncanny hint:** a wrongness at the edge — a light in an unoccupied building, a deer or undefined shape caught in flash at night, the sense that the tree line conceals something. The folklore is atmospheric; it is suggested, never depicted.
7. **Exaggerated Midwestern traditions:** the sale barn, the county fair, the harvest, the church basement — rendered drained and off-key rather than festive.
8. **Small-town and exurban fabric:** aerial or elevated views of the flat street grid with its modest houses and autumn yard trees; edge-of-town mobile home parks; the suburb as liminal threshold rather than as manicured set.

### Materials and textures

Weathered horizontal wood siding gone silver and gray; peeling lead paint; rusted corrugated metal and oxidized machinery; gravel, mud and puddle-scarred ground; dead or dormant grass; wet asphalt; brick walls carrying faded ghost-sign lettering; bare branch lacework against white sky; snow with a hard crust. Surfaces are matte, water-worn, and unloved. Nothing is polished, glazed, or newly built.

### Composition and framing

Deadpan and documentary. Common structures: a dominant sky pressing down on a low horizon; a lone structure or tree placed off-center in an otherwise empty field; roads, rail lines or fence lines receding to a flat vanishing point; frontal, eye-level portraits of buildings taken from the parking lot or roadside with no heroic angle; elevated or aerial views exposing the town grid's geometry; night grids of small flash-lit fragments. Negative space is load-bearing — the emptiness is the composition. Human figures are absent or tiny and distant; presence is implied by artifacts (lights on, a warm window, tracks in mud).

### Medium and finish

Vernacular snapshot realism: phone-camera or cheap-camera frankness, drone aerials, harsh on-camera flash at night, slight murk and low contrast, unretouched banality. The image should look found, not staged. Painterly rendering, glossy HDR, cinematic teal-orange grading, and fine-art print polish all break the register.

## Mood & Values

The feeling is dread without event: alienation, disconnection, and the macabre latent in the utterly ordinary. Midwest Gothic values honesty about decline — economic, demographic, meteorological — and finds its horror in indifference: the sky does not care, the field goes on, the house empties. It carries a melancholy anti-nostalgia, remembering small-town life precisely enough to know it was never warm. Folklore supplies a thin supernatural veneer (something in the corn, something in the woods behind the sale barn), but the deeper commitment is to isolation as a lived condition, and to nature as a mysterious neighbor rather than a resource or a refuge.

## Era & Origins

Coined and popularized in the mid-2010s on Tumblr, descending literarily from Southern Gothic and traveling with the Regional Gothic family (Northerncore, Anglo Gothic, Suburban Gothic). It reacts against two things at once: the wholesome heartland myth of American self-image, and the cozy rural fantasy of cottagecore, by insisting on the same terrain's cold, empty, strange reality. Its cinematic ancestors reach back through *The Last Picture Show*, *Children of the Corn*, *Fargo*, and *Gummo*; its literary shelf runs from Ray Bradbury and Toni Morrison to the Tumblr microfiction and the *Midwestern Gothic* journal. Its affinity with liminal space photography explains its love of transitional, unpeopled places.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Ordinary first, macabre second: the subject must be a believable piece of Midwestern rural or small-town life; the horror arrives through light, emptiness, and treatment, never through exotic content.
2. Let weather lead: overcast deck, snow field, fog, or true rural night. Delete directional sunlight and its shadows.
3. Empty the frame of people; imply them through leftovers — headlights, a lit window, tracks in mud, a recently closed door.
4. Desaturate to mud, bone, and ash; allow at most one muted rust or faded-red accent.
5. Keep the land flat: horizons are level, skies are big, distances are long and featureless.
6. Decay must be ordinary, not picturesque: a leaning farmhouse, a peeling sale barn, a rusted implement — never romantic ruins.
7. The uncanny is a whisper: a light where nobody lives, a shape at the tree line, a deer caught in flash. Never a visible creature or explicit horror.
8. Shoot it like a found snapshot: mundane vantage (parking lot, county road, drone pass), imperfect framing, low-contrast murk or harsh night flash.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across farmstead, barn, grain elevator, rail yard, mobile home park, church, cemetery, school, lake shore, corn or bean field, main street, or aerial town grid. Season may range across mud-season thaw, leaf-off late autumn, deep winter snow, and flat humid summer green-gray (the summer register is the thinnest-documented but legitimate). Time of day may range from white overcast noon to gray dusk to flash-lit night. Vantage may range from ground-level deadpan frontal to elevated aerial to inside-a-vehicle. Decay may range from merely shabby and occupied to fully abandoned. Uncanny charge may range from zero (pure emptiness) to a single subtle wrongness. Palette temperature may tilt snow-pale, mud-beige, ash-gray, or night-black. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a snowbound church parking lot at dusk or a summer bean field under a storm shelf can be perfect Midwest Gothic without resembling any documented example.

**Avoid.**
- Saturated color, golden hour, warm raking sunlight, blue sky with picturesque clouds.
- Picturesque or romantic ruins, ivied mansions, castles, cemeteries-as-ornament; any decay that reads as beautiful on purpose.
- Southern Gothic furniture: Spanish moss, antebellum columns, swamp humidity, magnolias, sepia heaviness, grotesque characters.
- Suburban Gothic manicure: pristine lawns, saturated twilight suburbia, glowing windows as menace-cliché, HOA neatness.
- Cryptidcore's explicit evidence: visible monsters, clear creature silhouettes, warning signs about creatures, flashlit forest close-ups as the whole image.
- After Hours' commercial night: neon, diners, sodium-lit plazas, colored signage glow, urban nightlife emptiness.
- Cottagecore warmth: flower gardens, cozy interiors, handmade charm, honeyed light, grazing animals as charm.
- Crowds, festivals, motion, traffic, any sense of activity or community in progress.
- Deep wilderness, dense old-growth forest, mountains, coasts, deserts: this is settled, worked, flat land.
- Glossy HDR, cinematic teal-orange grades, fine-art polish, text overlays or graphic-design treatment.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Against Cryptidcore:** both borrow night flash and folk dread, but Cryptidcore foregrounds the creature or its evidence (blurred bodies, track casts, signed warnings); Midwest Gothic keeps the uncanny ambient and spends most of its time in overcast daylight, snow, and ordinary infrastructure. If you can point at the monster, it is Cryptidcore.
- **Against Southern Gothic:** Southern Gothic is humid, lush, and grand — decaying antebellum architecture, moss, swamps, heavy shadow, grotesque human presence, often warm sepia tonality. Midwest Gothic is cold, flat, and modest: wood vernacular buildings, overcast or snow, shadowless light, and absence of people rather than grotesquerie of them.
- **Against Suburban Gothic:** Suburban Gothic haunts the manicured subdivision — identical houses, trimmed lawns, domestic uncanny at dusk. Midwest Gothic haunts the working rural edge: rail stock, grain structures, farmsteads, mobile homes, mud and gravel; its dread is economic and meteorological emptiness, not domestic secrecy. (Aerial small-town grids belong to Midwest Gothic when the fabric is shabby, autumnal, and gray rather than manicured and twilight-lit.)
- **Against After Hours:** After Hours is the commercial night — neon, lit signage, glowing diners and parking structures, urban liminality. Midwest Gothic night is rural and dark, lit by at most one weak source (headlights, flash, a single window), and its dominant registers are overcast day and snow, not night at all.
- **Against Anglo Gothic:** Anglo Gothic draws dread from antiquity — stone churches, churchyards, hedgerows, old-world fog, centuries of accumulated history. Midwest Gothic has no antiquity: its buildings are young wood and brick, its roads a surveyor's grid, its fog replaced by flat cloud and snow; the dread comes from newness emptied out, not from age pressing in.

Where the documented evidence is thin — human figures, interiors, high summer — the style's literary breadth ("depressing lives," folklore, tradition) licenses them, but they must carry the same flat light, muted palette, and empty deadpan to read as Midwest Gothic.
