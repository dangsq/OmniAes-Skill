# Burlesque

Burlesque began as a literary genre of mocking imitation — 17th-century verse that applied elevated manner to low subjects or irreverence to serious ones — and became a theatrical form in Victorian London, where operas, Shakespeare and grand manners were parodied in musical extravaganzas stocked with cross-cast performers in tights. In America it fused with variety and minstrel-show structure, absorbed striptease by the early 20th century, declined mid-century, and returned as neo-burlesque in the 2000s: inclusive of all genders and body types, borrowing from French cabaret, drag and performance art, and leaking into alternative youth culture through revival-era band visuals. Visually, the style is costume-first theatre: the body arranged as parody and tease, framed by the proscenium, the playbill or the dressing-room mirror.

## Visual Identity

**Palette.** Four anchors, per the style's own key colours: red, black, white, yellow — but handled theatrically, not flatly. Red carries the image: oxblood and crimson (#6E0F0F–#B31217) through scarlet and vermilion (#C41E3A–#D93A2B), appearing as velvet curtain, lamp shade, coat wool, lip and poster ground. Black is the ground and the shadow: jet and ink (#0B0B0B–#1A1A1A) in tails, vests, hats, netting. White is never optical white: ivory, bone and cream (#F3E9D2–#E8DCC0) in shirt fronts, glove satin, poster paper, powdered skin. Yellow arrives as metal and warmth rather than lemon: brass, champagne gold, mustard (#B8860B–#E6C78E) in buttons, sequins, instrument wood, marquee bulbs. Rouge pink and blush sit as permitted accents. Two sanctioned treatments span the variation space: a **saturated stage mode** (deep vignette shadow, red washes, gold highlights) and a **faded archival mode** (sepia or monochrome, sun-bleached olive, ochre and bark brown with red reduced to an accent). Note honestly: the yellow anchor shows up mostly as brass/gold and wood tones in surviving visual material, and the daylight-faded mode is a minority register — both are legitimate, neither is mandatory.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the text documents them.**
1. *Structured bodices*: corsets, bustiers, bullet bras, sweetheart and plunging necklines, off-shoulder or bare sleeves.
2. *Feathers*: the staple ostrich feather fan, feather headpieces, tulle and feather bustles, fur and feather boas.
3. *Tease geometry of legwear*: lace stockings, garters, boyshorts, fringe-trimmed miniskirts, mermaid skirts, V-shaped fringe built to move.
4. *Gloves and jewels*: opera-length gloves (fingerless, tasseled), pearl and diamond strands, chokers, festoon necklaces.
5. *Travesty regalia*: tights and cross-cast roles, tail coats, shirt fronts, cravats — the Victorian staple of women in tights and men in parody finery.
6. *Ringmaster and MC dress*: top hats, fitted military-cut coats, striped trousers, brass buttons (the circus overlap carried by revival-era troupe imagery).
7. *Harlequin diamond and checker patterning* on vests, clown coats and hat bands — parody made textile.
8. *Stage architecture*: proscenium arches, red velvet curtains, footlights, marquee and neon signage, bulb-lit dressing mirrors.
9. *Poster ephemera*: Art Nouveau lithograph playbills with lettering bands and dancing figures, torn poster fragments, pin-up lithographs.

**Materials and textures.** Satin sheen, velvet pile, sequin sparkle, tulle haze, lace openwork, fishnet grid, ostrich plume softness, patent leather, brass and pearl hardware; on the surface level, lithograph offset grain, halftone dots, aged paper foxing, and film grain. Nothing in this style is matte plastic or brushed metal.

**Recurring subjects.** Performers of any gender and body type mid-act or mid-gesture; chorus lines and troupe processions; the MC or ringmaster with cane or microphone; musicians in period dress (hollow-body guitars, upright bass, brass); backstage dressing rituals at candle or bulb light; marquees and curtains at the edge of the frame; poster girls and boys as emblem rather than portrait.

**Composition and lighting.** Frontal proscenium staging that acknowledges the audience; lateral ensemble lineups and processions crossing the frame; poster layouts with a single central figure and type bands above and below; grids and collages of ephemera fragments; intimate shallow-focus vignettes backstage. Gazes are direct and complicit — the wink is structural. Light is motivated by theatre: tungsten spotlight pools with vignette falloff, candle and red-shaded lamp glow, footlight uplight, saturated red washes; flat limited-ink colour in poster mode; soft overcast daylight only in the faded troupe register; sepia or monochrome in archival mode.

## Mood & Values

Parody, satire, comedy, sexuality, drama, exaggeration, flamboyance, extravaganza. The governing grammar is tease: conceal-and-reveal, the glove half-removed, the fan half-open, the stocking seam turned toward the light. Artificiality is celebrated, never hidden — cinched waists, towering headpieces, painted lips. Beneath the wink sits a sincere ethic of body celebration and queer inclusivity inherited from neo-burlesque, and a nostalgia for the shabby-glamorous working theatre.

## Era & Origins

Literary term from 16th–17th-century Italy and France (Berni's *Opere Burlesche*, Pope's mock-heroic, Butler's low burlesque); theatrical form in Victorian London 1830s–1890s (Vestris at the Olympic, the Gaiety and Strand houses, Planché and Gilbert); American burlesque from 1868's British Blondes through the three-act variety structure and early-20th-century striptease; mid-century decline; 2000s neo-burlesque revival via *Moulin Rouge!*, *Burlesque*, Dita Von Teese's manuals, and revival-band album visuals. The style always reacts against solemnity: it exists to mock the dignified by dressing it in tights and fringe.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Costume-first: every figure carries at least one period theatrical signifier — corset, fringe, feather, opera glove, top hat, tights, cravat, harlequin diamond.
2. Palette discipline: one dominant red over black ground with ivory and brass support, or the sanctioned faded archival variant.
3. Light as theatre: motivated spotlight, candle, lamp or footlight; shadow functions as curtain.
4. Tease grammar: partial reveal, props caught mid-gesture, seams and garters implied rather than displayed.
5. Aged surface: grain, halftone, paper tooth or satin sheen; never clinically clean render.
6. Exaggeration with a wink: pushed silhouettes and complicit expressions, parody visible in the staging.
7. Emblem or ensemble: a single poster figure or a troupe; rarely a lone uncostumed civilian.

**Variation axes.** Subject: stage act, backstage ritual, playbill poster, marquee exterior, outdoor troupe procession, ephemera collage, costumed musician. Composition: frontal proscenium, lateral procession, centred poster with type, grid collage, tight candlelit vignette. Lighting: spotlight pool, candle glow, red wash, flat lithograph ink, overcast daylight (faded mode only), sepia monochrome. Colour handling: saturated stage, faded archival, limited-ink poster. Mood: comic wink, sensual tease, extravaganza spectacle, faded nostalgic melancholy. Bodies and genders: any, per neo-burlesque's inclusivity. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a daylight woodland troupe procession and a candlelit dressing-room vignette are equally burlesque when costume, palette and tease grammar hold.

**Avoid.** Cool modern grades (teal-orange, blue neon, pastel minimalism); contemporary streetwear or athleisure as the primary read; chrome-pole modern club striptease with LED strip and club haze; gore, decay and Weimar dread (that is Dark Cabaret); clown-first identity — whiteface, red nose, oversized shoes, primary-colour clown suits as subject (harlequin diamond as costume accent is fine); anatomical cartoon distortion; glitter spandex, platform boots and stadium lighting (Glam Rock); voguing runway and house-ball documentary framing; clean vector flatness, modern sans-serif branding, CGI plastic sheen; broad unmotivated daylight inside the saturated stage register; and pin-up poses stripped of all theatrical signifiers and poster lettering, which drift into Pin-up proper.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Ballroom Culture**: contemporary Black and Latine LGBTQ+ house balls — voguing arms, runway categories, flash-lit halls, modern glam streetwear. Burlesque is period-costumed, stage-lit, vintage-surfaced, and its drama is striptease tease and parody, not category realness.
- **Caricaturism**: exaggeration lives in drawn distortion of anatomy and face. Burlesque exaggerates through costume, silhouette and gesture inside a realistically rendered scene; even its lithograph posters keep elegant figure drawing, not grotesque deformation.
- **Clowncore**: clown as identity — whiteface, red nose, garish primaries, contemporary ironic settings. Burlesque borrows circus coats and diamonds as costume vocabulary within a sensual revue; stage makeup is kohl and red lip, not clown whiteface as the rule.
- **Dark Cabaret**: shares corsets and stage, but trades comedy for dread — desaturated blacks, oxidized greens, decaying venues, melancholy. Burlesque keeps warm red and gold and a wink, even when nostalgic and faded.
- **Glam Rock**: 1970s arena aesthetics — metallic spandex, platform boots, glitter bolts, satin jumpsuits, guitar-hero stadium light. Burlesque's musicians keep hollow-body acoustics, vests, cravats and top hats inside a revue frame, and the style carries a lithograph poster lineage glam never had.
