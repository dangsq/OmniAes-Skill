# DIY Punk

DIY Punk is the graphic arm of the British punk explosion of the late 1970s: a design language invented by people with no design budget, using the only machine they could steal time on, the Xerox copier. Pioneered by Jamie Reid, Vivienne Westwood and Linder Sterling and propagated through early UK fanzines such as *Sniffin' Glue* and London's *Outrage*, it treats scissors, glue stick, lettraset-free hand lettering and photocopier degradation as a complete production pipeline. Everything about it declares that it was made yesterday, by the band itself, for ten pence. The style survived into the 1980s US hardcore scene's gig posters and remains the template for any self-produced music ephemera that wants to look assembled rather than designed.

## Visual Identity

**Palette.** The base is two inks: copier black (roughly `#0A0A0A` to a softened toner black `#222222`) on cheap paper white to aged cream (`#F7F4EC` down to `#E4DECC`, with foxing stains and gray copier mud around `#8A8A8A` in the halftones). The infobox keys the style to black and white, and strict monochrome is always correct. The surviving ephemera documents one licensed extension: a single flat spot color used as ground or second ink, drawn from a scavenged, non-harmonious range — union-flag red (`#B0192E`–`#D0021B`) and union blue (`#012169`–`#1B2A6B`), signal yellow (`#E8D000`–`#FFE800`), copier pink/magenta (`#D64560`–`#E85D75`), olive/military green (`#5F6B33`–`#7A8450`). Never more than one or two extras, never blended: spot colors sit flat, unmodulated, like a second toner cartridge or a printed flag behind the collage.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the style documents them.**
1. *Xerox degradation*: photographs reduced to thresholded black blobs or coarse halftone dot screens, midtones eaten, toner speckle and streaks across the sheet.
2. *Ransom-note lettering*: words spelled out from cut-out newspaper and magazine letters, mismatched typefaces and sizes per character; the same cut-out logic used as censorship bars pasted over the eyes and mouth of portraits.
3. *Slapdash amateur collage and mixed media*: cut photographs with visible scissor edges, torn paper, overlaps at careless angles, glue-shadow wrinkles.
4. *Fanzine apparatus*: stapled page spreads, hand-ruled boxes, typewriter paragraphs, mastheads, gig listings, hand-drawn starbursts announcing a price ("ONLY 10p!").
5. *Defaced official imagery*: appropriated flags, royal or institutional portraits, stamps and emblems overprinted, cut into and insulted by the collage.
6. *Gig-information layer*: band names in fat marker strokes, venue, date and price added by hand as if after printing, urgent adverbs ("TONIGHT!").
7. *Surreal photomontage substitution*: bodies spliced with machines and objects (a torso whose head is a domestic appliance), Dada-descendant cut-up humor.
8. *Stencil portraits*: heads, helmets, goggles and uniforms rendered as solid black silhouette shapes with blown-out highlights, the copier's version of a portrait.

**Materials and textures.** Wood-pulp copier paper and newsprint, visibly thin and aging; staple holes and rust ticks; tape ghosts; crease lines; marker ink that feathers at the stroke edge; typewriter ribbon fade; halftone dot rosettes and moiré; toner dust and dropout flecks; rough torn and scissored edges. The surface is always matte printed matter, never a screen and never a gallery print.

**Recurring subjects.** Gig flyers and posters; fanzine covers and inner spreads; record sleeves for small-label singles; photomontage portraits of singers and of anonymous authority figures; band logos scrawled rather than drawn; cheap photographs of rehearsals, clubs and streets.

**Composition and layout.** Asymmetry by neglect: elements pasted where there was room, tilted a few degrees, cropping off the sheet edge, overlapping without hierarchy. Two density poles are both correct — the maximalist stuffed zine spread boxed in hand-drawn rules, and the near-empty deadpan sheet holding one small cut-out figure and a couple of typeset lines with vast raw paper around it. Typeset grotesque type (a clean sans headline or lowercase band name) may collide with marker scrawl and typewriter in the same layout; the clash is the point. There is no grid, no consistent margin, no alignment, and misregistration between ink and paper is welcome.

**Lighting.** The artifact light is the copier bed: flat, shadowless, even, with paper stains reading as the only modeling. Photographs embedded inside the collage carry their own opposite light — harsh on-camera flash in dark clubs, blown highlights, murk swallowing the background, grain heavy enough to dissolve faces. No studio lighting, no gradients of light, no specular gloss anywhere.

## Mood & Values

Defiance performed as cheapness. The style's core value is that anyone can make it tonight with what is at hand, so amateurism is virtue and polish is betrayal. Urgency ("TONIGHT!"), sardonic humor and confrontational insult run through it: authority is literally cut down to size, its faces barred, its flags reused as wallpaper for a band name. Beneath the aggression is community — fanzines are conversation, gig lists are invitations, the ten-pence price is a promise that nobody is profiting. The mood ranges from furious to deadpan to absurdist, but never sincere-glossy.

## Era & Origins

Emergent 1976–1979 in the United Kingdom, out of the first British punk scene and its fanzine culture, with Jamie Reid's cut-up sleeve and poster work for the Sex Pistols, Vivienne Westwood's shop and clothing graphics, and Linder Sterling's photomontage as the canonical practices; the language spreads to US hardcore poster art in the early 1980s. It inherits directly from Dada photomontage and situationist cut-up, and reacts against both the bloated corporate rock industry and the professionalized graphic design that served it — every xerox blot is an argument against the record-company art department.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Output must read as *printed matter*, not as a photograph of a scene: a flyer, zine page, sleeve or poster on paper, scanned or photocopied.
- Two-ink discipline: black on paper white/cream; optionally one flat spot color as ground or second ink; optionally the flag tricolor as appropriated backdrop. No gradients, ever.
- Degrade every photographic element one mechanical step: threshold it to stencil black or screen it to coarse halftone dots; delete midtones; add toner speckle and dropout.
- Make lettering a collage: at least two hostile type systems in one layout (cut-out ransom letters, fat marker scrawl, typewriter, plain typeset grotesque), baselines irregular, sizes unmotivated.
- Show the assembly: scissor edges, torn paper, tilt, overlap, misregistration, staple or tape traces, crease and stain.
- Add information noise: prices, dates, venues, slogans, hand annotations added after printing, exclamation marks.
- Deface authority when a figure appears: bars over eyes and mouth, overprinted slogans, cut-out splicing of bodies with objects or machines.

**Variation axes.** Subject: gig poster, fanzine spread, record sleeve, photomontage portrait, stencil head, patch or handbill. Density: stuffed collage box-ruled spread ↔ sparse deadpan sheet with one cut-out. Color: strict black/white ↔ single saturated ground (yellow, pink, olive) ↔ flag tricolor appropriation. Photo treatment: stencil silhouette ↔ coarse halftone ↔ grainy flash snapshot. Lettering mix: marker ↔ typewriter ↔ ransom cuts ↔ typeset sans. Mood: furious ↔ sardonic ↔ absurdist. Era inflection: UK 1976–79 ↔ US hardcore early 80s. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a flyer for a fictional band, a zine page about anything at all, or a sleeve with entirely abstract cut shapes all qualify if the ink, paper, degradation and assembly rules hold.

**Avoid.** Clean single-family digital typography with kerning and aligned baselines; vector logos; gradients, drop shadows, lens flare, glossy highlights; smooth full-tone color photography or CMYK magazine finish; corporate grid layouts with consistent margins; pixelation, JPEG blocks, screen UI or webcam grain (that is digital lo-fi, not copier lo-fi); cartoon clip-art and paint-program wobble; concrete monoliths or architectural massing; refined minimalism with designed negative space; neon, metallic or cyberpunk palettes; painterly illustration; pristine paper and perfect registration. If it looks expensive, it is wrong.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Blogspot Scene:** also amateur and lo-fi, but digital-native — pixelated JPEGs, default web fonts, screenshot framing, screen glow. DIY Punk's degradation is mechanical and papery: halftone dots, toner flecks, scissor edges. Dots and paper fibers say DIY Punk; pixels and scrollbars say Blogspot.
- **Egg Punk:** crude by software, not by scissors — MS Paint scrawl, clip-art, garish flat digital color, absurdist cartoon figures. DIY Punk's crudeness is photomechanical monochrome on real paper with collaged photographic fragments.
- **Minimal Wave:** cold, precise, geometric; clean sans-serif type, controlled negative space, studio monochrome photography, synthesizer iconography. DIY Punk's empty paper is leftover, not composed silence, and its type is scavenged rather than specified.
- **Post-Punk:** shares the monochrome but adds art-school control — modernist grids, conceptual photography, muted duotones, an air of expense. DIY Punk refuses control: misregistration, torn edges, ransom lettering; post-punk can look gallery-made, DIY Punk must look ten-pence-made.
- **Brutalism:** monolithic raw concrete or raw structural layout, massive geometric form, unadorned but imposing. It lacks the scavenged ephemera entirely: no cut-paper collage, no halftone photos, no hand annotation. Brutalism is monumental; DIY Punk is hasty.
