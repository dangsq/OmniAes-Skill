# Bravú (Rock Bravú)

Bravú is the graphic and sartorial face of a Galician rock movement named in 1994, when bands meeting at the Castañazo Rock festival in the hamlet of Viana (Chantada) claimed the word for the gamey smell of wild, uncastrated meat — untamed, authentic, resistant. Musically it fused Galician folk (gaita, accordion, upright double bass) with punk energy; visually it fused the photocopy-and-scissors grammar of punk flyers with the symbols of the *aldea* and the fishing coast. The result is a "rurban" collage culture: village stone and washing machines, mussels and electric guitars, Galician slogans scrawled over halftone photos, all made to look cheap, loud and handmade on purpose. Its artifacts are CD covers, compilation sleeves, fanzine and magazine pages, festival logos, TV-program mascots and promotional band photos — almost always square or page-format, almost always lettered in Galician.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The core is a four-ink print palette on paper: black, flag red, high-contrast yellow, and green, plus off-white paper ground. Individual pieces rarely use more than two to four of these inks at once; yellow or red typically owns the background while black carries image and type, and green appears as accent (stars, side bands, hand-lettered arcs). Photographic inserts are allowed their own saturated, slightly wrong color casts — acid grass green, magenta dusk, teal sea, watercolor sky blue — but graphic overlays snap back to the core inks. Orange-red gradients and a chrome/silver gradient on display lettering exist as period 90s print effects, used sparingly.

### Typography: language as image

Galician text is the primary visual and ideological motif, not a caption. Band names and slogans arrive as hand-scrawled marker caps, rough stencil lettering, torn-paper letterforms, or arched hand-drawn scripts dominating the composition; body text, when present, is cheap magazine serif in narrow columns. Lettering should look drawn, cut, or sprayed — misregistered, uneven, emphatic. Slogans carry humor and provocation in Galician; the language itself is the statement.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how strongly the movement documents them)

1. **Galician-language lettering** as compositional backbone — slogans, titles, DIY patches.
2. **DIY collage grammar**: grainy black-and-white photography combined with bold hand-drawn illustration and stenciled text, torn edges, sticker and stamp overlays.
3. **Rural and farming life**: tractors, cows in fields, village stone steps and walls, farm equipment; the arrival of modernity in the village (washing machines, electric guitars) as ironic subject.
4. **Coastal symbols**: mussels (*mexillóns*, sometimes paired as a heart), crabs, worms and shellfish, boats and sea, lighthouse coasts.
5. **Folk-instrument-meets-punk**: gaita (bagpipe) or accordion played by mohawked, spiked-choker figures; the explicit tradition/modernity clash.
6. **Flags and stars**: Galician flag (white with blue diagonal band, sometimes with the red star), red-and-black banners, red stars as satirical-political punctuation.
7. **Xabarín Club boar mascot**: child-friendly cartoon boar with electric guitar, medallion-framed on flat grounds — the movement's pop-cultural crossover emblem.
8. **Selección galega football advocacy**: kit-clad footballer silhouettes, team-crest emblems, "selection now" urgency on compilation art.
9. **Folklore and satire figures**: references such as the mythological bandit Pepa a Loba appear mostly as titles and lyrric jokes rather than fixed iconography; humor and political satire color everything rather than forming a separate motif.

### Materials and textures

Photocopier halftone dots and crushed contrast; torn and cut paper edges; felt-marker and ink brush strokes; woodcut-like emblem linework; watercolor washes (documented mainly in festival logos and hand-drawn maps); rubber-stamp and sticker seals in corners; stencil spray; VHS-grade video still grain; flash-glossed snapshot photography. Everything reads as physically assembled and cheaply reproduced.

### Recurring subjects

Band members in workwear-plus-punk layers; elderly villagers holding electric instruments with deadpan gravity; livestock and sea creatures treated as protagonists; machines (tractor, washing machine, foosball table) as village icons; hand-drawn labeled maps of Galicia; footballers; flag-waving groups on coastal stone; cartoon mascots.

### Composition and lighting

Square CD-cover and page formats dominate. Recurring structures: tilted photo rectangles pinned over flat ink grounds; vertical side bands of stacked text; arched hand-scrawled names across the top; corner stickers and stamps; centered emblem or flag on a solid field; full-bleed saturated photo with lettering laid over; labeled hand-drawn map; mascot medallion. Photography favors overcast, diffuse Atlantic daylight outdoors (stone, grass, coast) or harsh direct on-camera flash indoors; collage and cartoon modes use flat, unlit graphic backgrounds. No studio softbox glamour, no atmospheric depth-of-field poetry.

### Fashion

Utilitarian base — denim, plain tees, sturdy workwear, work boots — layered with punk: leather jackets, local band T-shirts, DIY patches and slogans in Galician, mohawks, spikes, chokers. Presentation is unpretentious and untamed; the style explicitly rejects rockabilly slickness and exaggerated costume.

## Mood & Values

Defiant festivity with a gamey grin: rural pride, working-class authenticity, anti-globalization, and *morriña* (aching nostalgia) wrapped in sarcasm and political satire. Bravú celebrates the periphery against the center — the aldea against the metropolis, Galician against Castilian and English — while happily importing global rock energy. The mood is confrontational but affectionate, coarse but celebratory: an outsider status worn as a medal.

## Era & Origins

Galicia, Spain, mid-1990s; coined 1994 at Viana, Chantada. Pioneers include Os Diplomáticos de Monte-Alto and Os Rastreros, with a wide network of village-born bands. It reacts against the stigma on Galician-language rock, cultural homogenization, and urban centrism, inheriting from the Movida Viguesa indie scene, the late-80s Galician folk revival, The Clash, Mano Negra and Basque Radical Rock. Mainstream reach came via the children's TV program Xabarín Club (TVG, 1994–2009), the Bravú magazine and the Selección Xa! compilation (1997); anniversaries revived it through the Revenidas festival (2014) and the documentary BRAVÚ XXX (2024).

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. **Language first.** Galician text is a structural element: hand-scrawled, stenciled, or cut, dominating the layout.
2. **Cheap-print logic.** Imagine offset/xerox reproduction: halftone grain, misregistration, torn edges, sticker seals.
3. **One collision per frame.** Pair a rural/coastal Galician symbol with a punk/modern one (gaita + mohawk, tractor + electric guitar, mussel heart + red ink field).
4. **Deadpan humor.** Absurd juxtapositions played straight — a cow as cover star, an elder as rock guitarist.
5. **Four-ink discipline.** Build graphics from black/red/yellow/green on paper; let photo inserts carry saturated casts.
6. **Period frame.** Square CD sleeve, flyer, magazine page, festival logo, VHS still.
7. **Handmade mark.** Every line and letter should look drawn, cut, or sprayed by a person in a hurry.

Variation axes (a correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image):
- **Subject:** band portrait, villager, animal (cow, boar, crab, mussel, worm), machine (tractor, washing machine, foosball table), boat or coast, map of Galicia, footballer, flag ceremony, cartoon mascot, village street.
- **Composition:** cut-and-paste collage, flat emblem on solid ground, full-bleed photo with overlaid lettering, hand-drawn cartoon or map, mascot medallion, magazine page with columns and masthead.
- **Color handling:** strict four-ink print; black-and-white photo plus one accent ink; saturated photo cast with ink overlays; watercolor wash mode (festival-logo register); occasional chrome type accent.
- **Lighting:** overcast Atlantic daylight, direct on-camera flash, or flat unlit graphic ground.
- **Mood:** defiant, festive, sarcastic, tenderly nostalgic (morriña), child-friendly cartoon register for mascot work.

Avoid:
- Clean corporate vector design, modern sans-serif branding, minimalist grids, digital gradient gloss, smooth 3D renders.
- Studio glamour lighting, retouched skin, bokeh lifestyle photography.
- Castilian- or English-dominant typography; Galician must lead.
- Generic Anglo punk clichés without Galician content (Union Jacks, London safety-pin lore, anarchy-only iconography).
- Basque iconography (ikurriña, Basque-language slogans) or any other region's folklore mislabeled as Bravú.
- Postcard-picturesque folklore: pristine traditional costume displays, sentimental pastoral landscapes without irony or grit.
- Rockabilly/psychobilly slickness (slicked hair, neon diner polish) despite the shared double bass lineage.
- Heavy-metal blackletter, fantasy ornament, cyberpunk neon, pastel vaporwave.
- Corporate multicultural celebration; Bravú satire is coarse, insider, and affectionate, never branded.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Basque Radical Rock:** shared regional-language defiance and punk politics, but RRVS leans militant and uniform — ikurriña, clenched fists, harder metal-inflected logos, darker palette. Bravú swaps militancy for mussels, crabs, cows, football kits, cartoon boars, yellow grounds and watercolor play; its lettering is scrawled marker and stencil, not metal logotype.
- **Vs Folk Punk:** generic folk punk keeps acoustic warmth, pub-folk ornament, or clean modern digital illustration. Bravú insists on xerox grit, Galician-only text, rurban collision (tractor beside electric guitar), and the 90s CD-sleeve collage format.
- **Vs Indie / Movida Viguesa:** the Viguesa predecessor is cooler, slicker, synth-tinted and cosmopolitan with tidy type. Bravú is deliberately coarse, rural, loud-typed, misregistered — the village answering the city.
- **Vs Punk:** plain punk runs monochrome plus one neon, urban decay, safety pins and anarchy signs, nihilist sneer. Bravú keeps the cut-and-paste method but fills it with coastal fauna, farming machines, hand-drawn cartoons, flags of Galicia, and a festive, morriña-tinged defiance instead of nihilism.

Where evidence is thin, say so: watercolor and hand-drawn map modes are documented mainly in festival logos and promotional maps; chrome gradient lettering is an occasional period accent, not a rule; folklore figures like Pepa a Loba circulate as names and jokes rather than fixed depictions. Treat them as permitted registers, not required elements.
