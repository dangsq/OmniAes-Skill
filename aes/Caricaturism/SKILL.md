# Caricaturism

Caricaturism is the art of rebuilding a recognizable likeness out of its own most identifying traits, pushed past proportion until the distortion itself becomes the portrait. It is simultaneously a street-vendor trade (a paid likeness, affectionately amplified), a weapon of print satire (a public figure reduced to jawline and hairline), and a vernacular of internet memes (pre-drawn masks of feeling). The style tolerates both the humoristic and the grotesque: the same enlarged nose can be a joke, an insult, or a symptom of villainy. What never varies is the contract with the viewer — the subject must still be *recognizable*, and the exaggeration must be the point.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** Caricaturism owns no single palette; it owns a hierarchy — colour serves the face. Evidence clusters into three recurring families, and a correct image may draw from any of them. (1) *Saturated field*: sunburst yellow (#FFD100 → #F5A000) and signal red (#D40000 → #E83000) as flat or radial backdrops that shove a painted head forward, with costume pops of oxblood (#8E2B2B), royal purple (#7A4FBF) or jersey sky-blue (#6FA8DC). (2) *Print neutrals*: cream paper (#F2ECD8), graphite greys (#3A3A3A → #8C8C8C) and ink black (#111111), optionally lifted by a thin watercolor wash and a muted blue-grey ground (#A8BCCD). (3) *Grotesque flesh*: skin pushed off healthy tone toward ochre (#D8B840), ruddy pink (#E8A088) or sickly grey-green (#7A8A7A), often over flat sage or olive grounds (#6E8272 → #9DB89A). Crude meme raster sits outside all three: pure black line on a white void. Whatever the family, backgrounds stay subordinate — flat colour, radial burst, paper white, or one contextual prop.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the tradition documents them.**
1. *Exaggerated facial features* — the defining motif: monumental noses, jutting chins and jaws, heavy brows, protruding ears, gap teeth, bulging or pinprick eyes, receding hairlines compensated by frizz or tufts.
2. *Distortion of reality beyond the face* — animals, objects, buildings and landscapes inherit the same exaggeration logic; nothing in the frame is exempt.
3. *The satirical target* — politicians, celebrities and public figures; mockery as social and political commentary.
4. *Grotesque realistic texture* — pores, stubble dots, veins, bloodshot eyes and full shading applied to absurd anatomy, reserved for subjects meant to inspire disgust or read as villains.
5. *The portrait function* — a likeness rendered for a sitter or a crowd, the street-vendor lineage; recognition survives the distortion.
6. *Meme masks* — pre-made crude faces (rage faces, the troll grin, the bald everyman wojak and its variants) used as emotional shorthand.
7. *Toy and collectible embodiment* — the caricature logic frozen into bobbleheads and figures: head as half the object.
8. *Editorial apparatus* — comic panels, magazine covers, illustration blocks with logos or captions.
9. *The historical grotesque head* — the Renaissance and architectural lineage of swollen stone faces and gargoyles, the style's ancestral mask.

**Materials and textures.** Medium-agnostic by nature: graphite crosshatch on toothy cream paper; ink line with loose watercolor wash; airbrushed or painted gloss with specular sheen; flat digital cartoon colour with clean outlines; smooth clay-like or plastic 3D renders; crude aliased raster line from paint programs; molded vinyl and ceramic toy surfaces; weathered carved stone. Texture intensity is a dial: line modes stay matte and flat, grotesque modes load the skin with realistic grain precisely because the proportions are impossible.

**Recurring subjects.** Named or nameable people first — politicians, actors, musicians, sports figures — then street portraits of ordinary sitters, animals with human grimaces, anthropomorphized objects, and grotesque invented heads in the grotesque-film lineage (stop-motion puppets, uncanny rendered infants, masked effigies).

**Composition and lighting.** Head-dominant hierarchy is the structural constant: bust crops where the head fills a third to a half of the frame; full figures at head-to-body ratios of roughly 1:1 to 1:3 against nature's 1:7; ensemble rows of floating heads for political commentary; extreme close-ups when grotesque texture is the subject. Bodies simplify into costume shorthand — a suit, a jersey, a striped shirt — while the head carries all the detail. Lighting follows the joke: soft studio modeling with specular dots planted on nose tip, bald crown and cheekbones to sell the volume of the distortion; flat even light in line and meme modes; single-source chiaroscuro only in the grotesque and uncanny register.

## Mood & Values

Humoristic and satirical by declared value: laughter with a target. Caricaturism expresses democratic irreverence — power deflated to a sneer, vanity exposed in a hairline — alongside a parallel vein of affectionate mockery in the street-portrait mode, where the exaggeration is a compliment paid in attention. It accepts the grotesque and the absurd as legitimate comic tools, and in meme form it values communal deadpan: a crude shared mask that anyone can wear. Craft virtuosity is optional; the legibility of the joke and the likeness is not.

## Era & Origins

Originating in the 16th century and explored during the Renaissance by artists such as Leonardo da Vinci, caricaturism flourished in the 18th and 19th centuries — the infobox's 1800s crystallization — when James Gillray and Thomas Nast turned it into mass print satire. The 20th century carried it into magazine gloss, editorial pages, animated and stop-motion grotesquerie, and molded toys; the digital age pushed it into memes, rage comics of the late 2000s and early 2010s, and the bald everyman masks from 2009 onward. It reacts, always, to something: a portrait sitting, a scandal, a politician, a mundane frustration worth a shared grimace.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Anchor in likeness first: choose two or three identifying traits of the subject (hairline, nose mass, jaw, glasses, posture) and amplify them two-to-fivefold; keep the remaining features coherent so recognition survives.
2. Enforce head-dominance: shrink or simplify the body; let costume carry identity in a few strokes.
3. Let distortion leak: props, animals, furniture and skyline may inherit the same swollen or pinched logic.
4. Pick one mode and stay in it — print satire (ink line plus wash on cream paper), magazine gloss (airbrushed sheen on a saturated field), grotesque realism (realistic skin grain on impossible anatomy), flat cartoon grotesque (clean outlines on muted grounds), crude meme raster (black line, white void), or modeled embodiment (clay, vinyl toy, carved stone).
5. Keep the background subordinate: flat colour, radial burst, paper void, or a single prop.
6. Light the exaggeration: highlights and shadows exist to prove the volume of the distorted masses.
7. End on an expression — grin, scowl, sneer, leer, woe — never neutral prettiness.

**Variation axes.** Subject: politicians, celebrities, musicians, athletes, anonymous sitters, animals, objects, landscapes, invented grotesques. Composition: head-only bust, big-head small-body full figure, row of heads, extreme close-up, panel with caption. Lighting: flat even, soft studio modeling, grotesque chiaroscuro. Colour: any of the three palette families or meme monochrome. Mood: affectionate, savage, deadpan, uncanny. Medium: graphite, ink and wash, paint, flat digital, 3D render, sculpture, toy, crude raster. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — likeness anchor, amplified traits, head-dominance, comic or satirical intent — not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Symmetrical or beautified faces; generic chibi or mascot cuteness with no likeness anchor; undistorted photoreal portraiture; distortion so total that recognition collapses; grime, rust, glitch and decay overlays used *as* the distortion mechanism; faceless minimal figures in liminal flat geometries; sequins, feathers and stage-boudoir glamour; period-cool turtleneck-and-beret stylization standing in for anatomical exaggeration; handmade craft warmth (yarn, woodgrain, folk coziness) as the primary voice; horror grotesque with no joke inside it; detailed competing backgrounds with depth that pulls the eye off the head.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Burlesque** exaggerates through costume, stage spectacle and sensual performance glamour — sequins, feathers, curtain light — and celebrates the body; caricaturism exaggerates anatomy and face to mock or amuse, and treats the body as shorthand beneath a monumental head.
- **Beatnik** stylizes by period attitude: turtlenecks, berets, jazz-club monochrome, cool detachment. Its figures keep plausible proportions; caricaturism is loud, face-first, and anatomically dishonest by intent.
- **Grime Art** corrodes the *surface* of photographs — dirt, decay, desaturated murk, glitch — leaving proportions intact; caricaturism rebuilds *form*, redrawing anatomy itself, and keeps humor legible where grime keeps dread.
- **Hipness Purgatory** favors flat geometric bodies, muted retro palettes, faceless or minimally-faced figures and existential stillness; caricaturism is its inverse — maximal face, explicit joke, expressive excess.
- **Indiecraft** speaks in handmade warmth: yarn, wood, folk pattern, cozy sincerity. Caricaturism's textures are print, paint, plastic, stone or pixel, and its sincerity is always crossed with satire.
- Against plain cartoon or anime: caricature must point at a recognizable real-world subject or a plausible sitter; against pure horror: even at its most grotesque, caricaturism keeps a laugh, a jab, or a satirical edge inside the distortion.
