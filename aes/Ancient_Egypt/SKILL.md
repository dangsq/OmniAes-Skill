# Ancient Egypt

Ancient Egypt is the art of a three-millennium river civilization (3150 BC–30 BC, unification under the first pharaohs to Rome's conquest), and it is deliberately conservative: its forms persist because art served religion, funerary provision and *ma'at* — cosmic order, balance, permanence — rather than individual expression. The style therefore reads as a system, not a hand: a fixed figure canon, horizontal narrative registers, axial monumental symmetry, and a mineral palette applied in flat bounded fields. It is also a continuously received style (19th-century Egyptian Revival, 1920s Art Deco Egyptomania, film and game fantasy), so any corpus will mix authentic ancient objects with later homage; the core rules below describe the ancient canonical language, which the receptions borrow but never fully obey.

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule
Area is dominated by a warm mineral ground — sand, sandstone, ivory, linen white, brown — while saturated color appears only as *bounded accent fields*: inlay bands, broad collars, faience tiles, glyph fills, painted plaster registers. Black functions primarily as line and outline (kohl, contour, ebony veneer), secondarily as mass (basalt, black cats, resin). There are no atmospheric gradients: contrast comes from juxtaposed flat fields separated by a black or red contour.

Weathering is a legitimate state: the same palette may appear fresh and polychrome (as-made plaster, restored reconstruction), half-lost (pigment surviving in relief recesses), or fully bleached to monochrome sandstone.

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual emphasis)
First tier (infobox key motifs): pyramids; mummies; sand; gold; tomb artwork; the winged solar disc. Second tier (the wiki's visuals list, in its own order): hieroglyphs; the cobra uraeus; winged sun discs; gold jewellery and set jewels; thick kohl eyeliner; temple pylon gates; camels; palm trees; papyrus reeds; ushabti figures; blue scarabs; cats, especially black; the Nefertiti-style tall crown/headdress; the Cleopatra bob (shoulder-length straight black hair, heavy bangs). Structural grammar evidenced across monuments: battered (sloping) pylon walls with trapezoidal doorways, cavetto cornices, hypostyle forests of plant-bundle columns (papyrus, lotus, palm capitals), rock-cut seated colossi, register baselines, and cartouche ovalss enclosing names. Note honestly: the source list is flat and unranked, so tiers here follow infobox precedence; domestic daily-life scenes are documented only briefly (mastaba reliefs as afterlife provisions) and should stay a minor subject.

### Materials and textures
Grainy, erosion-pocked sandstone and limestone; polished quartzite and granite with waxy sheen; matte painted plaster with fine crackle; hammered gold leaf with specular bite; glossy faience glaze; deep matte lapis; ebony-and-ivory veneer banding with carnelian and faience inlay on wooden chests and boxes; crisp pleated linen; fibrous papyrus; dark patinated bronze and plastered wood for statuettes.

### Figures and subjects
The composite canon: head in profile with a frontal eye, frontal shoulders and torso, legs and feet in profile; feet planted on a ground line; gods and pharaohs larger than officials, officials larger than workers (hierarchical scale). Animal-headed deities (jackal Anubis, falcon Horus), pharaohs in nemes headcloth with false beard and kohl-lined eyes, mourning figures with raised arms, scribes, workers, sphinxes, cats, mummies in linen bands, ushabti figurines, and the weighing-of-the-heart vignette type.

### Composition and lighting
Two compositional logics coexist. Narrative surfaces work in stacked horizontal registers with even figure spacing and voids filled by vertical glyph columns — ordered horror vacui, flat picture plane, no illusionistic depth. Architecture and objects work in frontal axial symmetry: mirrored colossi flanking a doorway, repeated column bays receding into darkness, bilaterally symmetric boxes, urns and collars. Lighting climates: zenithal desert noon with short hard shadows, bleached stone and a saturated ultramarine sky; raking warm torchlight inside tombs and hypostyle halls revealing relief edges; diffuse even light for object still-lifes and ornament plates; optionally a warm painterly interior glow in the historicist reception mode.

## Mood & Values

Solemn order, eternity, functional magic: images are made to last forever and to work for the dead. Grandeur is expressed through mass, repetition and symmetry rather than movement. Beneath the ritual calm runs a second, modern register the culture itself documents — the mummy's-curse eeriness of horror film, treasure-adventure gold lust, and playful pop appropriation — usable as mood variants but always on the mineral palette.

## Era & Origins

Nile valley, Northern Africa, 3150 BC–30 BC. Old Kingdom: pyramid age and idealized royal sculpture; Middle Kingdom: somber, careworn faces and vibrant rock-cut tomb painting; New Kingdom: empire-scale temples, the brief Amarna relaxation into naturalistic intimacy, then Tutankhamun's classicizing treasures; Late and Ptolemaic periods: archaizing forms mixing with Hellenistic influence. Reception waves: Napoleon's campaign → 18th–19th-century Egyptian Revival; the 1922 opening of Tutankhamun's tomb → Art Deco Egyptomania, which resonated because both styles share simplified motifs, geometry, symmetry and repetition.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
- Line before shade: contour defines form; painted modes use flat fills, stone modes use sunk or raised relief, never painterly modeling.
- Keep the mineral palette rule: warm ground面积, black as line, gold/lapis/faience/red as bounded accents.
- Organize narrative horizontally (registers, ground lines, glyph bands) and architecture axially (symmetry, repetition, battered masses).
- Draw figures in the composite canon with hierarchical scale when depicting people in the ancient manner.
- Name the material in the prompt logic: sandstone, gold leaf, faience, ebony-ivory inlay, linen, painted plaster.
- Treat hieroglyphs as ordered ornamental text in bands and cartouches, never scattered decor.
- Light is climate: hard sun, tomb gloom, or museum diffuse — choose one and commit.

Variation axes:
- Subject: pyramid or pylon exterior; rock-cut facade with colossi; hypostyle interior; tomb-painting wall or papyrus vignette; royal or divine statue from colossus to statuette; jewellery, cosmetic boxes and inlaid chests; sarcophagus, mummy, canopic and ushabti assemblages; desert with palms, papyrus reeds, camels; cats; stylized illustrative or pop renditions.
- Composition: frontal axial monument; full-register wall; centered object still-life on plain ground; aerial desert; broken fresco fragment with irregular edges; grid ornament plate.
- Lighting: zenithal noon; raking torchlight; flat facsimile illumination; diffuse object light; warm historicist interior glow.
- Color handling: fresh polychrome, pigment-in-recess weathering, bleached monochrome stone, or ivory-ground plate with black and red line.
- Mood: ritual solemnity, golden triumph, curse eeriness, scholarly documentation, playful pop.
- Medium look: photograph, painted plaster facsimile, chromolithograph plate, academic oil, clean vector illustration.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A bare desert pyramid at dawn and a close-up of a faience scarab on linen are equally valid if the palette, line and material logic hold.

Avoid:
- Greco-Roman naturalism: contrapposto, deep carved drapery folds, single-point perspective in wall painting.
- Art Deco machine vocabulary: chrome, mirrored lacquer, zigzag setbacks, speed lines, sunbursts stripped of lotus/uraeus content.
- Egyptian Revival mounting: sphinxes and papyrus capitals glued onto European urns, candelabra, parquet salons and Beaux-Arts facades.
- Hellenic orders: fluted Doric/Ionic/Corinthian columns, pediments, meander bands, marble-white nude idealism.
- Latin or Arabic lettering posing as hieroglyphs; glyph scatter without band or column structure.
- Neon or pastel hues outside the mineral range; impressionist haze; cool grey concrete urbanism.
- Modern clutter (signage, plastics, wiring) unless the image is explicitly a pop-appropriation variant.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates it |
|---|---|
| Egyptian Revival | Egyptian motifs on European forms: urns, candelabra, cabinet furniture, Parisian facades; gilt-bronze and porcelain finish; sparse or invented glyphs; salon symmetry instead of temple axiality; no register narrative, no funerary function. |
| Art Deco | Shares geometry, symmetry and intense jewel tones, but adds machine-age materials (chrome, lacquer, mirror), sunburst and ziggurat step motifs, streamlined modern figures; lacks hieroglyph bands, composite-profile canon, sandstone mass and funerary content. |
| Hellenic | Naturalistic anatomy and three-quarter faces, draped fabric folds, fluted classical orders and pediments, marble-whiteness; Egyptian art keeps composite profile figures, plant-bundle columns, battered walls and flat polychrome fields. |

In short: if the geometry is Egyptian but the object logic is European furniture or facade, it is Revival; if the geometry is machine-modern with jewel tones on black, it is Deco; if bodies move naturalistically in draped marble, it is Hellenic. Ancient Egypt proper is the mineral palette, the black contour, the register and the axial stone mass, in service of eternity.
