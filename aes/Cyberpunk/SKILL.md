# Cyberpunk

Cyberpunk depicts the near future where advanced technology — artificial intelligence, cybernetic augmentation, pervasive networks, virtual reality — is fully integrated into everyday life yet coexists with social fragmentation, economic inequality and urban decay: the emblematic condition of "high tech, low life." Its protagonists are marginalized antiheroes, hackers, street criminals, renegade corporate workers, who turn the tools of control into tools of subversion. Stylistically it descends from hardboiled detective fiction and film noir, which is why its images read as detective stories told in light: dense neon-lit cityscapes, morally ambiguous figures, black markets, data smuggling, corporate espionage. The aesthetic was codified in the 1980s across North America and Japan by William Gibson's prose, Ridley Scott and Syd Mead's film design, and Katsuhiro Otomo's anime, and it has since absorbed a contemporary "neo-cyberpunk" register of gig workers, moderators and ubiquitous screens.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is a near-black base carrying emissive color: saturation lives in light sources and their reflections, never in sunlit surface color. Base blacks and charred neutrals sit around #050508–#121216; the neon accent family is electric cyan/blue (#00E5FF, #00A8FF), hot pink/magenta (#FF2E88, #FF00CC) and violet/purple (#7A00FF, #B26BFF); metallic neutrals of silver and chrome (#C0C8D0–#E8EEF2) carry helmets, prosthetics and wet hardware. Two documented secondary registers widen the range: a warm sodium-amber and smog-ochre counterpoint (#FF9A3C, #C97A2B) for dusk vistas and street markets, and an acid teal-green sky (#2E6E64 with pale #7FD4C1 highlights) in hand-drawn anime cityscapes. A frame may use one accent only (monochrome black techwear with a single cyan ring-light) or three at full saturation (night street); pastel, cream and daylight-white palettes are outside the style.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Neon-lit cityscapes** — the genre's first named motif: signage blades, vertical billboards, glowing windows stacked into depth.
2. **Cybernetics and prosthetics** — chrome or matte-black helmets, facial plates, ports and implants; the transhuman body as object.
3. **Rain-slicked streets** — wet asphalt and puddles doubling every neon source.
4. **Towering megastructures** — skyscraper canyons, elevated monorail beams and walkways cutting diagonally across the frame.
5. **Computer terminals and hackers** — CRT and flat-screen glow on faces, cluttered workstation interiors.
6. **Holograms** — giant translucent advertisements and projected faces looming over small figures.
7. **Japanese kanji and katakana signage** — multinational commercial sprawl rendered as vertical text (handle as texture of corporate globalism, not exotic decoration).
8. **Tactical gear** — vests, straps, buckles, harnesses, combat boots; the warcore/techwear layer.
9. **Wires and cables** — frayed copper and fiber bundles strung across alleys and bodies.
10. **Fashion archetype** (text-documented): trench coats, combat boots, shiny PVC or latex, and the minimalist monochrome-black draped silhouette of darkwear.

Lower-tier, image-corroborated signifiers consistent with the text's punk ethic and noir lineage: mohawks and DIY street styling, glowing visors and mirrored shades, motorcycle light-trails streaking through traffic, surveillance cameras and corporate logos, and editorial infographics annotating a figure's gear.

### Materials and textures

Chrome and brushed steel; matte black polymer and carbon-fiber shell; PVC, latex and coated nylon with a wet sheen; rain-slick asphalt, glass and polished stone; stained concrete and corroded metal underlayers; frayed cable insulation; CRT and screen glass; translucent holographic film; volumetric smog, steam and rain haze. Surfaces are either mirror-bright or grimy-matte, rarely soft or fibrous.

### Recurring subjects

Hackers and netrunners at terminals; masked, hooded or helmeted outsiders; augmented figures with visible prosthetics; couriers, technicians and precarious workers (the neo-cyberpunk register); street crowds and vendors dwarfed by advertising; bikers and vehicles trailing light; prosthetic helmets and masks as pure object studies; fashion models in techwear presented as lookbook plates.

### Composition and lighting

Verticality and layering are structural: foreground cables or railings, midground signage, background tower stacks receding into haze. Two canonical scales coexist — the sublime high vantage looking across a lit canyon, and the claustrophobic street-level alley where signage presses in. Figures are small against holograms and megastructures, or tightly cropped in portrait with neon as the only key light. Lighting is practical and emissive: neon tubes, screens and vehicle lamps motivate every highlight, with chiaroscuro inherited from noir, specular reflections on wet ground, and haze diffusing glow into volume. Documented variations include overcast desaturated daylight over grey megacity canyons, dusk amber smog, screen-glow interiors, and neutral studio or white-background presentation for fashion plates and prosthetic object studies.

## Mood & Values

The core feeling is the tension of "high tech, low life": awe at technological sublime paired with decay, crowding and precarity. Values expressed are anti-authoritarianism, skepticism of corporate power, transhumanist questioning of identity and embodiment, and a DIY punk commitment to small acts of sabotage and solidarity inside systems too vast to overthrow. Moral ambiguity, paranoia and melancholy pervade; the neo-cyberpunk tone is more disenchanted and claustrophobic, preoccupied with maintenance and endurance rather than rebellion. Even at its coolest — a detached lookbook plate or a clean helmet render — the style implies surveillance, commerce and control somewhere just outside frame.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in the 1980s in North America and Japan; the term was coined by Bruce Bethke's 1983 short story and codified by Gibson's *Neuromancer*, Sterling's *Mirrorshades*, Scott's *Blade Runner* with Syd Mead's design work, and Otomo's *Akira*, extending through *Ghost in the Shell*, *The Matrix* and games like *Cyberpunk 2077* and *Deus Ex*. It reacts against technocratic optimism and neoliberal capitalism, insisting each new infrastructure becomes a new apparatus of control; its noir roots supply the visual grammar of night, rain and moral grey. Neo-cyberpunk updates the register to platform capitalism, smartphones and algorithmic governance, extrapolating 20–30 years from now rather than a spectacular distant future.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Pair advancement with decay in the same frame: gleaming chrome beside frayed cable, luxury hologram above a street stall.
2. Let light be information: neon signage, screens and vehicle lamps are the key lights; reflections and haze complete the shot.
3. Build density vertically: layer structures, signage, windows and cables into depth; empty sky is wasted surface.
4. Keep the human marginal or modified: hooded, masked, strapped, augmented, dwarfed — never triumphant and clean.
5. Discipline the palette: black base, one to three neon accents from the cyan/magenta/violet family, metallic neutrals, optional sodium-amber or acid-teal secondary register.
6. Honor the noir inheritance: shadow, moral ambiguity, wet ground, smoke.

**Variation axes.**

| Axis | Allowable range |
|---|---|
| Subject | city vista; street alley or market; terminal/bar/transit interior; portrait or fashion plate; prosthetic/helmet object study; editorial diagram or collage |
| Composition | high sublime vista; vertical street canyon; tight claustrophobic crop; flat neutral-background lookbook; centered object study; layered collage |
| Lighting | night neon practicals; dusk amber smog; overcast desaturated daylight; interior screen glow; neutral studio light |
| Color handling | full neon saturation on black; near-monochrome black with a single accent; desaturated teal-grey with sparse signage; flat anime-cel color with acid sky |
| Mood | technological sublime; paranoid noir; claustrophobic grind; cool lookbook detachment |
| Medium | photoreal concept art; hand-drawn anime cel; 3D render; fashion photography; infographic editorial |

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A daylight overcast street canyon in desaturated teal with kanji signage is as valid as a magenta night alley, and a grey-studio helmet render is as valid as a sprawling vista.

**Avoid.** Pastoral or natural settings without urban infrastructure; clean white utopian futurism and optimistic corporate sleekness with no decay; sunny suburbia and daylight leisure; brass, wood and clockwork (steampunk); beige chunky analog consoles and cream institutional interiors (cassette futurism); bare unadorned concrete monoliths with no signage, cables or light (brutalism); grown tissue, vats and organic lab flesh (biopunk); playful Memphis geometry, bright primary color blocking and smiley optimism (90s cool); purely non-representational wireframes and data fields with no diegetic world (abstract tech); handcrafted cottagecore textures, soft natural fabrics, warm nostalgic domesticity; absence of any technological artifact or commercial signage.

## Discrimination Cues

- **90s Cool** uses bright primaries and teal-purple as flat graphic color on light grounds, with playful geometry and optimistic energy; cyberpunk's neon is emitted light inside darkness, always partnered with grime, rain or decay.
- **Abstract Tech** abandons the diegetic world for wireframes, grids and particle fields; cyberpunk keeps streets, bodies, weather and readable signage even when it depicts cyberspace.
- **Biopunk** imagines augmentation as grown biology — vats, sinew, chitin, lab green; cyberpunk augmentation is machinic: bolted chrome, carbon shell, ports and cables.
- **Brutalism** presents raw concrete mass and modular repetition under flat overcast light with no commerce; cyberpunk concrete is always overwritten by signage, windows, cables and neon — commercial surface density is the tell.
- **Cassette Futurism** shows beige/cream institutional tech, chunky CRTs, analog switches and a bureaucratic, hopeful future; cyberpunk is black, chrome and digital gloss under predatory corporate light, its future commercial rather than civic.
