# Biopunk

Biopunk is a derivative genre of cyberpunk that swaps the computer chip for the cell: its stories and images concern hacking biology — rewriting DNA, culturing synthetic life, grafting human and non-human traits — under the shadow of corporations that monopolize genetic resources. Visually it keeps cyberpunk's dystopian futurism but replaces machined hardware with organic, unsettling biological architecture, so environments feel simultaneously advanced and alive. The recurring dramatic engine is a contrast: cold laboratory sterilization on one side, organic chaos on the other, often in the same frame. Beneath the gloss of corporate biotech runs a DIY current — biohackers, rogue scientists, and "grinders" who apply the hacker ethic to their own bodies — which gives the style a makeshift, underground register alongside its clinical one.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is a dark, organic base occupying most of the frame, punctured by small areas of intense bioluminescent neon. The base is never clean: it is shadowy gray, sickly green, bile yellow, and blood-and-rust brown, frequently sliding into raw flesh tones. The pierce is cultured, not commercial — the glow of specimens, tanks and holograms, not signage.

Acceptable handling variants: a near-monochrome sepia or grayscale mode (airbrushed biomechanical art, grainy analog photography); a warm flesh-and-tungsten mode (handmade diorama interiors); and a cold blue-pink clinical mode (macro glassware, culture grids). In every variant the neon or glow remains a puncture, not a wash.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Living architecture / biomechanical environments** — the text's core visual claim, anchored to H. R. Giger's biomechanical art: corridors, tunnels, vessels and machines whose ribs, hoses and membranes read as tracheae, gut and sinew; technology that appears grown, incubated, grafted.
2. **Laboratory apparatus and the sterile/organic contrast** — glass culture vats and cylindrical tanks, petri dishes and well plates, pipettes and trays, holographic DNA helices, clone chambers; cold sterilization juxtaposed with leaking organic chaos.
3. **Body horror** — disturbing mutations, failed experiments, invasive bio-implants; in the "fleshpunk" register documented by the text, technologies, architectures and beings made of living skinless flesh.
4. **Engineered organisms and synthetic life** — designer creatures, specimen nurseries and vessel complexes, caged or crated growths, organisms fused with human or machine traits.
5. **DIY / underground science** — makeshift rigs, household-scale equipment, grinder body-mod workshops, rogue labs outside the law.
6. **Corporate containment and hazard culture** — clinics operating like tattoo parlors, stenciled "KEEP OUT" signs, hazard stripes, sealed doors, branded monopolies over genetic resources.
7. **Fashion** (text-documented; only sparsely visible in imagery): black outfits with neon accent colors, lab or trench coats, futuristic glasses, eyewear or masks, cargo pants, belts, buckles and pockets, visible body modifications, human/animal-machine fusions — practical scientific darkwear rather than clubwear.
8. **Specimen documentation** (image-supported secondary motif): pinned anatomical diagrams, parchment notes, taxonomic charts and labels cluttering walls — science as archive and obsession.

### Materials and textures

Wet glisten and membrane translucency; ribbed hose like cartilage; bone, chitin and exoskeleton plating; surgical steel and glass (chrome only as instrument, never as body); rubber tubing and latex; corroded metal and fluid-stained concrete; cultured tissue in nutrient gel; paper documents yellowed by humidity. Surface finish varies by medium: airbrush-smooth illustration gradients, handmade diorama tactility, grainy analog or pixel grit, murky low-visibility render.

### Recurring subjects

Scientists and technicians in protective suits or lab coats, often small,背 turned or silhouetted; modified and mutating bodies; cloned or cultured specimens in rows; biomechanical vehicles and ships whose hulls are tissue; sewers and underground facilities contaminated by escaped biology; macro fields of cells and cultures; the environment itself as protagonist, with humans absent or dwarfed.

### Composition and lighting

Claustrophobic one-point corridors of flesh or pipe; cluttered tabletop tableaux of specimens, crates and cages; symmetrical altar-like monuments (the ritual symmetry of biomechanical art); grid-like macro arrays of wells and dishes; cavernous organic interiors with tiny figures for scale; side-on stage framing inherited from 90s game graphics. Lighting is low-key and motivated from within the world: a single cold bioluminescent source (tank, hologram, lightbox) piercing inky darkness; sickly fluorescent top-light with a green cast; warm tungsten in handmade DIY spaces; murk, haze and underwater scatter; rim light outlining wet surfaces. Note honestly: the text also licenses sprawling neon-lit cityscapes with biotech clinics, but the surviving imagery skews to interiors, specimens and bodies — exteriors are permitted, not required.

## Mood & Values

Dread paired with fascination: the body treated as editable code, at once commodity and violation. The style values suspicion of corporate ownership of life, sympathy for the underground ethic of grinders and open-source transhumanism, and unresolved questions of ethics and identity — what remains human when biology can be rewritten. Beneath the horror is melancholy: failed experiments, abandoned nurseries, specimens outliving their makers. Curiosity is taboo but irresistible; the viewer is always slightly too close to the wet thing.

## Era & Origins

Biopunk names itself as an offshoot of cyberpunk, inheriting that 1980s dystopian futurism while redirecting it from information technology to biotechnology. Its literary foundations arrive mid-1980s (Blood Music, Schismatrix, Lilith's Brood), its visual grammar earlier still from H. R. Giger's biomechanical airbrush work of the 1970s, cemented in popular culture by the Alien franchise and Akira (1988). The 1990s consolidate it through anime (Neon Genesis Evangelion, Elfen Lied) and games (Parasite Eve, Resident Evil, Half-Life, Metal Slug's grimy stages); the 2000s add real-world grinder culture and handmade diorama practices; the 2010s onward bring CRISPR-era plausibility and webfiction extensions (SCP flesh anomalies, Mystery Flesh Pit National Park, Vita Carnis). The style reacts against corporate monopolies on genetic resources and against tidy transhumanist optimism alike.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Wet over chrome: any technology should look fed, incubated, or grafted — vats, tissue, bone, hose — not machined.
- Two temperatures: place cold sterilization and organic chaos in tension, even if only implied by a single clean tray amid ruin.
- Darkness first: build the frame in gray-green-brown shadow, then puncture it with emissive specimen light.
- Flesh is infrastructure: walls, doors, pipes and hulls may be tissue; scale it architecturally.
- Always imply process: labels, tools, containment, documentation — science happening or recently interrupted.
- Human scale as vulnerability: small suited figures, turned backs, or absence.
- Imperfection mandatory: leaks, stains, rust, grime, one failed specimen among the rows.

**Variation axes (what may change freely)**

- *Subject*: lab interior, flesh corridor, clone nursery, grinder workshop, corporate clinic, biomechanical ship or vehicle, petri-dish macro, mutated portrait, contaminated sewer, fashion figure in scientific darkwear, city exterior with biotech parlors.
- *Composition*: corridor one-point, cluttered tableau, symmetrical monument, macro grid, cavernous wide with tiny figure, side-on game stage.
- *Lighting*: single bioluminescent source, sickly fluorescent top-light, warm tungsten DIY glow, murky scatter, high-contrast rim.
- *Color handling*: full dark-plus-pierce palette; monochrome sepia/grayscale biomechanical mode; warm flesh mode; cold clinical blue-pink mode.
- *Medium*: airbrushed illustration, diorama or analog photography, 3D game render, pixel art, cinematic still.
- *Mood register*: clinical detachment, visceral horror, melancholy ruin, taboo wonder, DIY grit.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**

- Polished chrome cyberware, humanoid robots, circuit-board ornament as the focal technology.
- Neon advertising signage, rain-slicked billboard cityscapes as primary subject (neon here is bioluminescence, not commerce).
- Sunlit optimism, leafy vegetation, photosynthetic harmony.
- Glossy clean retro-futurism, cheerful pastel competence, pristine white minimalism with no organic intrusion or dread.
- Club/rave gear, neon hairfalls, dancefloor strobes as the scene's purpose.
- Radical 90s geometric graphics, flat saturated primary color blocks, xtreme ironic attitude.
- Brass gears, steam boilers, piston engines.
- Pure occult fantasy — runes, candles, magical mutation — with no scientific framing.
- Candy-gradient vaporwave washes; clean blue-sky exteriors; dry matte desert emptiness.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Cyberpunk**: cyberpunk's hardware is machined (chrome, carbon, screens) and its neon is signage; the city is the protagonist. Biopunk's hardware is grown (vats, tissue, bone), its neon is emitted by specimens and tanks, and its palette carries sickly greens, bile yellows, browns and flesh tones cyberpunk lacks. Quick test: does the technology look like it needs feeding, bleeding, or incubating?
- **Vs Cyberprep**: cyberprep is bright, glossy, optimistic 80s retro-future with clean whites and confident primaries. Biopunk may share futurism but never gloss without grime, never optimism without leakage.
- **Vs Solarpunk**: solarpunk's green is foliage in sunlight, community hope, glass-and-wood harmony. Biopunk's green is bile, algae murk and fluorescent stain; its organic matter is flesh and mutation, and its light is artificial and low.
- **Vs 90s Cool**: 90s Cool is attitude-first — saturated teals and magentas, geometric shapes, skate/MTV irony. Biopunk of the same decade (pixel games, anime cels) shares medium and era but not mood: bodily dread, desaturated organic bases, punctured glow instead of flat radical color.
- **Vs Cybergoth**: cybergoth is nightlife and fashion first — black clubwear, neon hair, goggles, industrial strobes on a dancefloor. Biopunk borrows darkwear and neon accents but the neon emanates from cultures and containment, the setting is lab, clinic or flesh corridor, and a scientific context is always present.
