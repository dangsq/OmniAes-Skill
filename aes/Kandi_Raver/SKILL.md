# Kandi Raver

Kandi Raver is not a clothing style first; it is a craft culture worn on the body. Participants ("Kandi Kids") make and trade handmade beaded jewelry — "kandi" — inside the American rave scene, and the jewelry itself, piled in dense stacks on the arms, is the style's spine. Everything else (neon garments, dyed hair, fishnets, leg warmers) is secondary scaffolding around the beads. The culture runs on PLUR — Peace, Love, Unity, Respect — and on a DIY ethos in which store-bought kandi reads as inauthentic: every strand is supposed to be strung by the hand that wears or gives it. The defining social act is the trade: a ritualized handshake (peace sign, joined half-hearts, clasped hands, bracelet passed between them, hug at the end) through which friendship is made visible. An image in this style should feel like evidence of that generosity — beads, hands, contact, joy.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette
The rule is full-spectrum rainbow at maximum chroma, never a curated gradient. Any hue is admissible if saturation sits near the top of the wheel and value is high; the working range runs from hot magenta (#FF0090–#FF4FD8) through electric orange (#FF6A00), acid yellow (#FFE600), lime (#A6FF00), kelly green (#00C853), cyan (#00E5FF) and electric blue (#2979FF) to violet (#7C4DFF). Colors are meant to clash cheerfully within one cuff; a single strand may cycle five hues in random order. Under blacklight the same plastics fluoresce — cyan, magenta and lime glowing against deep violet shadow (#1A0B2E–#3D1E6D) — which is the style's second canonical palette state. Candy pastels may appear as a minority accent (pastel pony beads exist), but a pastel-dominant frame drifts toward Kidcore; muted, earthy or neutral tones are outside the style entirely.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the culture documents them
1. **Pony-bead bracelets and strands** — barrel-shaped plastic beads on elastic or plastic lacing; the irreducible core.
2. **Elaborate multi-layered cuffs** — wide, woven, three-dimensional beadwork covering the forearm from wrist toward elbow; arms are armored in them, stacked strand over strand.
3. **Perler-bead pixel panels and letter beads** — fused-bead pixels rendering cartoon characters, hearts, stars and spelled words (names, "PLUR", crew tags); white cube beads with black letters spelling messages are the common street form.
4. **The trade ritual and PLUR gestures** — clasped hands passing a bracelet, fingers touching in peace signs, two hands joining half-hearts, the hug; kandi on the right arm reads as available to trade, the left arm as kept.
5. **Bead-covered garments and masks** — beaded bras/bikini tops, beaded skirts, face masks and headdresses; text-documented and present but rarer than arm wear.
6. **Secondary rave fashion** — dyed and split-dyed hair (pink, purple, blue, green), over-the-top glitter makeup, fishnet stockings, rainbow stockings, fuzzy leg warmers (often themselves beaded), 1990s phat pants or bell-bottoms in the period look.
7. **Adjacent rave props, image-attested** — plush toys clipped to bodies and bags, pacifier necklaces, cat ears, unicorn horns, fairy wings. These are companions, not core; beads must still dominate.

### Materials and textures
Glossy plastic above all: pony beads, letter cubes, perler beads, occasionally wood or metal accents (text-documented, visually rare). Texture contrast is part of the look — hard clacking plastic against matte fused-perler pixel panels, fuzzy plush and leg-warmers, open fishnet mesh, glitter on skin, plastic lacing knots visible at the wrist. Handmade irregularity is a feature: mismatched bead counts, slight warping in perler panels, uneven strand lengths.

### Subjects, composition, lighting
Subjects range from a macro of a single cuff to a crowd circle of traders; the constant is beadwork on living arms and hands. Recurring compositional habits: forearm-first framing (wrists raised to chest or thrust toward camera so the stacks are the portrait's subject); close crops of two people's hands mid-trade with a bracelet sliding between clasped fingers; full-body standing poses showing leg warmers over fishnets and a beaded skirt or top; group shots with hands piled together at the center. Candid party expressions — grins, tongues out, peace signs — beat poised modeling.

Three lighting regimes are canonical: (1) harsh on-camera flash in a dark club, black backgrounds, blown highlights on plastic beads, slight motion blur; (2) blacklight/UV wash, deep violet ambient with fluorescing beads and magenta-tinted skin; (3) hard daylight at outdoor festivals, asphalt or dust behind, saturated plastic catching sun. The medium is snapshot: point-and-shoot or phone candid, mild overexposure, imperfect cropping, shallow depth of field in cuff close-ups. Polished editorial finish contradicts the culture's lived-event honesty.

## Mood & Values

Generosity made visible. Kandi Raver imagery radiates communal warmth, childlike playfulness deployed with total sincerity, and pride in handmade objects. PLUR is an ethic, not a slogan: peace as conflict dissolved, love as hugs and goodwill, unity as open invitation, respect as care for the other person. The trade ritual means every image of hands touching carries meaning — connection performed in plastic. DIY authenticity underwrites it all: imperfect stringing is truthfulness; glossy manufactured perfection would be a lie.

## Era & Origins

Early 1990s United States rave scene. PLUR crystallized around DJ Frankie Bones' New York Storm Raves (the term popularized after a 1993 Bronx rave; "Respect" added later), aggregating hippie peace-movement language and black/hip-hop culture's "respect." Kandi itself descends from friendship bracelets and grew from simple strands into cuffs, masks and beaded clothing across the 1990s and early 2000s. 2000s emo/scene forums and imageboards spread kandi beyond raves, which is why scene-styled hair and makeup often accompany it. Today it persists at EDM festivals. Note the evidence gap: surviving imagery skews contemporary festival looks; the 1990s phat-pants era is text-documented but visually thin.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Beads are the subject. Foreground beadwork on arms, hands, or bead-covered garments; neon clothing alone is generic rave, not Kandi Raver.
- Stack and layer: multiple strands per wrist, cuffs wide enough to read as sleeve-like armor.
- Keep the handmade fingerprint: irregular stringing, visible knots, spelled words, slightly warped perler pixels.
- Rainbow maximalism: at least five high-saturation hues in frame, clashing on purpose; chaos over gradient.
- Put people in contact: trading hands, hugs, peace signs, heart hands, grins at camera.
- Shoot it like a participant: flash falloff, UV glow, or festival sun; snapshot energy over studio control.

**Variation axes.** Subject: macro cuff detail, hands mid-trade, single portrait, full-body outfit, group trade circle, beaded mask/headdress, or a craft table strewn with pony-bead piles mid-make (DIY is core). Composition: centered snapshot, off-kilter candid, vertical portrait, overhead hand pile — all valid. Lighting: club flash, blacklight UV, daylight festival, or soft indoor light for craft scenes. Color handling: full rainbow in white light, fluorescent neons on violet under UV, or pastel-accented stacks so long as saturation dominates. Mood: euphoric dance-floor, tender trade, proud display, quiet craft concentration. Era costume: 1990s phat pants and bell-bottoms or contemporary festival bare midriffs, fishnets and leg warmers. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**
- Muted, earthy, neutral or monochrome palettes; beige, olive, mauve; tastefully ordered rainbow gradients.
- Fine jewelry: gold, silver, gemstones, pearls, sleek manufactured bracelets.
- Black-dominant outfits with a single neon accent and moody poses (Scene's signature).
- PVC, chrome, circuitry, LEDs, gas masks, techwear silhouettes (Cyberpop/cybergoth).
- Coordinated brand-cute layering, hair saturated with non-bead clips and stickers as the main event (Decora).
- Clean nostalgic-childhood flat lays, sticker graphics and playground props without worn beadwork (Kidcore).
- Hypergraphic Y2K web-collage glitter graphics (Scenecore).
- Polished editorial studio lighting, seamless backdrops, retouched skin; aloof, ironic or contemptuous expressions.
- Any frame where the beads are absent or incidental.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Their tell | Kandi Raver's counter-tell |
|---|---|---|
| Cyberpop | Chrome, PVC, LED circuitry, sleek synthetic futurism, monochrome plus neon accent | Handmade plastic beads, craft irregularity, rainbow chaos, zero tech fetish |
| Decora | Harajuku street layers of hair clips, stickers, brand-cute plush, coordinated color themes | Bead cuffs on forearms as the core object, trade gestures, club/festival context |
| Kidcore | Nostalgic childhood props, rainbow stripes, clean primary-color studio sets | Worn beadwork on adult rave bodies, snapshot party evidence, handmade texture |
| Scene | Black skinny base, band tees, teased coontails, studded belts, neon as accent only | Rainbow-dominant and bead-dominant; joyful contact instead of moody posing |
| Scenecore | Hypergraphic internet collage, Y2K web glitter, digital mashup aesthetics | Physical craft and in-person ritual, photographed as lived event |
| Rave (parent) | Neon clothing, glow sticks, PLUR flags without beadwork | Kandi beads foregrounded; the jewelry, not the clothing, defines the frame |

Overlap is real — scene kids wear kandi, ravers wear neon — so judge by dominance: if the beads on the arms carry the image and the mood is PLUR warmth, it is Kandi Raver.
