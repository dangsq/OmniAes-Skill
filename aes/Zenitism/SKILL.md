# Zenitism

Zenitism was the avant-garde of the young Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes: founded by Ljubomir Micić in 1921, argued for in manifestos, and broadcast through the international magazine *ZENIT* until the state banned it in 1926. Its philosophy — the "Barbarogenius," the claim that the "barbaric" vital spirit of the Balkans could rejuvenate a decadent, over-rational West — produces a visual language of deliberate collision: Constructivist geometry and Cubist fragmentation welded to folk-primitive distortion, machine-age signage welded to Orthodox-tinged frontality, utopian pacifism welded to militant layout. Everything about the look is that of a printed argument: journal covers, posters, relief prints and gouache designs that behave like pages, walls and proclamations rather than windows.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The text names **black, white and red** as the key colours, and the surviving work confirms the triad — with the crucial caveat that "white" is never optical white but the **aged cream of cheap paper stock**. Treat the palette as a rule, not a swatch list:

- **Paper ground:** warm ivory to oatmeal cream, `#EFE7CF` → `#E2D5B4`, often visibly fibrous and yellowed at the edges.
- **Ink black:** `#101010` → `#1C1C1C`; black also doubles as a *ground*, a void field (`#0A0A0A`–`#141414`) on which cream architecture and signage float.
- **Signal red:** `#B3271E` → `#D8402F`; used for overprinted mastheads, diagonal stamps, lettering, small discs, seal stamps.
- **Sparing accents (one or two per composition, never a full spectrum):** ochre/mustard `#C99A3C`–`#E4C05C`, celadon/teal green `#4F7F6F`–`#8FB3A2`, occasional muted cobalt `#3F63A0`, oxide maroon `#6E3526`–`#8A4432`.

Rule of thumb: the triad carries the structure; accents appear only as flat planes, painted letters, or small circles and discs. A composition in cream + black alone, or black void + cream + one red, is fully Zenitist; a rainbow gradient is not.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Dynamic typography.** The movement's first visual language. Masthead-scale sans-serif and slab lettering, **Cyrillic and Latin scripts coexisting on one surface**, type set on diagonals as structural beams, words repeated and overprinted, letters treated as architecture.
2. **Geometric abstraction.** Monumental glyphs — the ring or circle on a stem, heavy bars, triangles, lattice grids — anchoring asymmetric layouts; Cubist facet-planes broken into ochre and green shards with letters and numerals embedded.
3. **Machines, the modern city, advertising.** Kiosks, towers, signage masts and painted hoardings, brand-name lettering on banners, machinery and scaffolding; the city as a hero portrait.
4. **The Barbarogenius and the distorted figure.** Stylized, angular, deliberately "primitive" human figures — carriers, builders, mask-like heads — whose distortion injects unease into the geometric order.
5. **Linocut / woodcut line.** Strong relief-print cutting: jagged white gouges in black fields, carved portrait heads, blunt black-on-white contrast.
6. **Collage and photomontage fragment.** Pasted typographic slips, torn paper edges, small halftone photograph insets dropped into the grid.
7. **Airplanes and speed subjects.** Documented in the movement's motif list but thin in the surviving visual sample; treat as a licensed subject rather than a required prop.
8. **Balkan folklore and Orthodox Christian traces.** Asserted by the text as a blending element; visually it surfaces subtly — icon-like frontality, folk-carve cutting, ornamental stamps — and should stay a whisper, never a costume parade.

*Secondary observed device:* the **black-and-white checkerboard plane** as a tilted ground under isometric structures. It recurs in the painted work but is not text-documented; use it as an optional spatial device, not a definition.

### Materials, textures, medium

Letterpress and typographic print on coarse cream stock (ink bite, slight misregistration, halftone dot in photo insets); matte gouache or tempera on paper/board with flat, unblended colour planes; relief-print ink with visible cut texture; glued-paper collage edges; handwritten inscriptions, signatures and small red seal stamps in the margins. Surfaces are matte, dry and printed — never glossy, never airbrushed.

### Composition and lighting

Composition is **structured yet energetic**: asymmetric grids held by one monumental glyph or a diagonal type band; isometric/axonometric projection for architectural assemblages (no vanishing point); collage fields of overlapping fragments; vertical signage masts rising from a tilted plane. Lighting is **print light**: flat, even, shadowless. Black functions as void or as flat shape, never as modeled shadow. There is no chiaroscuro, no atmospheric perspective, no light source.

## Mood & Values

Militant optimism with a raw nerve. The layout shouts like a proclamation; the distorted figures and carved heads betray post-war anxiety. Values encoded in the look: anti-traditionalism, pacifism and humanism (the machine celebrated, the war machine refused), Balkan cultural self-assertion, the synthesis of "East" and "West," and a Dada-adjacent absurdity kept inside a programmatic frame. The mood is prophetic, not ironic: Zenit declares, Dada sneers.

## Era & Origins

1921–1926, Zagreb then Belgrade, in the aftermath of World War I. It reacts against Western European rationalism and decadence, against bourgeois salon tradition, and explicitly against Italian Futurism's glorification of war, while borrowing Futurism's dynamism, Cubism's fragmentation and Expressionism's distortion. Its natural habitat is the magazine page and the poster wall; its death was censorship, not exhaustion.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. *Typography is architecture.* Lettering — ideally bilingual Cyrillic/Latin — must carry structural weight: diagonals, masts, overprints, monumental scale.
2. *Geometry first, distortion as spice.* Build with circles, rings, bars, grids and facet-planes; then let one distorted figure or carved head introduce the primitive nerve.
3. *Print logic, not picture logic.* Flat matte planes, paper cream or void black grounds, no perspective rendering, no photographic light.
4. *Machine-age subject, humanist temper.* Cities, kiosks, signage, machines, airplanes — celebrated without militarism.
5. *Limited palette discipline.* Black–cream–red core; at most one or two flat accents (ochre, teal, cobalt, maroon).
6. *Folklore as trace.* An icon-like frontal stare, a folk-carve cut, a seal stamp — never literal ethnographic costume scenes.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across journal covers, posters, isometric architectural fantasies, relief-cut portraits, Cubo-Futurist fragmentations with embedded letters and numerals, diagrammatic figure compositions, machine or airplane subjects, and abstract glyph-and-bar layouts. Composition may range from a single monumental glyph on a cream field, to a dense collage of type slips, to an isometric assemblage on a black void, to a full-bleed black linocut. Colour handling may range from cream + black only, to cream + black + red overprint, to black void + cream volumes + two accents, to white paper + black relief ink + one red stamp. Mood may range from exuberant machine energy to quiet lyrical geometry to anxious distortion. Lighting stays flat print light throughout, varying only in ground value (cream field vs. black void). **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Photorealistic 3D rendering, gradients, gloss, lens effects, depth of field; one-point perspective with modeled shadows and cast light; full-spectrum or neon colour, electronic/cybernetic imagery; war glorification, uniforms, weapons-as-heroes; Art Nouveau ornament or bourgeois salon finish; smooth vector-perfect curves and corporate Swiss minimalism (too clean — Zenitism keeps paper grain, misregistration and raw cutting); readymade anti-art chaos and mockery; unstructured gestural anguish; analytical Cubist still-life with no typography, machine content or print logic.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs. Constructivism:** Constructivism is purist and engineering-clean — strict red/black/white on white, photomontage, industrial rigor, no folklore, no distorted anxious figures, no ochre/teal accents, no checkerboard play. Zenitism is the eclectic, myth-haunted cousin: same geometry, plus primitive distortion, collage disorder and Balkan symbolism.
- **Vs. Cubism:** Cubism fragments still lifes and figures in muted ochre-greys with no typography, no machines, no page layout. Zenitist fragmentation always coexists with dynamic type, print-media logic or machine-age subject matter.
- **Vs. Dadaism:** Dada collages with chance, satire and readymade anti-art; it mocks. Zenitism collages with a program: manifesto seriousness, geometric discipline, utopian humanist message beneath the absurdity.
- **Vs. Expressionism:** Expressionism distorts with swirling hand-drawn or carved emotion and saturated non-natural colour, with no grid and no type-as-image. Zenitism borrows the cut and the distortion but subordinates them to geometric structure and typographic composition.
- **Vs. Signalism:** Signalism is the later Yugoslav neo-avant-garde (1960s–70s) — cybernetics, electronic and oscilloscope imagery, concrete poetry, xerox and photocopy texture. Zenitism is strictly interwar: letterpress paper, gouache planes, isometric signage, 1920s scripts.
- **Vs. Futurism (context cue):** Futurism blur-lines speed and glorifies war; Zenitism renders dynamism through static geometry, isometric thrust and diagonal type, and refuses the war cult entirely.
