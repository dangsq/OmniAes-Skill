# Modernisme (Catalan Modernism)

Modernisme was the cultural and artistic movement of Catalonia — above all Barcelona — from the late 1880s to the early 1910s, the regional expression of international Art Nouveau charged with a distinct political and cultural identity. Born from the Renaixença revival of Catalan language and culture and from the industrial boom of the Eixample bourgeoisie, it pursued a *Gesamtkunstwerk*: architecture fused with sculpture, ceramics, glass, ironwork and graphic design into one total work. Gaudí, Domènech i Montaner and Puig i Cadafalch led its architecture; Rusiñol, Casas and Alexandre de Riquer its painting and poster art; Llimona its sculpture. Everything below derives from the movement's documented priorities — organic line, natural motif, polychrome craft surface — with the visual record confirming how those priorities look in stone, shard, glass, marble and ink.

## Visual Identity

### Colour language
Colour in Modernisme behaves as a **skin over an earthy mass**, never as uniform saturation. Structural bodies stay in natural tones — honey limestone, terracotta brick, basalt grey-brown, mortar cream — while colour arrives in *fragments*: ceramic shards, tile bands, glass panes, mosaic medallions. A second, flat colour world exists in posters and Symbolist graphics; a third, suppressed grey-earth world in the realist painting branch.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how centrally the movement documents them)
1. **Organic, curvilinear "whiplash" line as structure**: walls and ceilings that undulate, parabolic or catenary arches, elliptical and keyhole openings, balconies like bones or masks, facades read as sculpted mass rather than flat plane.
2. **Natural motifs — plants, animals, geology**: leaves, vines and flowers carved or ironworked; reptiles, dragons and birds as mosaic sculpture; cave- and rock-like masonry.
3. **Trencadís**: mosaic of broken ceramic fragments with visible grout, encrusting benches, spires, roofs, finials and animal forms.
4. **Stained glass**: leaded skylights and canopies in jewel tones; light itself treated as building material.
5. **Wrought ironwork**: stems, leaves and whiplash scrolls in gates, balconies, lamps and finials.
6. **Symbolism**: dreamlike nymphs and fairies, melancholy allegorical figures, stylized natural settings (painting, poster, sculptural groups).
7. **Material innovation and honesty**: exposed ceramic brick, Catalan tile vaults, cast iron and metal structures paired with craft surfaces.
8. **Catalan Gothic and medieval memory**: stepped gables, tracery-like openings, national-historical reference melted into organic form rather than copied.
9. **Poster and magazine graphics**: flat planes, ornamental borders, silhouetted figures, integrated hand-lettered type.
10. **Melancholy marble sculpture**: polished nude figures condensed against rough, picked stone blocks.

### Materials and textures
Sharp shard edges and grout lines of trencadís; glossy glazed tile against matte mortar; rough-hewn basalt and brick against polished white marble; hammered, twisted iron; carved stone florals; tile-lined vaults; warm wood, textile and ceramic in applied arts. The hand must be visible: irregular shards, asymmetrical curves, tool marks.

### Recurring subjects
Urban facades and corner buildings of the bourgeois Eixample; park pavilions, benches, fountains and mosaic animals; church spires and crypts; concert-hall and palace interiors flooded through stained glass; independent and architectural sculpture; posters and magazine covers; café, street and crowd scenes; grey suburban plein-air views. Furniture, lamps, jewelry and textiles are text-documented members of the same total-work programme, though visual evidence for them is thinner — apply the same organic-line and craft rules to them.

### Composition and lighting
Facades composed as continuous sculptural surfaces, often caught at curved corners or in full frontal mass; skylines punctured by encrusted spires and checkered pinnacles; interiors shot looking up into radiant glass canopies; close surface views where mosaic skin is the subject. Posters use a flat frontal plane with borders and lettering, no modeled shadow. Paintings of the realist branch crop figures off-center with high horizons and large empty foregrounds (Degas/Whistler lineage). Light modes: strong Mediterranean daylight raking textured stone; transmitted glow through stained glass; warm lamplight at dusk on facades; diffuse neutral light on marble; overcast silvery grey in plein-air work; even shadowless light in graphics.

## Mood & Values
Vitality and craft joy coexist with Symbolist melancholy — the inconsolable nude as much as the glittering mosaic salamander. Underlying values: Catalan national identity and cultural revival, artistic freedom against academicism, integration of all arts, pride in material innovation, and an urban bourgeois confidence tempered by a socially aware, often progressivist conscience. The mood is ornate but earnest: exuberant surface carrying serious cultural ambition.

## Era & Origins
Catalonia, Spain, c. 1888 (Barcelona Universal Exposition) to c. 1911, rooted in Barcelona's Eixample expansion and earlier provincial architecture schools. It reacts against Neoclassical academicism and eclectic imitation of history, and against the superficial decorativism of early industrial production; it absorbs Arts and Crafts craft ethics, Ruskinian medievalism and European Art Nouveau line, then redirects them toward a modern Catalan national art.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Let nature dictate structure, not applied decoration: curves grow from load and plan (arches, columns, roofs), ornament emerges from the same organic logic.
- Break symmetry deliberately: asymmetrical massing, irregular shard layouts, non-repeating curves.
- Layer crafts in one image: iron + glass + ceramic + carved stone + sculpture should feel co-authored.
- Keep colour fragmentary over earthy mass, or fully flat in graphic mode, or grey-earth in realist painting mode — pick one colour regime and commit.
- Show the hand: irregular grout, hammered metal, tool-marked stone; nothing machine-perfect.
- If figurative, choose a documented branch: Symbolist dream-allegory, realist social/urban scene, or melancholy sculptural nude.

**Variation axes.** Subject: facade, interior, park element, spire, sculpture, poster, magazine cover, café or street scene, plein-air suburb, crowd, lamp/furniture/jewel design. Composition: frontal mass, curved corner, upward interior view, surface close-up, flat graphic plane, off-center painterly crop. Lighting: Mediterranean sun, dusk lamplight, transmitted glass glow, diffuse museum light, overcast grey, shadowless graphic light. Colour: full ceramic polychromy, earth-dominant with polychrome accents, muted graphic olive/ochre/rose, grey-beige realist. Mood: festive vitality ↔ decadent Symbolist melancholy ↔ sober social realism. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**
- Rigid bilateral symmetry, classical orders, entablatures, coffered regularity.
- Machine-perfect repetition, seamless vector smoothness, invisible craft, CGI cleanliness.
- Airbrushed or neon uniform saturation; gradient glow instead of shard-and-pane colour.
- Art Deco zigzags, sunbursts and chrome; Bauhaus or International Style curtain walls; minimalism.
- Austere structural Gothic: pointed arches, rib vaults and flying buttresses in monochrome ashlar used as engineering.
- Noucentisme/Neoclassical calm: ordered symmetry, idealized nudes in pastoral Mediterranean balance, restrained terracotta-and-cream decorum.
- Victorian kitsch florals, shabby-chic pastels, generic "whiplash" line on smooth anonymous walls with no earthy mass or polychromy.
- Anachronisms: plastic, LED lighting, contemporary signage, digital interfaces.
- Mistaking the grey branch for French Impressionism: keep its earth-grey tonality, black silhouettes and Catalan urban/social subject, not broken-color garden leisure.

## Discrimination Cues
- **Vs. Art Nouveau (French/Belgian international):** shared whiplash line, but Modernisme adds heavier earthy mass — rough stone, exposed brick, cave-like masonry — and Mediterranean ceramic polychromy (trencadís) plus Catalan Gothic memory and explicit national-political charge; Parisian/Brussels counterparts favor linear iron elegance on smoother ashlar with paler pastels.
- **Vs. Symbolism:** Symbolism supplies Modernisme's dreamlike figure content and flat decorative backgrounds, but standalone Symbolism lacks the organic structural line, the craft-programme polychromy and the architectural ambition; Modernist Symbolist works keep stylized natural ornament and, in graphics, integrated lettering.
- **Vs. Arts and Crafts:** Arts and Crafts is rural-vernacular, joinery-honest, muted and medieval-simple; Modernisme is urban-bourgeois, sculptural, curvilinear, and happily mixes cast iron and metal structures with artisan surfaces at brighter saturation.
- **Vs. Gothic architecture:** Gothic is geometric vertical engineering — pointed arch, rib vault, buttress, monochrome stone; Modernisme borrows its memory but melts it into parabolic arches, undulating planes and polychrome ceramic skin.
- **Vs. Noucentisme:** the successor-rebel: Noucentisme returns to order, clarity, classical nude and balanced Mediterranean pastoral with restrained palette; Modernisme is excess, curve, asymmetry, polychromy and city energy.

**One-line recognition test:** if the building or object looks grown rather than built — bone, stem or cave logic — and its skin glitters with broken ceramic, glass and iron over earthy mass, under a fin-de-siècle Catalan mood, it is Modernisme.
