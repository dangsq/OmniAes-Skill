# Toyism

Toyism (Dutch: *Toyisme*) is an international art collective founded in 1992 in Emmen, Netherlands, by the artist Dejo, growing out of his 1990 graphic work "Escape of the Computer Spiders." Its members renounce their names: each adopts a pseudonym beginning with an unused letter of the alphabet (capping the group at 26), builds a puppet avatar, and appears in public behind a crafted mask, so that attention falls on the artwork rather than the artist. The collective's secret notarized manifesto, "Mother," treats every piece as a "child" of the manifesto and its artist "parents." Visually, Toyism is figurative storytelling executed with distinct unmixed colors, a persistent dotting technique, and a high level of craftsmanship, addressing contemporary subjects behind a joyful, toy-like surface that conceals a serious underlying message. The style spans paintings, sculptures, masks, and full architectural transformations — a painted gas sphere, a water tower, a hotel interior, building facades — all speaking one dialect: flat loud color, crisp contour, and dots everywhere.

## Visual Identity

### Color: unmixed, adjacent, equally loud
The defining color rule is that each hue appears as itself — flat, matte, straight-from-the-tube — placed beside another hue of equal intensity so that the shared edge vibrates. Colors are never blended into gradients, never shaded toward black or white, never muted into harmony. Across the body of work the recurring range runs: fuchsia/magenta (roughly #E5007E–#EC008C), signal red (#E32636–#ED1C24), orange (#F7941D–#FF8000), lemon and egg yellow (#FFD500–#FFF200), grass and lime green (#00A651–#8DC63F), cyan and sky blue (#00AEEF–#27D7E7), cobalt and ultramarine (#0072BC–#2E3192), violet and deep indigo (#662D91–#3F2B96, down to near-black #2E1A47), plus support tones of cream (#FFF6D5), white, and occasionally an earthy maroon-brown ground (#5C3A2E–#7B4026). A single work typically fields four to eight of these as discrete compartments. The ground is a full participant: it may be as loud as the figures (yellow, magenta, red) or dark (indigo, violet, brown) so bright creatures flare against it. These hex bands are a distilled working range, not a fixed palette; any set of saturated unmixed hues obeying the adjacency rule reads as Toyism.

### Dots: the movement's fingerprint
Dotting is the most consistent technical signature. Dots perform four jobs: **contour** (an even line of dots outlining a shape instead of, or alongside, a solid line), **fill** (stipple swarms that occupy background zones, often curling into eddies and spirals), **ornament** (concentric dot rings encircling eyes, joints, and medallions), and **trail** (dot comets and chains linking elements across the picture). Spacing is regular and size consistent within a zone — the dots advertise patience and steady hands, which is the movement's idea of craftsmanship.

### Line, surface, finish
Forms are closed shapes with sharp, clean contours; interiors are flat color or patterned dot/stripe fills. There is no visible brushwork, no impasto, no glaze: the surface reads as matte, opaque, meticulously laid paint (acrylic-like). Visual texture comes from ornament — dots, stripes, ribs, concentric rings — never from material relief.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the movement documents them
1. **Figurative storytelling** — narrative scenes, not abstractions: invented hybrid fauna (bird-fish-insect bodies with beaks, fins, antennae, tendrils), concentric-ring eyes, striped or ribbed limbs and tails, creatures interlocking like puzzle pieces into a legible tale.
2. **Dots** in the four functions above.
3. **Distinct unmixed colors** standing out against each other.
4. **Contemporary subjects with hidden serious undertones** — technology, labor, environment, love, conflict — presented cheerfully; the earliest period (1992–2000) fixated on computers, space shuttles, and video-game logic (Super Mario-era symbols representing the founders).
5. **Masks and avatars** — crafted full-face masks: painted shells with goggle eyes, eye-stalks on wires, striped cone snouts, rainbow-ring respirator muzzles, worn with hoods and coveralls; the masked figure is the movement's public self-portrait.
6. **Architectural transformation** — the style wrapped around whole objects: spheres, cylindrical towers, hotel rooms including ceilings, street-facing facade bands.
7. **Game and duet structures** — checkerboard or grid compositions born of two-artist collaborative "games."

### Recurring subjects
Hybrid creatures and birds (lovebirds, dolphins, songbirds), masked humans, machines (computers, shuttles, motorbikes, chess-playing apparatus), anthropomorphized domestic moments (drinking coffee, making music, digging for gold), and buildings themselves as canvases. Everyday narrative moments are recast as creature folklore.

### Composition
All-over density: the frame is filled edge to edge, and negative space is decorated with stipple eddies rather than left empty. Grounds are compartmentalized — a horizontal split (e.g., indigo over yellow), diagonal bands, a checkerboard, or one saturated field — and creatures overlap and interlock within these compartments without perspective, horizon, or vanishing point. Balance is achieved by color weight and counterweight across the frame, not by symmetry or a single focal hero. Early-period works may instead stage sparse surreal machines in a pale, grid-lined void; this is a legitimate period variant.

### Light, medium, texture
Painted works use non-directional, even light: hue and contour carry form, never shading or cast shadow. The same flat logic wraps three-dimensional objects and interiors. Documentary contexts (masks, murals, painted rooms) show even daylight or diffuse indoor light, deadpan and frontal. Finish is matte; gloss, glow, and specular highlights are foreign to the style.

## Mood & Values

Toyism feels like a festival invented by children and administered by adults: exuberant, mischievous, tender, occasionally melancholic beneath the candy. Its values are collective over ego (anonymity, pseudonyms, masks, collaborative "game" pieces), craftsmanship as respect for the viewer, public space as a guerrilla stage (anti-establishment, in kinship with graffiti and Keith Haring), and storytelling that engages children and adults simultaneously — joy as the wrapper for a serious message. The secret manifesto "Mother" gives the work its family logic: rules first, invention inside the rules.

## Era & Origins

Emergent 1990–1992 in Emmen, Netherlands; Dejo's "Escape of the Computer Spiders" set the grammar, and the notarized manifesto "Mother" (5 September 1992) set the philosophy, as a critique of postmodern individualism and artistic egocentricity. Two periods: 1992–2000, simple surrealistic figurative paintings with recurring computer and shuttle symbols; from 2002 (after the 2001 manifesto rewrite), the symbols were dropped while the figurative base, sharp lines, and vibrant colors remained, and the collective internationalized — murals and objects in Iceland, China, Hong Kong, and across the Netherlands. Art-historical neighbors named by critics: Pop Art, Keith Haring, graffiti, Surrealism, the avant-garde.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Lay color as flat unmixed fields; let contrast between adjacent hues do the work of modeling. No gradients, no shading toward black or white.
- Close every shape with a crisp contour — solid line, dot line, or both — and keep edges razor-clean.
- Dot relentlessly and regularly: contours, stipple fills, concentric rings, connecting trails. Even spacing is the craftsmanship signal.
- Tell a story: populate the frame with invented hybrid creatures (eyes, beaks, fins, striped limbs) or masked humans enacting a contemporary narrative.
- Compartmentalize the ground (split field, bands, checkerboard, single saturated field) and fill every remaining gap with pattern; no bare negative space.
- Hide one serious idea under the joyfulness — environmental, social, technological — so the picture rewards a second look.
- Keep illumination even and shadowless in painted contexts; matte finish throughout.
- When depicting the artists themselves, use crafted masks and avatars, never recognizable faces.

**Variation axes (what may change)**
- *Subject*: any contemporary topic — nature, labor, love, chess, music, migration, technology, environment — cast as creature folklore, masked tableau, or early-period machine surrealism.
- *Composition*: all-over interlock, split field, diagonal bands, checkerboard duet, central emblem with radiating forms, or a whole-object wrap (sphere, tower, room, facade).
- *Color handling*: bright ground or dark ground; four to eight hues per work; the pale grid-lined early-period variant and the earthy brown-ground variant are both inside the style.
- *Lighting*: flat and even for paintings; diffuse daylight or even indoor light for photographic contexts (murals, masks, rooms).
- *Mood*: from pure carnival to bittersweet; the serious undertone may be barely visible or quite close to the surface.
- *Scale and medium*: canvas, mural, sculpture, mask, painted interior.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — unmixed flats, dotwork, figurative narrative, crisp craft — not any reference image's composition, subject or framing.

**Avoid (style-breakers)**
- Gradients, airbrushing, soft blur, glow, neon bloom, glossy 3D-render or chrome finishes.
- Muddy or blended hues, desaturated or monochrome palettes, pastel-only schemes.
- Chiaroscuro, cast shadows as form-givers, photorealistic modeling, sfumato.
- Gestural brushwork, impasto, drips, visible painterly texture.
- Brand logos, celebrity portraits, comic-strip speech balloons, Ben-Day halftone shading.
- Pure abstraction with no figurative narrative; Memphis-style terrazzo, laminate squiggles or geometric confetti used as the whole content.
- Anime/manga face grammar or minimal cute-mascot layouts with large empty grounds.
- Ironic media-reproduction detachment; empty undecorated negative space.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Overlap | The Toyism tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Pop Art** | Flat bold color, dots, pop-culture kinship | Pop's dots are mechanical Ben-Day halftones shading reproduced advertising, comic and celebrity imagery; Toyism's dots are hand-placed ornamental stipple outlining and filling *invented* narrative creatures. Pop cites mass media with ironic detachment; Toyism cites contemporary life through original folklore, masked anonymity, and a hidden serious message. |
| **Memphis Design** | Clashing saturated color, patterned surfaces | Memphis is a product-and-furniture language: laminate squiggles, terrazzo speckle, pastel-plus-neon geometric confetti, asymmetrical objects — with no figurative storytelling, no dot contours, no creatures. Toyism is always narrative figuration; its patterns serve bodies and stories, never standalone geometric decor. |
| **Neo-Pop** | Playful figuration, pop lineage | Neo-Pop embraces brands, anime, celebrity and industrial fabrication with glossy finishes; Toyism rejects ego and branding (masks, pseudonyms), keeps a matte hand-painted dot craftsmanship, and wraps social commentary in folk-playfulness rather than consumer gloss. |
| **Surrealism** | Hybrid creatures, impossible scenes, early-period surrealism | Historical Surrealism renders dream logic illusionistically — modeled light, blended paint, deep perspective. Toyism's surrealism is subject-only: flat unmixed color, sharp contour, dotwork, flattened space, and a narrative playfulness rather than psychoanalytic dream. Even its sparse early machine scenes stay graphic, not painterly. |

**Quick recognition checklist:** flat unmixed hue compartments + regular hand-dotwork + crisp-contoured hybrid creatures with concentric eyes + filled-all-over composition + even shadowless light + a story with a sting. If the dots are halftone shading, it is Pop; if the pattern has no figures, it is Memphis; if the surface is glossy and branded, it is Neo-Pop; if the light models form illusionistically, it is Surrealism.
