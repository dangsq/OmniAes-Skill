# Junkpunk

Junkpunk — also Salvagepunk, Scavenged Punk, Scrappunk — is a stylized setting in which technology and culture run on scavenged junk. Weapons, tools, clothing and sometimes entire cities are assembled from repurposed and recycled material, and the defining rule is mis-use: a piece of trash doing a job it was never manufactured for, as opposed to something merely repaired and reused. In speculative fiction this happens in the remnant of society after an apocalypse, but the style equally describes contemporary practice — Cuban household DIY under trade embargo, the refurbishing of Western e-waste in developing countries, the self-built fabric of favelas, shantytowns and pueblos jóvenes. Philosophically it descends from the twentieth century's arts of reappropriation: montage, collage, détournement and farce. Where postmodern pastiche juggles interchangeable signs, junkpunk bolts together objects with particular properties, textures and tendencies — the art of salvage is knowing which objects can be lashed into a viable construction. Against bleaker post-apocalyptic moods, junkpunk keeps rebuilding as its central motif: ruin is raw material, not verdict.

## Visual Identity

**Palette.** A desaturated earth band carries the image: soot black-brown (#241C15), rust brown (#6B4A2F), bronze (#8C7853), tan and bone (#C19A6B), dust gray (#A99C8A). An oxide band — #7A3B1E through #B7410E — bridges neutral into accent. Accents may reach full saturation but only as the painted or molded skin of found objects, never as atmosphere: ochre yellow (#D9A400), patina or slab-paint green (#4F7942), signal red (#A32020), and occasional primary blue (#1F4FA3) and red (#C8102E) on panels, container walls, telephone bakelite, mobile vanes. Rule of thumb: neutrals and rust occupy the majority of surface area; saturated color appears in minority patches on things that were once manufactured goods.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.**
1. *Repurposed objects in mis-use* — the definition-level motif: spent cartridge casings strung as necklace beads, a rotary telephone body carrying fan blades, a car door worn as a shield, barrel-and-rebar robots.
2. *Rusted old objects, broken machines, vandalized decrepit buildings* — named as the most present imagery: dead engines, CRT monitors heaped in e-waste yards, gutted hulls, peeling facades.
3. *Scrap metal and trash accumulation; barricades* — piles, heaps, strata of stuff; improvised walls and checkpoints of welded debris.
4. *Collage and photomontage* in the Dada and Surrealist manner — junk-mosaic portraiture, cut-and-bolt assemblage fields.
5. *Scavenger fashion* — mismatched layered outfits; faded, dirty, greasy items; ripped cloth; leather; survival gear; many belts, zippers and chains; toolkit or big bag of odd items; weapons and tools hanging from belts; handmade rusty weapons; scrap-metal armor; layered jewelry made from recycled trash; strange objects attached to clothing. Steampunk, goth and grunge are the strongest borrowed vocabularies.
6. *Informal settlement architecture and rebuilding labor* — hillside brick-and-tin densities, tangled overhead cable webs, corrugated shacks, cranes and winches mid-lift, makers beside their machines.
7. *Assemblage sculpture and mobiles* — wire armatures with balanced colored vanes, kinetic stabiles, wall reliefs of bolted fragments.

**Materials and textures.** Flaking rust, oxidized bronze and copper patina, corrugated sheet, weathered plywood, cracked CRT plastic, twisted copper cable, twine and cord lashings, zip ties, duct tape, welded seams with visible beads, patched leather and torn canvas and camo, bottle caps, hubcaps, cartridge brass, chain links, greasy canvas webbing. Surfaces keep their history: scratches, dents, sun-bleaching, grease blooms, dust coats.

**Recurring subjects.** Costumed scavengers and makers; jewelry and small assemblages documented on neutral busts or plinths; junkyards and e-waste piles; settlement streets and alley corridors; scrap-built vehicles, barges and walking sheds; workshop benches; collage portraits; market stalls of odd components.

**Composition and lighting.** Density is the default: heaped piles, layered walls, hillside sprawl, cable tangles overhead; negative space survives only as sky above a heap or the seamless ground of an object study. Typical framings include the full-body environmental portrait with working clutter behind, the wide establishing shot of accumulation, the vertical corridor under wires, the flat frontal collage grid, and the calm centered still-life of a worn object on neutral ground. Light ranges from bleached overhead desert sun with hard shadows and airborne dust, through flat overcast on yards and piles, to night alleys lit by single bulbs and string lights, warm tungsten pockets in blue dusk, and soft diffuse studio light for object documentation. None of these is mandatory; the constant is that light reveals texture — every dent and oxide bloom stays readable.

## Mood & Values

Ingenuity over consumption; dignity of salvage. Junkpunk is optimistic apocalypse: hands at work, things made to function again, habitation pressing into ruin. It carries a strand of humor and farce — contraptions that barely work, costumes that brag — and a communal, festival-craft energy. Its values are material honesty (objects keep their specific histories), anti-gloss (nothing is factory-fresh), and refusal of despair: the dump is a quarry, the embargo a design brief.

## Era & Origins

The term crystallized in the 2010s through Evan Calder Williams's theory of combined and uneven apocalypse and China Miéville's salvage fictions, with later consolidation in online aesthetic discourse. Its visual grammar is older and borrowed: Dada and Surrealist photomontage, mid-century assemblage and kinetic sculpture, late-twentieth-century salvage cinema of desert convoys and junk cities, and early-2000s game worlds of rustbelt settlements. Its real-life substrate is ongoing and global: Cuban DIY electronics, e-waste refurbishment trades, self-built informal urbanism. It reacts against postmodern pastiche's friction-free juxtaposition, against consumer newness, and against post-apocalyptic hopelessness.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- *Mis-use must legible:* at least one prominent object should obviously serve a purpose other than its manufacture — casings as beads, propellers on phones, tubs as pauldrons.
- *Joinery is ornament:* show the weld, the lashing, the bolt row, the zip tie, the tape wrap; construction method is part of the beauty.
- *Material honesty:* rust, dust, grease, fade and wear on every surface; no blemish-free zones.
- *Accumulation over minimalism:* layer, heap, stack; strata of stuff.
- *Rebuilding, not ruin:* include evidence of use, repair, cultivation, habitation — someone lives here and made this work.
- *Accent discipline:* saturated color only as painted or molded surfaces of found objects, in minority area.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a costume portrait, a settlement street, an object still-life, an assemblage sculpture, a collage field, a scrap vehicle or vessel, a workshop bench, a market stall, an e-waste yard, a hillside city. Composition may run from centered full-body environmental portrait to wide dense establishing shot, vertical alley, flat collage grid, or calm neutral-ground object study. Lighting may be bleached noon sun, flat overcast, tungsten practical night, blue-hour dusk, or soft studio diffuse. Color handling may sit anywhere from near-monochrome rust-and-tan to neutral base plus bold primary accents. Mood may be defiant carnival, quiet documentary, humorous farce, or tender still-life. Medium may be photograph, film still, concept painting, collage, or sculpture documentation. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Factory-fresh consumer goods and unblemished plastic or chrome; neon glow, holograms, cyan-magenta night lighting, rain-slick corporate gloss; pure collapse with nothing reused — empty despairing ruin; minimalism and luxurious negative space; matte beige designer interiors; seamless CGI smoothness and perfect symmetry; polished steampunk brass filigree and decorative gears without grime; new-looking tactical nylon and uniform military kit; pastel gradients and vaporwave haze; rewilding overtaking ruins as the subject (junkpunk is human reuse, not nature reclaiming); digital glitch and datamosh artifacts; pristine street-art muralism standing in for weathered tags and peeling paint.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Post-Apocalyptic:** neighbor shows collapse as endpoint — empty streets, dread, survival minimalism. Junkpunk shows collapse as inventory: repaired joints, lived-in clutter, gardens in tires, work in progress.
- **Avant Apocalypse:** neighbor is runway-coded, editorial, couture silhouettes with art-directed distress. Junkpunk is DIY-legible and functional-looking — festival craft, workshop seams, documentary grain rather than fashion lighting.
- **Cyberpunk:** neighbor's technology is alive, networked, glossy: neon signage, chrome, interfaces, rain-slick glass. Junkpunk's technology is dead and re-employed: analog, mechanical, rusted, lit at most by a bare bulb.
- **Hauntology:** neighbor mourns lost futures through faded institutional modernism, archive haze and melancholy stillness. Junkpunk handles the same detritus with active hands — labor, density, bolted new purpose instead of nostalgic fog.
- **Hip-Hop:** a philosophical cousin through sampling, but visually centered on fresh streetwear, audio gear, gold chains, block-party color. Junkpunk shares only the collage-and-sample logic; its field is rust, cordage and scrap construction, not boombox freshest.

Where evidence is thin, say so: the style's music writing names genres and sampling ethics but offers little dedicated iconography, so turntables, cassette splices and speaker cones belong to the optional collage vocabulary, not the core motif set.
