# Spicers

Spicers (singular "Spicer") is the name suburban Dublin gave to a teen subculture that crystallised in the late 2000s around Swords, Lucan, Malahide and Raheny and ran into the early 2010s. It is a loud, local remix of British scene/emorave fashion and continental techno-dance culture: straightened, heavily dyed mullets, Adidas tracksuits, shutter shades and neon-tacky outfits, lived out offline in suburban streets and house parties and online on Bebo, where custom profile backgrounds, Skins-inspired photo collages, glitter-stylised text, auto-playing flashbox tracks and the public drama of the "Top 16" friends list formed the style's second wardrobe. The look was designed to be instantly recognisable at a distance — hair first, volume and chroma maxed, taste deliberately tacky.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** Colour is artificial and maxed: box-dye chroma, never earthy, never pastel, never blended. The text names green, blue and red as the key colours; surviving imagery confirms those and extends the same neon family to hot magenta/pink and teal, plus occasional tacky sunshine-yellow accents. Neons sit in hard-edged relationships — streaks, chunks, dip-tips, half-and-half splits, shaved-side crests — against a base of jet black or bleach platinum. Boundaries between hues are crisp because the tools are box dye and a hair straightener, not a colourist's brush.

**Hair is the protagonist (top-ranked motif).** Heavily dyed, straightened mullets with heavy fringes: spiked and fanned at the crown, long at the nape, fringe ironed flat and often falling over one or both eyes. Acceptable silhouettes range from full spiky mullet, to undercut with shaved sides and a coloured crest, to split-dye bobs with long ironed fringe, to short crops with a colour-swept rainbow fringe — unified by flat-ironed shine, fanned spikes and hard colour edges. The hair straightener is the style's defining tool.

**Wardrobe and body mods (text-ranked).** Adidas tracksuits, shutter shades, skinny jeans, graphic tees, bandanas; facial piercings — lip studs, nose studs — read as standard hardware. Makeup corroborates: heavy black liner worked into the waterline, pale base, coloured shadow echoing the hair, sometimes thin drawn brows. Materials are cheap and shiny: polyester tracksuit sheen, plastic shutter-shade slats, cotton print tees, small steel studs.

**Setting, composition, lighting.** The snapshot grammar of a compact camera or early phone cam: direct on-camera flash producing flat frontal light, hot highlights on forehead and hair shine, dark falloff behind; or bare overhead bulb light in bathrooms and bedrooms. Crops favour head-and-shoulders or three-quarter views that privilege the hair silhouette; mirror selfies, tilted casual angles, cluttered domestic backgrounds (tile, curtains, bedroom doors) are typical. Low megapixel softness, JPEG artefacts and slight overexposure belong to the medium. Group frames — suburban street, house party, shuffling circle — are the text-documented social extension of this grammar.

**The digital layer.** Where screens or graphic layouts appear: Bebo profile furniture — custom embedded backgrounds, Skins-toned photo collages, glittery stylised text, "Top 16" friend grids, flashbox music players — is period-correct set dressing.

## Mood & Values

Party-like attitude is the declared core value: loud, grinning, up-for-it. Tackiness is a virtue and a badge of belonging; visibility is social currency, performed twice — once on the suburban street, once on the Bebo profile where friendships and dramas were publicly ranked. The mood is exuberant, cheeky, self-staged and unapologetic, never melancholic or austere.

## Era & Origins

Dublin, Ireland, late 2000s into the early 2010s; Bebo was its digital home before Facebook's takeover, and the teen drama Skins (2007) supplied profile-background iconography. Musically it ran on jumpstyle, hardstyle and dubstep, with techno shuffling practised in groups. The style reacts to and re-mixes imported currents — UK scene fashion, continental techno dance culture — into an insistently local, suburban Irish identity.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. Hair leads the frame: straightened, spiked, hard-edged neon over black or platinum.
2. Keep chroma artificial and edges crisp; no blended gradients in hair or palette.
3. Dress the body in tacky-loud sportswear economy: tracksuit, shutter shades, bandana, skinny jeans, graphic tee, studs.
4. Light like an amateur: direct flash or bare bulb, domestic interior, compact-camera softness.
5. Carry the party attitude in posture and face — grin, pose, cheek — even in stillness.
6. If including screens or graphics, use Bebo-era furniture, not modern platforms.

Variation axes: subject (solo portrait, mirror selfie, group shuffling, house party, suburban street, bedroom Bebo session, screen/layout composition); composition (tight head crop to full-body group, tilted or straight, mirror or direct); lighting (on-camera flash, overhead bulb, overcast suburban daylight, party-room lamplight); colour handling (which neons dominate, streak vs block vs split-dye vs undercut crest, black vs blonde base); mood (grinning, deadpan-cheeky, mid-dance, posed-bravado). A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. Note honestly: surviving imagery skews to hair portraits, so wardrobe, dance and digital-layer motifs rest mainly on textual documentation and may be drawn on freely.

Avoid: polished studio or softbox lighting, ring-light catchlights, modern smartphone HDR sharpness; muted, earthy or pastel palettes; ombré, balayage or any blended hair colour; curly, shaggy or soft mullet-revival texture (the Spicer mullet is ironed and fanned); couture, minimalist or boho wardrobe; goth, grunge or melancholic staging; modern platform UI, LED strips, vape clouds or other post-2012 props; dominant American scene signifiers (coontail stripes, studded belts, metalcore band merch); dance-brand choreography poses with neon wristbands as the sole subject.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Scene** (US/UK): shares neon hair and skinny jeans but centres rock fandom — teased coontail-striped layers, band tees, studded belts, Myspace peace-sign angles, ironic-angst tone. Spicer tell: flat-ironed spiky mullet plus tracksuit/shutter shades plus flash-lit Irish domestic interior, party-loud rather than angsty.
- **Krocha** (Austria/Germany, same years): also neon and hardstyle, but presents as event-crew uniform — matching neon sportswear sets, group party and club frames, often full-head single neon colour, no Bebo/Skins layer, Central European settings. Spicer tell: domestic selfie grammar, streaked mullet, Bebo furniture, Dublin suburbia.
- **Tecktonik** (France): dance-first movement; imagery is dominated by branded arm-flinging poses, geometric cropped single-colour cuts, neon wristbands and arm warmers, club or street-dance backdrops. Spicer tell: hair is the subject and the body is casual; shuffling appears as group activity, not as choreographic branding.
