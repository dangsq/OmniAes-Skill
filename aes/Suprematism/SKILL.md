# Suprematism

Suprematism is the first fully non-objective art: painting with nothing to depict. Founded by Kazimir Malevich in the Russian Empire and publicly announced in 1915 at the "Last Futurist Exhibition of Paintings 0,10" in Petrograd, it declares the supremacy of pure artistic feeling over the representation of objects. Its pictures are vessels of sensation built from the simplest means — a square, a circle, a cross, a bar of colour — suspended in a white field that stands for infinite space, the cosmos in which forms drift in dynamic relation. Malevich's *Black Square* (1915) was presented as the "zero point" of art, the wipe-clean beginning from which everything else in the style unfolds: red squares, black crosses and circles, white-on-white near-vanishing, and busy constellations of tilted planes. The movement lived fast and died young — ideologically attacked by Constructivism, which borrowed its geometry but demanded art serve the Soviet state, and suppressed by the 1930s in favour of Socialist Realism — yet its leap into pure abstraction fed De Stijl, the Bauhaus and everything after.

## Visual Identity

### Colour: the void and the pure hue
The palette rule is limitation. Every composition sits on a ground that is itself a colour statement: an unbounded white, off-white or cream field — the void, the cosmos — ranging from fresh paper white (`#FBFAF6`) through warm cream (`#F4EFE3`) to aged ivory/beige (`#E9E0CC`). Against it, forms take a small number of pure, unmixed-feeling hues: black (`#0B0B0C`–`#201D1A`, often a warm, aged oil black), vermilion red (`#C2311C`–`#E0492A`), yellow and ochre (`#E5B33C`–`#D9A341`), green (`#3F8E4C`–`#63A55C`), cobalt/ultramarine blue (`#1E3A8F`–`#2E4FA3`), with secondary accents allowed: sky blue (`#5E93D6`), pale rose (`#E8B7C9`), ochre-brown (`#8A5A2B`), grey-teal (`#7CA3A8`), deep aubergine-black (`#241F26`). A correct work typically uses one ground plus two to five form colours plus black; the monochrome poles are equally canonical — a single black form on white, a single red form on white, or white-on-white where the form nearly dissolves into the ground. Never a full-spectrum rainbow, never earthy Cubist greys and umbers as the dominant key, never neon or digital saturation.

### Signature motifs, ranked as the movement's own theory ranks them
1. **The square** — the founding gesture, from the monumental *Black Square* to the tilted coloured square spinning in space; the square may be centred and dominant or rotated and adrift.
2. **The circle** — a single dense disc holding the field, or a satellite among planes.
3. **The cross** — thick-armed, flat, frontal, occupying the canvas as an absolute sign.
4. **Rectangles, bars and lines** — elongated planes at conflicting angles, crossing and overlapping like beams in zero gravity; hairline rods and thin arcs as counterpoint.
5. **The floating relation itself** — forms never rest on a ground line; they hover, overlap, scatter, and the empty white between them is an active participant, not leftover space.
6. **Proun extensions** (El Lissitzky's orbit within the movement): axonometric solids built of flat tonal facets, fine radiating hairlines and slender curved arcs anchored to a virtual axis — a bridge variant, still weightless, still non-objective.

### Materials and surface
Oil paint on canvas or linen is the characteristic medium (with gouache-like paper works in the same idiom). The paint film is matte to low-sheen, laid in visible brush drags inside large planes; edges of forms are crisp but hand-cut — slight wobble, a softened corner, a faint bleed where a bar meets the ground — never vector-perfect. Age is part of the look in many works: fine craquelure cracking dark monochrome surfaces, grounds yellowed toward cream, canvas weave faintly visible. No gloss varnish shine, no airbrush gradient, no 3D-render smoothness.

### Composition and space
Two poles define the compositional range, with everything between them permitted. **Monochrome monument**: one form — square, cross, circle, tilted quadrilateral — centred or near-centred, occupying most of the canvas, the ground reduced to a breathing margin. **Dynamic scatter**: a constellation of tilted planes and bars, rotated off the orthogonal (commonly 10–45 degrees, but any angle that produces drift), crossing at shallow diagonals, clustered asymmetrically in one or two zones while vast stretches of field stay empty; strong scale contrast between a monumental plane and sliver-thin rods. Forms float free of the canvas edges and never touch or align with the border as a rule. Depth is implied only by overlap and relative scale — there is no perspective, no horizon, no room, no table. The Proun variant adds axonometric facet planes and hairline geometry on a diagonal axis, still suspended in the void.

### Light
There is no depicted light. No cast shadows, no highlights, no gradients, no modelling: forms are flat colour planes under even, shadowless illumination. Any sense of light belongs to the surface, not the scene — matte paint, occasional raking light revealing canvas texture if the work is photographed as an object. Depthless white is the only "atmosphere" Suprematism admits.

## Mood & Values

Suprematism feels like silence with velocity in it. The scatter compositions carry cosmic energy — sensation in motion, planes launched and drifting — while the monochromes are meditative absolutes, and white-on-white approaches immaterial transcendence. Underlying values: the supremacy of pure artistic feeling over depiction; non-objectivity as liberation; art as a spiritual, autonomous act rather than a tool; rejection of mimetic representation; the ambition of a universal visual language readable beyond any nation or object-world. The mood is austere but utopian, ascetic but ecstatic — the "zero point" as both annihilation and beginning.

## Era & Origins

1910s Russian Empire, announced 1915 in Petrograd by Kazimir Malevich, with El Lissitzky, Liubov Popova, Olga Rozanova, Aleksandra Ekster, Ivan Kliun and others in its orbit. It grows out of and then breaks with Cubo-Futurism — the launching exhibition was literally titled "Last Futurist" — and reacts against all representational art, declaring the object obsolete. It fought a defining ideological battle with Constructivism, which kept the geometry but demanded utility for the Soviet state, and was crushed by state Socialist Realism in the 1930s. Its afterlife runs through De Stijl, the Bauhaus and all later geometric abstraction.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Begin with the void: an unbounded white/cream field that reads as infinite space, not as a page or a wall.
2. Place flat planes of pure colour into it — square, rectangle/bar, circle, cross, line — and let them float: no gravity, no shadow, no support.
3. Tilt for life: rotate forms off the axes so the composition implies drift, launch, suspension.
4. Limit the palette: one ground, a handful of pure hues plus black, or commit to a monochrome pole (black-on-white, red-on-white, white-on-white).
5. Make negative space a protagonist: empty field should feel vast and intentional, even (especially) when forms cluster.
6. Paint by hand: matte oil or gouache surface, brush drag inside planes, edges crisp but imperfect, optional craquelure and aged ground.
7. Keep it non-objective: no form may read as a thing — no flag, window, ball, machine, face or letter.
8. Overlap and scale-contrast for depth; never perspective, never gradient.

**Variation axes.** *Subject matter*: from a single monumental form to a twenty-element constellation; thin arcs, hairline rods and Proun-style axonometric facets are within range. *Composition*: centred monochrome; off-centre tilted square; crossing-bar lattice; asymmetric cluster against emptiness; diagonal Proun axis. *Colour handling*: full pure-hue scatter; two-colour dialogue; single hue on white; near-invisible white-on-white; ground from bright white to aged beige. *Lighting/surface*: always shadowless and even; surface may read as fresh matte paint or century-old cracked oil on linen. *Mood*: kinetic and cosmic, solemn and absolute, or transcendent and near-silent. A correct image can use any subject and arrangement within this non-objective range; it should echo the style's rules — void ground, flat pure planes, weightless tilt, limited palette, hand-painted surface — not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Recognizable objects, figures, faces, landscapes, architecture, machines. Text, letters, numerals, logos, typography of any kind. Cast shadows, drop shadows, gradients, volumetric shading, gloss, 3D-rendered bevels or glow. A continuous orthogonal grid or black line armature spanning the canvas; planes flush to or cropped by the canvas border as a system. Photomontage, photography, halftone, print texture. Decorative symmetry, ornamental pattern, painted frames or borders. Earthy faceted fragmentation of a hidden subject (Cubism). Serial repetition of identical modules (Minimalism). Neon, metallic or digital colour; machine-perfect vector edges.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Constructivism** shares the geometric vocabulary but is instrumental: sans-serif typography, photomontage, agit-prop diagonals, implied industrial materials (metal, wood, glass), wedges and arrows with political referents. Suprematism carries no text, no function, no message; its planes are weightless and referent-less, and its white is cosmos, not a page or poster.
- **Cubism** still parses the visible world — guitars, bottles, newspapers, figures — into faceted ochre-grey planes in a shallow compressed space, often with collage and lettering. Suprematism abolishes the object entirely, uses saturated pure hues on open white, and never facetates a hidden subject.
- **De Stijl** builds a continuous orthogonal armature: black horizontal and vertical lines running edge to edge, primaries locked into a grid, planes flush with the canvas border. Suprematism tilts its forms, lets them float free of all edges, and never draws a spanning grid line; diagonals and drift are Suprematist, orthogonals-in-grid are De Stijl.
- **International Typographic Style** is communication design: sans-serif type, photographic imagery, a mathematical grid, rational asymmetric layout on white. Suprematism has no type, no photography and nothing to communicate; its asymmetry is sensation, not information hierarchy.
- **Minimalism** (1960s on) is literal and fabricated: industrial surfaces, serial repetition, monochrome or neutral colour, edges asserting the physical object, no illusion of space. Suprematism is hand-painted, singular and dynamic, and its white field reads as infinite depth in which forms drift — brush drag, wobble and aged canvas are the tell.

Quick test: engineered for use or message → Constructivism or Swiss design; locked in a grid → De Stijl; fabricated and literal → Minimalism; parsing an object → Cubism; pure feeling adrift in boundless white → Suprematism.
