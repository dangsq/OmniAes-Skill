# Digital Horror

## Overview

Digital Horror is a broad genre of online horror defined by its medium and its themes rather than one cohesive look: it reproduces the aesthetics, artifacts and limitations of early-to-mid digital culture (late 1990s to early/mid 2010s) to manufacture dread, nostalgia and unease. Its scare engine is the corruption of familiar digital experience — haunted video games, cursed websites, sentient AI, corrupted save files, disturbing web animations, ghost sightings caught on digital camcorders — and above all the twisting of innocent early-internet objects (a Flash game, an old YouTube upload, a children's cartoon character, a personal homepage) into something sinister. Because the genre is medium-defined, its visual identity is a family of "captured digital object" looks: screenshots, ripped video frames, game captures, title cards and terminal logs, all deliberately lo-fi to feell authentically uploaded rather than designed. Platforms and lineage matter: YouTube, Newgrounds and creepypasta wikis; Salad Fingers, Ben Drowned, Petscop; David Firth, Alex Hall (Jadusable).

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The color language works in registers rather than a fixed scheme; a correct image picks one register and lets darkness invade it.

| Register | Hex range | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Void black / near-black | #050505–#161616 | backgrounds, letterbox bars, hollow eyes, empty corridors |
| Alarm and dried blood red | #7a0c0c–#e60000 | monochrome tints, splatter shapes, glowing eyes, corrupted title screens |
| Desaturated organic murk | #2a241c–#4a4438 (olive-brown, gray-green) | decayed rooms, sickly skin, darkened cartoon backdrops |
| Bone white / pale gray | #cfcfcf–#f2f2f2 | cartoon bodies, overexposed faces, monospace log text, scratchy line art |
| Garish early-web pastels | sky blue #6ec6e8, magenta #c840a0, butter yellow #f0d060, hot pink #e888c8 | the "innocent" layer: Flash game UI, kids' vector art, title-card wallpapers |

Rule: saturation is either crushed (murk, grayscale noise) or weaponized (a single red flooding the frame); never a balanced full-spectrum palette, never warm cinematic grading. The pastel register only appears as the banal base that the horror contaminates.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how centrally the text documents them)

1. **Low-quality digital artifacts.** Low-resolution video, pixelated graphics, compression smear, dithering, nearest-neighbor upscaling — lo-fi presentation used deliberately to evoke authenticity and nostalgia. This is the genre's first marker.
2. **Obsolete software and old-web interface chrome.** Bandicam-style screen-recorder watermarks, Windows Movie Maker title cards, default system fonts (Comic Sans above all), HUD counters, menu lists, subtitle strips, timestamp and year stamps ("2010"), monospace terminal logs, handwritten scrawl overlays ("you're still here").
3. **Corrupted nostalgia.** A beloved or banal media object — children's cartoon mascot, network logo, birthday dedication, personal photo — revealed as sinister: bloodshot or void-black eyes inserted into a cheerful face, blood on a logo, a wrong line of dialogue under a friendly scene.
4. **Corrupted game data and haunted game worlds.** Faked console gameplay, low-poly corridors and rooms, crude title screens with impossible menu options ("IMMORTAL / HE MUST DECIDE"), polygon seams, affine texture warp, N64/PS1/early-PC graphics.
5. **Flash-era web animation crudeness.** Flat vector fills, scratchy pen-line characters, stick figures, wobbly MS-Paint drawings, limited-palette gore scribbles — the Salad Fingers / Newgrounds lineage.
6. **Haunted technology and glitch entities.** Sentient AI, corrupted data, paranormal forces making digital worlds behave impossibly; entities that exist inside the software rather than in a room.
7. **Digital found footage.** Ghost-sighting clips and webseries shot on early smartphones and digital camcorders: harsh on-camera flash, dark rooms, tiny figures at the edge of a murky frame.
8. **Existential dread and lingering presence.** Text and imagery of being watched, remembered, or left behind; melancholy captions addressed to a dead or lost person.

### Materials and textures

JPEG macroblocking and chroma smear; GIF banding; sensor noise and dither overlaid even on flat vector art; pixel grid visible at low effective resolution; polygon seams and warped low-res textures in 3D scenes; patterned JPEG wallpaper behind title cards; scratchy ballpoint-like line work in drawn pieces; soft focus and flash blowout in camcorder pieces. Everything reads as a file that has been re-encoded too many times.

### Recurring subjects

Cartoon mascots and animal characters (rabbits, cats, foxes, sponge-like figures) with hollow black or bloodshot eyes; pale human faces with void eyes and stretched or smeared mouths; crouching humanoid creatures in dark rooms; low-poly game avatars standing alone in corridors or voids; game title screens and level HUDs; home-video dedication cards with a small blurred photo inset; terminal windows of log text beside a distorted face; crude drawings of figures on red fields.

### Composition and lighting

Composition follows screenshot logic, not painterly logic: frontal, center-weighted captures; letterbox bars; UI chrome intruding at edges (sidebars, watermarks, subtitle strips, menu columns); large fields of empty black with one small lit figure; title cards with text floating over patterned backgrounds. Lighting modes: flat sourceless vector light in Flash-style pieces; murky underlit practicals (a single yellow pool of light in a dark corridor); harsh on-camera flash overexposing a face in a black room; total red monochrome glow; near-blackness with one dim spotlight. Shadows are crushed to pure black rather than graded.

## Mood & Values

Dread built from intimacy: the things that comforted a childhood online — cartoons, games, birthday videos, personal homepages — turn out to be haunted, watched, or grieving. The mood is lonely, amateurish and sincere: uncanny faces stare out of frames that feel uploaded by a single anonymous person at 2 a.m. Values expressed: DIY web culture and ARG community play (the audience is meant to investigate, archive and argue about authenticity), reverence for forgotten software, and anxiety about sentient technology and corrupted memory. Melancholy sits beside menace — captions mourn as often as they threaten.

## Era & Origins

Roots in early-2000s creepypastas and viral shock content; foundational works include Salad Fingers (2004) and the Ben Drowned ARG (2010) with its faked console footage, plus mid-late-2000s ghost-sighting videos on early video-hosting sites. Late-2000s/early-2010s webseries (Marble Hornets, EverymanHYBRID, No Through Road) brought the supernatural into digital camcorders and smartphones. Petscop (2017) pushed game-based horror storytelling; the 2020s revived and refined the formula through forgotten Flash-game and 2000s-website aesthetics (Lacey Games, The June Archive and Restoration Project) and haunted-video recreations of the era (Morley Grove, Mystifying Oracle, Mandela Catalogue's digital-era work). The genre reacts against polished modern media by fetishizing the limits of early digital tools, and against Analog Horror by choosing software decay over tape decay.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Decide what digital object the frame is before composing it: a game capture, a ripped video frame, an uploaded GIF, a web page, a title card, a terminal log. The artifact class dictates the chrome.
2. Build one innocent period-correct layer (cheerful cartoon, game menu, birthday card, kids' game UI) and introduce exactly one wrong element: void eyes, a blood smear, an impossible menu option, a caption that knows too much.
3. Degrade with period tools: cap effective resolution, add pixelation, JPEG macroblocks, dither, noise, banding. No crisp edges anywhere.
4. Use typography as a prop: Comic Sans, Times, Arial, monospace; watermarks, HUD counters, subtitle strips, year stamps, handwritten scrawl. Misspellings and over-punctuated amateur text ("Happy Birthday Dustin!!!!") are authentic.
5. Stay inside the palette registers: black-plus-red monochrome, desaturated murk, bone-white on black, or garish pastels being invaded by dark.
6. Prefer dread over splatter; hollow eyes, wrong smiles and lingering captions carry more weight than gore.
7. Keep composition amateur: frontal, slightly misaligned, default-transition flatness. It should look uploaded, not art-directed.

**Variation axes**

- Subject: corrupted mascots, low-poly game worlds, Flash-animated creatures, cursed websites and UI, terminal logs, camcorder ghost footage, crude paint drawings, edited personal photos, dedication cards.
- Composition: full-frame screenshot with UI chrome; letterboxed video frame; centered face close-up; vast black void with a tiny figure; title card with inset photo; split text-panel plus image.
- Lighting: flat vector light; murky single-practical corridors; on-camera flash blowout; red monochrome flood; spotlight in blackness.
- Color handling: any single register from the palette table, with saturation crushed or weaponized.
- Mood: mock-cheerful, melancholic, paranoid, mournful, clinically detached (log text).

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — digital mediation, period artifacting, corrupted nostalgia — not any reference image. The sample set skews toward corrupted-mascot and game-capture variants; the camcorder found-footage variant is well documented in the text though thinner in the sample, and is equally valid.

**Avoid**

- VHS tracking bars, tape decay, interlace combing, broadcast test cards as the dominant artifact (that is Analog Horror).
- Film grain, lens flare, anamorphic bokeh, cinematic color grades, golden-hour warmth.
- Crisp 4K rendering, modern flat/material-design UI, post-2015 iconography (smartphone notches, streamer overlays, modern emoji).
- Photographic explicit gore or viscera as the payload (Goreweb).
- Pure abstract datamosh, pixel-sorting or rainbow channel-shift compositions with no legible scene or UI (Glitch Art).
- Posed fan-character portraits in scene/emo styling without any media-artifact framing (2010s Dark Fandom).
- Gothic or Victorian occult iconography: candles, mansions, fog, ornate frames.
- Clean undegraded vector mascot art; balanced saturated full-color palettes.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Analog Horror:** both are found-footage horror with text cards and dark rooms, but Analog Horror's artifacts are physical-media decay — tracking errors, tape hiss visuals, interlace, VCR timecodes, 1960s–90s broadcast ephemera. Digital Horror's artifacts are computational: macroblocks, pixelation, dithering, polygon seams, software watermarks, system fonts, game engines, web pages, late-1990s-to-2010s ephemera. Check the artifact family and the interface era first.
- **Horror (general):** traditional horror needs no technological mediation — painterly or photographic dread, monsters, gothic settings. Digital Horror always frames the scare inside a digital object; strip the screen, file, UI or capture logic and the style collapses.
- **2010s Dark Fandom:** dark edits organized around fan characters and creepypasta proxies with scene/emo styling, usually clean or filter-heavy digital art where character identity is the subject. Digital Horror subordinates characters to the haunted media object: they appear as low-fi captures inside corrupted games, videos or pages, not as posed portraits.
- **Glitch Art:** corruption as subject and pleasure — abstract datamosh floods, pixel sorting, channel shifts, composition dissolved into error. In Digital Horror, glitches are narrative symptoms inside a legible scene or interface, sparing and pointed (one wrong face in a correct game), always paired with nostalgic content and dread.
- **Goreweb:** shock-site explicit photographic gore where splatter is the payload. Digital Horror implies violence or renders it crudely in Flash-era scribbles and low-res smears; gore stays subordinate to the haunted-media framing, and photographic viscera without digital mediation is not this style.
