# Ouji (Boystyle / Prince Style)

## Overview

Ouji — literally "prince" — is the youthful pole of Boystyle, a Japanese fashion invented as the masculine brother of Lolita in the late 1990s and early 2000s. It borrows its wardrobe from 18th- and 19th-century European menswear: British Victorian young gentlemen, nobility, and a spray of French Rococo ornament, filtered through Japanese brand fashion (Alice and the Pirates, Atelier Boz, Moi-Même-Moitié, Metamorphose) and manga imagination (*Black Butler*, *The Rose of Versailles*, *Ludwig Revolution*). Despite the "boy" in Boystyle, the look is deliberately androgynous and is most often worn by women; longer hair, makeup and delicate grooming are part of the grammar, not an exception to it. The style lives in a fandom ecosystem — dansou cosplayers, BL/fujoshi subculture, visual kei musicians — which explains why its imagery ranges from tidy magazine coordinate pages to decadent band promo photographs. Whatever the setting, the ideal is constant: a boyish yet elegant prince, cohesive and highly detailed from hat to boot sole.

## Visual Identity

**Silhouette rule (the load-bearing element).** The bottom half is shorts: knickerbockers or "pumpkin pants" gathered or cuffed at the knee, voluminous through the thigh and cinched below it. This single garment separates Ouji from every skirt-based neighbor. Above the waist the line is fitted and layered — blouse, vest, jacket — never loose or boxy. Jackets use period cuts: swallowtails, coats that flare and drape at the waist, pirate- or military-uniform-inspired fronts. The resulting figure is a narrow tailored torso over a puffed, knee-terminated leg: a vertical, princely line with a deliberate break at the knee, where socks or stockings show between pant hem and boot top. Keep the knee in frame; cropping above it destroys the style's main tell.

**Palette rule.** Start from a neutral base of ink black, charcoal, ivory and shirt-white, then commit to one substyle family and stay inside it; metallic hardware (antique gold, silver) punctuates any family. Prints — tartan plaid, stripe, brocade, crest motifs — are welcome but must sit inside the chosen family.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the style documents them.** (1) Knee-gathered knickerbockers / pumpkin pants / shorts — the defining component. (2) The fitted upper stack: blouse with jabot (neck ruffle), large collar or lace accents; vest worn alone or layered; period-cut jacket. (3) Headwear: the top hat is called essential; mini hats, berets, ribbon-trimmed caps and, at full extravagance, crowns follow. (4) Boots: knee- or mid-calf, laced or chunky, often platform-soled, low-heeled. (5) Visible legwear in the knee gap — knee socks, over-knees, stockings. (6) Silver necklaces, antique leather belts, buckles and chains. (7) Military/pirate uniform grammar: braid, epaulettes, button rows, cross-belts and harness straps (strongly amplified in visual-kei-adjacent imagery). (8) Royal fabrics and prints: silk, velvet, high-quality printed cloth explicitly preferred over cotton; tartan and brocade recur. (9) Androgynous grooming: styled longer hair, makeup, soft features on any gender.

**Materials and textures.** Velvet pile, silk sheen, brocade and jacquard weave, crisp tartan wool-look, lace and frilled chiffon at the throat and cuffs, matte leather belts and boot shafts, cold silver and dulled gold metalwork, fur stoles in the decadent gothic register. Surfaces should look like brand garment construction — pressed seams, lined jackets, real buttonholes — not thin costume satin.

**Recurring subjects.** Androgynous youthful models and cosplayers in full coordinates; princes and young nobles from manga ancestry (the Ciel Phantomhive type); visual kei musicians in uniformed group poses; mannequins and flat garment displays; costume design sheets with front/back views; magazine coordinate pages with item callouts and Japanese text blocks.

**Composition and lighting tendencies.** Full-body or three-quarter framing dominates because the silhouette is the message; group lines and paired figures appear in band and brand contexts. Documented lighting modes span three registers: even, soft studio light on plain pale backgrounds for product and coord photography; warm, dim, candle-toned light in antique interiors (carved wood cabinets, gilt frames, paneled walls, staircases); and cool, desaturated low-key light for gothic and visual-kei crossover imagery. Magazine scans add a fourth "medium": flat page layout with scattered item photos and text columns.

## Mood & Values

Ouji values the princely ideal: romantic, tailored, slightly theatrical boyish elegance. Cohesion and detail density are moral virtues — a coordinate succeeds when every accessory agrees with the whole. The style also carries a quiet argument about gender: frills, jabots, makeup and long hair on a "prince" challenge masculine norms while keeping grace rather than parody. Youthfulness is the Ouji pole's specific charge — playfulness, crowns, extravagant accessories — against the restrained maturity of its Dandy sibling. Fandom belonging (BL, visual kei, cosplay) supplies a layer of devoted, collectible enthusiasm: brands, coordinate blogs, magazine features.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in Japan in the late 1990s/early 2000s as the masculine counterpart to Lolita fashion, conceptually built to parallel the Lolita "princess" with a complementary graceful masculinity. Its visual sources are 18th–19th-century European menswear — Victorian British young gentlemen and nobility, with Rococo ornament — romanticized rather than reenacted. Adjacent currents: Aristocrat, Dandy, Gothic Lolita, Military Lolita, Visual Kei, Victorian Goth, Steampunk; illustrators such as Ayami Kojima and Shinichi Sakamoto bridge it to Tanbi Kei.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build bottom-up: knee-gathered shorts or knickerbockers first; everything else hangs off that decision.
2. Tailor the torso: fitted blouse + vest and/or period-cut jacket; jabot, large collar or lace at the neck.
3. Crown the head: top hat, mini hat, beret or crown in nearly every coordinate.
4. Finish the leg: knee- or mid-calf laced or chunky platform boots, with socks or stockings visible in the gap.
5. Commit to one palette family (Gothic jewel, Classic earth, Sweet pastel) over a black/ivory base; add gold or silver hardware accents.
6. Keep detail cohesive: belts, chains, buttons and braid should feel curated, not random.
7. Present an androgynous, youthful figure; elegance over ruggedness.
8. Keep the knee line inside the frame.

**Variation axes.** Subject: studio portrait, magazine coord page, antique-interior scene, staircase or parlor tableau, group band promo, cosplay of a prince character, mannequin or flat product display, design sheet. Composition: full-body, three-quarter, paired or group figures, page-layout collage; camera straight-on or slightly low to dignify the figure. Lighting: even soft studio, warm dim interior with candle glow, or cool desaturated low-key gothic. Color handling: any of the three families, prints or solids, high or muted contrast within the family. Mood: innocent sweetness, refined classicism, decadent gothic drama, theatrical visual-kei intensity. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Skirts, dresses, petticoats or bell silhouettes as the primary bottom; full-length trousers (that is Dandy); sneakers, denim, hoodies, T-shirts, sportswear or modern streetwear; boxy, oversized or untailored cuts; matte cotton casual cloth, fleece, jersey; cheap costume-satin sheen and rough cosplay construction; steampunk goggles, brass gadgets or clockwork props as focal points; maid-apron frill without tailoring; rugged hypermasculine or sexualized presentation; neon or clashing modern color outside the families; minimalist modern business suiting; cropping that hides the knee gather.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Lolita / Gothic Lolita:** the bottom half decides. Lolita keeps skirt, petticoat and bell silhouette (even in black jewel tones with a bonnet); Ouji keeps knee-gathered pants and a fitted, non-bell line.
- **Vs Aristocrat:** Aristocrat is the mature register — long hemlines, floor-sweeping coats, skirt-or-trouser ambiguity, subdued decadence; Ouji is youthful, knee-terminated, crowned and playfully extravagant.
- **Vs Military Lolita:** Military Lolita mounts braid, epaulettes and uniform buttons on a feminine skirt-and-petticoat silhouette; Ouji mounts the same grammar on pants and a tailored jacket, with no petticoat volume.
- **Vs Dandy:** Dandy is the adult half of Boystyle — long trousers, suited waistcoats, subtle jewelry, restraint; Ouji is shorts, crowns, abundant accessory detail and youth.
- **Vs Visual Kei:** overlap is real (uniformed band promos), but visual kei leans on stage leather, spandex and heavier metal aggression; Ouji remains a wearable brand fashion with pressed tailoring, knee pants and coordinated accessories even at its most theatrical.
