# Jiangshi Fiction

Jiangshi (僵尸) fiction is a film-born aesthetic: a Hong Kong genre of the 1980s to mid-1990s that welds Chinese folk horror to slapstick comedy and kung-fu. Its visual language is borrowed wholesale from real Taoist exorcism tradition and Qing-dynasty corpse lore — the reanimated mandarin, the paper talisman, the rice, the mirror — and filtered through the grainy, fog-machine night worlds of studio-era Hong Kong sets. The style survives today across media: film stills, airbrushed poster art, cartoon poster illustration, and modern stage and cosplay homages, all of which keep the same costume and prop grammar even when the medium changes.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** The base is a cold nocturnal field: deep teal and slate blue-green ambience (#0d2b33–#29565c), fog and incense haze desaturating everything behind the foreground figures. Against it, three fixed accents carry the identity: talisman yellow (#e9c53a–#f6d43c), cinnabar red for script and seal marks (#a12a1c–#d1492a), and corpse skin — a waxy pale green (#b9cdb9–#7e9a86, shadowed toward #4f6b62). Official robes sit at near-black satin (#0b0d10–#22262c) with cool sheen highlights; funeral paper and rice supply off-white (#e6e1d3–#f2efe6); candles and lanterns drop small warm pools (#d99a3a–#f0c060). An acceptable outer range includes saturated theatrical color casts — magenta smoke, gel-red washes, poster-bright primaries — seen in stage reinterpretations and illustrated posters; what must never drop out is the yellow-on-dark talisman contrast and the cold green undertone of undead skin.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how the tradition documents them.**
1. *The jiangshi body*: reanimated corpse in a formal Qing mandarin robe — round-collar satin coat with embroidered rank badge, court bead necklace, black official hat with upturned brim and red finial, thick-soled boots — pale green-tinged face, dark sunken eye rings, arms extended stiffly forward at shoulder height, torso upright, movement implied as a hop from locked knees.
2. *Yellow Taoist fulu*: a narrow paper strip with cinnabar calligraphy, stuck to the jiangshi's forehead as a seal, held between a priest's fingers, or burned mid-ritual.
3. *The exorcism kit*: glutinous rice, peachwood sword, strung copper coins with square holes, eight-trigram bronze mirror — props of actual Taoist practice, handled as tools, not decoration.
4. *Funerary settings*: dim ancestral halls, desolate mortuary rooms, walled courtyard mansions, rural Lingnan villages, dark night forests; hanging white cloth, coffins, incense burners, candle rows, paper funeral gear.
5. *The Taoist priest exorcist* (the Master Kau archetype): yellow or grey Taoist robe, topknot or taoist cap, ritual hand seals, cinnabar ink brush, combat stance against the undead.

**Materials and textures.** Satin and silk sheen on robes; embroidered badge thread; wood-and-amber court beads; stiff fibrous talisman paper; loose rice grains; carved peachwood; oxidized copper and patinated bronze; lacquered coffin wood; candle wax; drifting fog and incense smoke; beneath all of it, soft film grain or airbrushed poster gradient.

**Subjects and composition.** Subject range runs from a lone jiangshi to family ensembles of undead (adult, child, ghostly woman in pale flowing dress), priest-versus-jiangshi confrontations, bumbling apprentice comedy pairs, ritual preparations at an altar, and object still-lifes of the exorcism kit. Composition tends frontal and symmetrical: tiered vertical poster stacks of figures facing the viewer, horizontal receding lines of hoppers in queue, centered ritual tableaux, close two-shots of a talisman being pressed to a forehead, and fog-layered depths with silhouettes behind. Lighting is low-key night: cool moonlit teal fill, warm candle practicals, beams scattered by mist; poster variants substitute smooth airbrushed skies; stage variants substitute saturated gel washes.

## Mood & Values

The emotional core is uncanny comedy: genuine dread undercut by slapstick, a hop that is frightening and absurd in the same frame. Underneath sits folk seriousness — filial piety and funeral propriety, the corpse owed its journey home — and Taoist order: the talisman is the threshold object, attached means sealed and calm, peeled means chaos loose. Ritual precision (correct robe, correct seal, correct tools) is the style's value system; disorder always wears the same formal costume as order.

## Era & Origins

Folk roots lie in Qing-dynasty corpse lore and living Taoist exorcism practice; the genre itself emerged in Hong Kong cinema around 1979–1980 (*The Spiritual Boxer Part II*, *Encounters of the Spooky Kind*), was codified and popularized by the *Mr. Vampire* series through the mid-1990s, and persists in cosplay and stage homage. It reacts to the late-kung-fu-film era by hybridizing martial choreography with ghost folklore and comedy.

## Generation Guidance

Principles: dress the undead correctly (Qing mandarin robe, rank badge, official hat, court beads, thick-soled boots); keep the corpse's posture rigid — arms forward, wrists limp or palms down, no relaxed bend; make the talisman legible as yellow paper with cinnabar script and give it narrative logic (seal, release, burn); hold the cold-teal-plus-yellow palette rule; set scenes in funerary or night-folk space with fog; light low-key with warm practicals; let tone slide between dread and slapstick but never leave both absent; treat props as functioning tools.

Variation axes: **subject** — lone jiangshi, queue, family ensemble, priest duel, apprentice comedy, altar ritual, kit still-life, poster illustration, stage homage; **composition** — frontal stack, receding hop line, close talisman two-shot, foggy wide courtyard, centered altar; **lighting** — moonlit teal, candle-warm interior, saturated stage gels, airbrushed poster gradient; **color handling** — desaturated filmic to poster-bright, within the palette rule; **mood** — from pure folk horror to broad comedy. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. Note honestly: glutinous rice, peachwood swords, coin strings and mirrors are text-canonical but rarely visible in surviving stills, so their presence is optional-but-correct.

**Avoid:** Western vampire kit (fangs, capes, crosses, garlic, stakes, blood-drinking); rotting gore zombies with exposed flesh — jiangshi skin is intact and waxy; flowing wuxia movement for the undead (leaps, swirling sleeves, mid-air swordplay); bent or casual arms on a jiangshi; wrong-dynasty costume (cross-collar Han/Tang/Ming hanfu, samurai armor, generic "ancient" dress) in place of the Qing mandarin robe; blank paper or Japanese ofuda instead of cinnabar-script fulu; bright cheerful daylight with no fog or night framing; neon cyberpunk, sci-fi, modern casual wear or phones on core figures; Halloween sheet-ghosts and skeletons; sexualized or couture remixes of the official robe.

## Discrimination Cues

Closest neighbor is **wuxia**, and the two share kung-fu choreography and period China — so separate them by grammar, not setting. Movement: wuxia bodies flow, fly and spin on weightless qinggong; the jiangshi hops rigidly, knees locked, arms fixed forward, hat never dislodged. Costume: wuxia favors cross-collar hanfu, sashes and swordsman gear across dynasties; jiangshi fiction is specifically Qing official dress with rank badge and mandarin hat. Props: wuxia carries swords, guandao, qin; jiangshi fiction carries fulu, rice, peachwood, coin string, bagua mirror. Protagonist: wuxia centers the wandering xia living by a honor code; jiangshi fiction centers the Taoist priest exorcist and the undead he manages. Tone and palette: wuxia leans chivalric romance or ink-wash austerity, often in daylight; jiangshi fiction leans horror-comedy in cold funerary teal with talisman yellow, fog and candlelight. If the sleeves stream and the hero soars, it is wuxia; if the sleeves hang stiff and the figure bounces, it is jiangshi.
