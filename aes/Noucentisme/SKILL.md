# Noucentisme

Noucentisme was the Catalan cultural and political movement that held sway from roughly 1906 to 1923, coined and preached by the writer-philosopher Eugeni d'Ors ("Xènius") as a deliberate correction to the perceived decadence, emotional excess and individualism of Modernisme, Catalonia's variant of Art Nouveau. It proposed a return to classical ideals — order, reason, harmony, clarity — drawn from ancient Greece and Rome and the Italian Renaissance, and fused them with a regional program of civic building: the movement's "Mediterraneanism" idealized the Catalan coast and countryside as a timeless Arcadia while simultaneously celebrating the orderly city as the emblem of reason and progress. Art was understood as artifice, a refinement of reality rather than its replication; any subject, from a nude bather to a tourist guide cover, could be elevated through formal beauty. This double allegiance — to classical serenity and to modern, even industrial, civility — is the key to reading the style visually.

## Visual Identity

### Colour palette

The palette rule is low chroma, earth-first, sun-softened. Grounds and backgrounds sit in ochre and gold earth (roughly #C8A24B down to #8A6D2F) or in cream and beige paper and plaster (#EFE6D0, #D8C9A9). Architectural mass and shadow run through warm stone greys (#A79C8C) and deep warm browns (#5C4632). Sky, sea and shade supply soft, greyed blues — a pale washed #A9C0D2 down to a slate #5E7E93 — never a saturated azure. Vegetation is olive and sage (#7D8F69, #55684A). The warm accents are muted brick and terracotta red (#A34A32, #8E3B2C) and dusty rose (#C68A7A), used sparingly for drapery, lips, roses and heraldry. Whites read as cream or plaster white, never optical white; blacks appear only as outline and lettering ink, never as a dominant field. Saturation stays subdued across the board, as if every hue were mixed with earth or lime.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Classical architectural forms**: Greek columns, round arches, Roman walls, balustrades, urns, coffered or skylit halls — the movement's first named motif and the backbone of its architecture (Goday, Masó) and graphic design.
2. **Mediterranean landscape**: the Catalan coast, sea horizons, terraced hills and pine-dotted valleys, idealized and serene.
3. **Idealized rural life and the nude**: pastoral figures, peasants, horses and shepherds, and above all the idealized female nude placed calmly in landscape (Sunyer, Togores), treated as sculptural volume rather than erotic anecdote.
4. **Order itself as motif**: axial symmetry, stable horizons, parterre gardens, measured facades — balance is a subject, not just a method.
5. **Classical ornament in restraint**: rose garlands and swags, wreaths, heraldic shields, tiled floors; ornament is permitted but must be classical, geometric or floral-classical, never organic whiplash.
6. **The orderly city**: civic interiors, galleries, apartment facades with rhythmic balconies, and celebratory city-guide posters that marry column and garland to lettering and map.
7. **Sculptural monumentality**: smooth, simplified, Maillol-like bodies and the revival of monumental statuary; figures carry the weight and calm of carved stone.

### Materials and textures

Matte oil paint on visible canvas weave, with smooth blending and little impasto; gouache and watercolour washes on paper for pastoral scenes; lithographic poster stock with flat colour planes, fine outline work and printed grain; in architecture and interiors, smooth stucco, cut stone, brick, balustered terraces and glazed tile. Decorative arts show formal purity and minimal ornament, accepting industrial and mass-produced means — surfaces look designed, finished, and quietly manufactured rather than hand-wrought and irregular.

### Recurring subjects

Frontal or profile portraits with calm, level gazes and simplified, almost archaic features; nudes and draped figures resting in landscape; pastoral groups with animals; architectural vistas, facades and courtyards; skylit civic interiors; geometric garden parterres; coastlines framed by classical follies; posters, guides and book covers with lettering, coats of arms and garlands; standalone sculpture.

### Composition and lighting

Balance precedes everything: axial symmetry or carefully weighted equilibrium, a stable horizon, figures seated on clear verticals, views framed through arches and colonnades, interiors in measured one-point perspective under skylights. Graphic work may cross a strong vertical axis (a column, a banner of type) with a garland diagonal, but the whole remains poised. Space is often slightly flattened and decorative — a portrait head against a patterned ground of faint wreaths is entirely idiomatic. Light is diffuse and even: southern midday softness outdoors, short gentle shadows, a cool even glow in skylit halls, flat diagrammatic light in posters. There is no tenebrism, no storm light, no raking drama.

## Mood & Values

Serenity, civility, moderation, intellectual calm. The style expresses collective progress and civic pride over bohemian self-expression; timelessness over immediacy; harmony between tradition and modernity. Its figures contemplate rather than emote; its landscapes invite settlement rather than awe. Underneath the quiet lies confidence — the assurance of a culture that believes itself heir to Greece and Rome and builder of a modern, cultured civil society.

## Era & Origins

Catalonia, Spain, c. 1906–1923: bookended by the formation of Solidaritat Catalana and Carner's poetry collection *Els fruits saborosos* on one side, and Primo de Rivera's dictatorship on the other. Instititionally backed through the Mancomunitat under Enric Prat de la Riba, the movement invested in education, infrastructure and culture. It reacted against Modernisme and Romanticism — their emotion, rule-breaking and medievalism — and was in turn broken with by the interwar avant-gardes (Cubism, Futurism, Dadaism, Surrealism), which rejected its bourgeois order.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** Compose with order first: choose an axis, balance the masses, steady the horizon. Keep chroma low and earth-dominated; let ochre, cream and greyed blue carry the image, with terracotta or rose as a controlled accent. Speak the classical vernacular — column, arch, balustrade, urn, garland, parterre — even in modern settings. Light everything diffusely, as under a mild Mediterranean midday or a skylit hall. Model bodies and objects with smooth sculptural volume, simplifying detail toward monumentality. Treat the scene as artifice: arranged, purified, slightly formalized, never snapshot-casual. When designing graphics, use early-twentieth-century serif or structured display lettering, heraldry and garlands on cream stock. Allow modernity to coexist with classicism: a mass-produced object, a tiled floor, a city map or a functional facade is authentically Noucentista.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across portrait, nude-in-landscape, pastoral with animals, city facade or courtyard, civic interior, garden parterre, coastal view with classical architecture, sculpture, or poster/graphic design. Composition may range from centered frontal portrait to axial architectural vista, banded horizontal landscape, one-point interior perspective, or poster layout with vertical type axis. Lighting may range from open-air diffuse sun to skylit interior glow to flat graphic light. Colour handling may range from pale watercolour wash to denser ochre oil to flat lithographic planes, provided chroma stays muted and earth-anchored. Mood may range from contemplative stillness to pastoral idyll to civic celebration. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Whiplash Art Nouveau curves, asymmetrical florid ornament, iridescent or symbolist dark palettes and medieval/Gothic motifs (these are Modernisme). Art Deco chevrons, sunbursts, chrome, lacquer black, jazz-age glamour and machine-speed iconography. Dramatic chiaroscuro, tenebrism, stormy Romantic skies and sublime untamed nature. Avant-garde fragmentation: Cubist facets, Futurist speed lines, Dada collage, Surrealist dream illogic. High-saturation primaries, neon, glossy digital finish, or heavy gestural impasto. Marble-bright Hellenic starkness with pure white and saturated Aegean blue. Cluttered, casually cropped, snapshot realism; sentimental Victorian anecdote; ruinous picturesque decay.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Art Deco**: Deco is machine-age luxury — gloss, chrome, black-and-gold contrast, zigzags, sunbursts, streamlined speed. Noucentisme is matte, earthy, pre-war calm; its geometry is the column and the arch, not the chevron, and its mood is civic-pastoral, not nightlife.
- **Classicism (general)**: Classicism is any era's revival of Greco-Roman ideals. Noucentisme is specifically early-twentieth-century Catalan: muted earth palette, regional Mediterranean landscape, and classical form fused with modern industrial and civic elements rather than pure antiquarian revival.
- **Hellenic**: Hellenic imagery is marble-bright — stark white, saturated Aegean blue, archaeological or fashion framing. Noucentisme is softer and warmer: ochre, cream, greyed blue, brick and olive, painterly matte surfaces, and Roman/Renaissance and Catalan vernacular mixed with the Greek.
- **Modernisme**: The direct opponent. Modernisme gives whiplash lines, asymmetry, florid organic ornament, Gothic and medieval references, emotional intensity and dark iridescent colour. Noucentisme answers with straight axes, symmetry, restraint, daylight clarity and emotional moderation.
- **Neoclassicism**: Neoclassicism (eighteenth–nineteenth century) is academic, polished, often grand historical narrative in cool grey-white tones. Noucentisme is simpler in volume (Maillol-like mass over anatomical display), warmer and earthier in palette, regional in subject, and comfortable with posters, mass production and functional modern design.

**Evidence note.** The movement's music, theatre and philosophy strands yield little visual guidance and are not encoded here; sculpture is documented mainly by names and the descriptors "classical and naturalistic" plus the revival of monumental statuary, so read it as smooth volumetric calm rather than as any specific work. The decorative-arts record in visual evidence centers on posters and civic interiors, consistent with the text's claim of formal purity with minimal ornament.
