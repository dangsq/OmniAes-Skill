# Spy Fiction (Spycore)

Spy Fiction is the visual culture of espionage: the covert gathering of information for a third party, performed by agents who answer to an organization. Its imaginary runs on a spectrum from fantastical, even campy glamour — highly trained agents in exotic luxurious locales, hindered by sophisticated technology and seductive characters — to dark, gritty field realism. What holds both ends together is a set of constants: escape from danger, action, disguise, and an invisible chain of command. Visually, the style oscillates between two poles: the glamour brochure (cocktail lit interiors, chrome, ballgowns, private jets) and the evidence table (kit laid out in low light, passports, cash, cartridges, a lens sewn into lingerie). Between them sit the paper registers — instructional and propaganda typography about disappearing, new identities, and silence — and a grainy monochrome archive strand of training photographs. The mood is competence under paranoia: everything ordinary may be apparatus, everyone poised may be running.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is a black-dominant field punctured by metal and by one alert colour. Black carries the clothing and the night; silver and steel carry the hardware; red never fields, it signals — poster type, a stamp, a lipstick, a laser dot, a warning line. A secondary field register admits militaresque neutrals (tan, gray, brown, navy, green) worn for durability and concealment, with camouflage appearing only as concealment, never as uniform. Paper cream supports the document and poster strand.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Concealed weaponry and dual-purpose gadgets.** Concealed-carry guns, clandestine weaponry, poisons and chemicals, gadgets with a second life — a pen with a camera, an umbrella that is not an umbrella, a micro-camera and wire hidden in a lace bra on a museum mannequin. The concealed layer is the style's core gesture.
2. **Papers of identity and instruction.** Sheets of plans and maps, coded messages, passports, stacks of banknotes, and bold instructional or warning typography: imperatives about disappearing completely, creating a new identity, seeing everything, hearing everything, saying nothing.
3. **Disguise wardrobe.** The trench coat and fedora as the symbolic spy disguise; the 1960s film grammar of black turtlenecks and tan trench coats; for infiltration of balls and events, white- or black-tie tuxedos, long dramatic ballgowns, heels, dress shoes, abundant jewellery.
4. **Field kit and carry.** Concealed thigh holsters for gun or knife, briefcases and duffel bags, goggles and glasses with dual purpose, hats from thick winter to small summer caps, belt kit, climbing harnesses, friction gloves, grappling hooks, ninja-style clothing for night ops, concealed tactical vests, layered blouson jackets centralizing EDC, footwear built to run, jump, climb.
5. **Locales and vehicles.** English manor houses, estates, castles, secret passageways; abandoned and condemned buildings; balls and events; sports cars and vintage cars, planes especially private jets, yachts; field work anywhere from busy city centres to arctic tundras to hot desert valleys.
6. **Glamour and threat props.** The signature drink (the martini), fire and explosions, jewellery as both adornment and cover.

### Materials and textures

Grained leather (holsters, bags, jackets), wool knit and gabardine (turtlenecks, trenches), matte paper stock and printed poster ink, brushed and blued steel, lace and satin as concealment surfaces, tinted glass lenses, canvas duffel, wire and solder where the gadget shows its seam. Surfaces read handled, packed, folded — not pristine.

### Subjects, composition, lighting

Figures appear poised rather than posed: face obscured by sunglasses, shadow, or a turned head; full-black silhouettes against landmarks or wood-panelled interiors; pairs in prone training alignments. Objects appear as evidence: top-down flat-lays of kit on dark surfaces, or isolated on neutral seamless backgrounds like museum displays. Papers appear frontally, symmetric, type-driven. Lighting splits by register:

| Register | Lighting | Composition | Typical content |
|---|---|---|---|

| Clandestine | single practical source, deep vignette, low-key | tight flat-lay, over-the-shoulder on documents | kit table, coded map, holster, cash |

| Camp / parody | deadpan flat light | instructional cover or terse meme layout | "how to" typography, absurd gadget reveals |

## Mood & Values

Secrecy as virtue and silence as safety; competence as identity. The style values the hidden layer over the display: what matters is what the bag's false bottom holds, what the lace conceals, what the coded sheet decodes. It holds duality comfortably — glamour and mundanity, loyalty and double-cross, the constructed self and the discarded self. Parody is internal to it: humorous spy fiction pushes back against Cold War fear by mocking its own seriousness, so deadpan instruction and absurd gadget reveals belong to the style, not outside it.

## Era & Origins

Literary roots reach to the 19th century and older, but the visual grammar was fixed by 1960s spy cinema — the Bond blueprint that glamorized the trade — and weighted by World War Two and Cold War anxiety. Post–Cold War revelations produced the spy-school strand; the late 20th century added a children's, softened variant. The style therefore always carries two clocks: a mid-century analogue heart (paper, film grain, steel) and a perpetual present of sunglasses and black knitwear.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. **Hide a layer.** Every image should imply concealment: an apparatus inside an ordinary object, an intent inside an ordinary outfit, an identity inside an ordinary paper.
2. **Keep the palette contract.** Black-dominant ground, silver hardware, one signal-red accent; field scenes may swap black for tan, gray, navy, olive, but never for saturated colour.
3. **Speak in tradecraft nouns.** Holster, passport, cartridge, micro-camera, dossier, grappling hook, martini glass — concrete objects carry the style more than adjectives do.
4. **Pick one register and hold its physics.** Glamour, clandestine, archival, poster, monochrome-archive, or camp: lighting, grain and material finish must agree throughout the frame.
5. **Obscure the face or the intent.** Sunglasses, shadow, turned heads, hands mid-task; the agent is a function, not a portrait.
6. **Type, if present, is imperative.** Condensed grotesque or propaganda serif, all caps, verbs of disappearance, identity, silence.

Variation axes: subject (gadget, document, vehicle, locale, outfit, event, field operation, training snapshot, poster); era handle (1940s monochrome through 1960s colour glamour to contemporary digital snapshot); register from camp to gritty; location from ballroom to tundra to desert valley to condemned building; composition from flat-lay to wide establishing; lighting from flat scan light to single-bulb chiaroscuro to glossy key. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. Note honestly: vehicles, yachts, explosions and manor interiors rest on textual authority and appear rarely or not at all in surviving image samples, while the sample over-represents tradecraft objects and identity papers — treat motif ranking, not sample frequency, as the guide.

Avoid: openly slung rifles, plate carriers, helmet cams and uniformed formations (the spy conceals; the operator displays); camouflage worn as uniform rather than concealment; candy-bright or pastel saturation and rainbow clutter; neon holograms, HUD overlays and sci-fi interfaces; rain-slick city night with venetian-blind shadows as the entire subject; tweed, pipes and armchair deduction tableaus; satin-and-smoke seduction with no tradecraft object anywhere in frame; handwritten script or whimsical illustration; cosy domesticity with no hidden layer; explicit gore or wounds — threat arrives as objects, not injuries.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Film Noir** borrows spy fiction's chiaroscuro but stays married to the city: rain-slick streets, night offices, moral ambiguity without an institution behind it. Spy fiction adds the object-language of tradecraft (gadgets, dossiers, passports), an organization giving orders, and globe-trotting locales — manors, tundras, jets — that noir never visits.
- **Femme Fatale** centres the seductive woman as the weapon and the compositional anchor: silk, smoke, direct gaze. Spy fiction includes seductive characters and ballgowns, but the anchor is the mission apparatus; a figure in black with sunglasses reads spy when a bag with a false bottom or a dossier enters the frame, fatale when the gaze itself is the only hardware.
- **Golden Age Detective Fiction** shares the English manor but looks backward: clues laid out for deduction, drawing rooms, tweed and tea, puzzle fairness. Spy fiction looks forward — prevention, intrusion, escape — and fills the same castle with secret passageways, holsters and technology the drawing-room mystery deliberately excludes.
