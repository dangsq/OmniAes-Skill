# Analog Horror

Analog Horror (also called VHS Horror) is a subgenre of found-footage horror that emerged on the internet in the mid-to-late 2010s and builds its entire visual language out of *mediation*: the horror never arrives directly, it arrives through a degraded analog artifact — a taped-over VHS, a hijacked television broadcast, an emergency alert, a public-access ident, an employee training reel. The style corrupts the familiar, trustworthy media formats of the late 20th century (roughly the mid-1980s to mid-2000s video era, occasionally reaching back to 1960s film grain) by inserting unsettling content into their bureaucratic grammar. It was popularized and codified by Kris Straub's *Local 58 TV*, with *Gemini Home Entertainment* and *The Mandela Catalogue* further fixing its conventions, and it descends from earlier online horror such as the creepypasta "Candle Cove," "The Wyoming Incident," the ARG *Marble Hornets*, and pre-2007 Emergency Broadcast System scenario videos. Because the genre deliberately lacks visible protagonists or camera operators, its images read as frames of "official" media rather than as photographs of events: title cards, alert screens, vanity plates, and static-laden close-ups of faces or objects standing alone in the dark.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette rule is *black field first, signal color second, scene color last*. Most frames sit on pure or near-pure black (#000000–#0a0a0a), because the diegetic surface is a television screen, a tape freeze-frame, or a void around an isolated subject. Image content inside that field is monochrome or heavily desaturated: grainy greys (#6b6b6b–#c9c9c9) for black-and-white public-access and assessment footage, muddy drained tones (#4a4f45, #6e6a5e, #8a8578) for taped interiors and tunnels, and occasional sepia/amber casts (#b8a078, #8a7a5c) when the emulation reaches back to older film. Saturated color is allowed only as *broadcast signal color*, never as naturalistic scene lighting: alert red (#c1272d–#e03a3e) for warning triangles, pictograms and bloodless stains; phosphor green (#33ff66, #7fdc50) for terminal text, radar spirals and instructional cards; deep CRT blue (#0d1b4b–#1a2a9a) for broadcast fields and station idents. Text permits plain backgrounds of any single color — magenta, purple and red gradient cards all occur — as long as the layout stays flat and bureaucratic. Tracking errors and chromatic aberration add thin cyan (#00ffff) and magenta (#ff00ff) fringes along high-contrast edges. A rare warm outlier exists: silhouette scenes against a red-orange dawn gradient (#7a1f1f → #d46a2a) with a burn-in morning timestamp. What unifies the range is desaturation or isolation of hue, plus the sense that color itself is slightly wrong — bleeding, shifted, or too pure for the decay around it.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the style documents them

1. **Corrupted official media and phony bureaucracy.** Emergency Alert System cards with warning triangles and monospace directives; employee training tapes; instructional pictogram panels ("STAY HOME"); fictional company vanity plates and seals; hotline numbers; centered serif or sans proclamations ("THERE IS NOTHING TO FEAR"). The authority voice is present, legible, and subtly or overtly wrong.
2. **Analog degradation as substrate.** VHS grain, tape softness and color bleed, interlaced scanlines, static and snow fields, tracking-error tear bands, head-switching noise at the frame bottom, dithered or halftoned black-and-white noise, CRT curvature and phosphor bloom.
3. **Burn-in broadcast metadata.** Monospace or pixel timestamps and dates ("SEP 15 92," "1/16/1999"), clock readouts confined to late night (12 AM–5 AM), play/SP mode icons, channel bugs and station logos ("local 58," "CIVIC TV Channel 83 Cable 12"), program schedules, "WE'LL BE RIGHT BACK" and "THE END" cards.
4. **Facial horror.** Distorted, warped, blackened or hollowed faces; unnaturally wide grins; eyes enlarged or replaced by voids; heavy shadow with harsh sharpening and over-detailed features; faces isolated on black, often frontally centered like a mugshot or test pattern.
5. **Ominous glitch as narrative signal.** Chromatic aberration splits, frame jumps, torn and displaced scanline bands, posterized color breaks — always reading as an interruption *of* a coherent transmission, never as free abstraction.
6. **Interrupted nostalgia objects.** Children's-show mascots and cartoon logos (smiling rabbit, apple-farm sprite), pixelated game characters, muzak-scored commercial graphics — comforting retaux media rendered uncanny by wrong proportions, dead eyes, darkened surroundings.
7. **Sinister space imagery.** Planets, moons and orbital bodies depicted in ominous light, often as vanity-plate globes with gradient shading and serif corporate logotype.
8. **Empty or unpeopled found-footage framing.** Tunnels, stairwells, stone corridors, domestic rooms on tape; when a human figure appears it is passive, distant, or the thing being examined — never a hero holding a camera.
9. **Hidden messages.** Subliminal text frames, confidential informant cards, reversed or obscured type that invites frame-by-frame reading.

### Materials and textures

Magnetic tape (soft focus, smeared chroma, noise floor), cathode-ray tube glass (scanlines, glow, vignette, slight barrel distortion), analog broadcast noise and snow, film grain and print halftone for older-stratum frames, phosphor terminal glow, pixel sprites from low-resolution game or sprite-based media, and paper-like institutional cards with seals and stamps. Everything should look *copied*, at least one generation removed from the original signal.

### Composition and lighting

Two compositional families dominate. First, the **flat broadcast graphic**: symmetrical, frontal, centered text or emblem on a plain field, exactly like real EAS cards, idents and vanity plates — deliberate mediocrity of design is the point. Second, the **found-footage frame**: fixed or surveillance-like camera, unconventional or awkward framing, broken 180° axis, sudden scale jumps, extreme close-ups of a CRT surface or a face filling the frame, subjects isolated in a black void with heavy vignetting. Lighting is low-key and source-poor: a single practical or the screen's own glow, silhouette against a gradient sky, chiaroscuro on faces with crushed blacks and over-sharpened highlights, or the perfectly even, shadowless light of an instructional graphic. Depth is shallow or absent; space feels sealed.

## Mood & Values

The emotional core is psychological dread and uncanniness rather than gore: the poisoning of familiarity. Analog Horror values the unsettling of trusted systems — public broadcasts, instruction videos, government alerts — portraying them as hijacked by incomprehensible forces, which produces powerlessness and a depersonalized, unbuffered encounter with the threat because no protagonist stands between viewer and screen. It questions authority and expresses fear of malfunctioning technology as a conduit for malevolent entities, alongside cosmic horror's vast, unknowable threats. Beneath the dread runs poisoned nostalgia: the warm memory of late-night television, mascots and muzak, deliberately curdled. Loneliness is constant — tape hiss, static and empty rooms — punctuated by sudden jarring intrusion into stillness.

## Era & Origins

Emergent mid-to-late 2010s on YouTube and adjacent internet spaces, codified by Kris Straub's *Local 58 TV* and reinforced by *Gemini Home Entertainment*, *The Mandela Catalogue*, *The Walten Files*, *Worldcorp Enterprises*, *Vita Carnis*, *BLUE_CHANNEL_THALASIN*, game works like *Amanda the Adventurer* and *Andy's Apple Farm*, and cinematic relatives from *Videodrome* (1983) to *Skinamarink* (2022). Its roots reach back to "Candle Cove" (2009), "The Wyoming Incident," *Marble Hornets*, and 2007-era EAS scenario videos. The *depicted* era is always earlier than the *making* era: emulation targets mid-1980s to mid-2000s video technology, sometimes 1960s film, and timestamps should fall inside that window.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Choose the medium before the subject: decide which analog artifact this frame belongs to (broadcast card, alert screen, tape freeze-frame, public-access ident, training reel, surveillance copy) and let its layout conventions dictate everything.
- Degrade consistently and physically: grain, scanlines, chroma bleed, tracking tear, vignette, soft focus — artifacts should obey one plausible signal chain, not stack randomly.
- Let text carry the horror: period-correct monospace, pixel or serif type, centered bureaucratic copy, seals, hotlines, timestamps; the wrongness can live entirely in a single sentence.
- Corrupt exactly one familiar thing per frame — a grin too wide, a mascot in the dark, an alert that instructs something impossible — and keep the threat ambiguous and off-center.
- Keep humans absent or passive; the viewer is the witness, never a character.
- Anchor time: late-night clocks, 1980s–2000s dates, schedule grids, station logos.
- Restrain color: black field, desaturated content, saturation reserved for signal red / phosphor green / CRT blue or a single plain card background.

**Variation axes.** A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.
- *Subject*: alert and instructional cards, vanity plates and seals, station idents and schedules, distorted faces, uncanny mascots or pixel sprites, cryptid-like creatures glimpsed on tape, sinister planets, empty interiors, tunnels and liminal corridors, silhouetted figures at dawn, terminal/radar screens, memorial or proclamation cards.
- *Composition*: symmetrical flat graphic; awkward fixed found-footage framing; extreme CRT or face close-up; subject centered in void; grid of degraded stills; silhouette horizon line.
- *Lighting*: screen-self-emission in darkness; single practical; flat shadowless graphic light; silhouette against gradient; harsh sharpened chiaroscuro.
- *Color handling*: full monochrome; desaturated mute; deep blue broadcast field; any single plain card color with sparse accents; sepia film stratum; chromatic-fringe edges; rare dawn gradient.
- *Era filter*: 1980s–90s VHS; early-2000s consumer video; 1960s grainy film; black-and-white public access.
- *Mood*: quiet bureaucratic coldness, creeping dread, abrupt intrusion, curdled childhood nostalgia, cosmic insignificance.

**Avoid.**
- Clean digital sharpness: crisp edges, DSLR bokeh, modern flat UI, streaming or smartphone interfaces, contemporary web typography and gradients.
- Explicit gore, splatter, weapons, monster-action poses; the style implies rather than shows.
- Glitch used as free decorative abstraction (pixel sorting, datamosh collages) with no diegetic broadcast document underneath.
- Neon-saturated cyberpunk or retrowave palettes; comforting, uncorrupted nostalgia (pure vaporwave coziness).
- Visible protagonists, camera operators, hero framing, or explanatory narrative clarity.
- Gothic or supernatural-horror dressing: candles, fog, Victorian interiors, dramatic rim-light monsters.
- Timestamps, logos or typefaces from after the mid-2000s; modern corporate design language.
- Pristine, noise-free blacks; every frame must bear at least one generation of analog copy decay.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Digital Horror** shares the corrupted-media concept but its artifacts are native digital: broken file icons, OS error dialogs, compression mush, deep-web interfaces, pixel-sorted screens. Analog Horror's decay is specifically electromagnetic — scanlines, tracking tear, tape hiss made visible, CRT glow — and its typography and layouts belong to broadcast bureaucracy, not operating systems.
- **Glitch Art** makes disruption the subject: colorful, abstract, celebratory datamosh and pixel sorting with no narrative frame. In Analog Horror the glitch is a symptom inside an otherwise legible document (an alert card, a face, a ident); remove the diegetic media layer and you have left the style.
- **Horror** at large needs no mediation: gore, monsters, haunted spaces photographed or painted directly, with protagonists and dramatic lighting. Analog Horror always inserts a degraded medium and an institutional voice between viewer and threat, and prefers dread to violence.
- **Signalwave** uses the same broadcast grammar — idents, blue gradients, muzak mood, looping station logos — but purely nostalgically, clean and unthreatening. Analog Horror is Signalwave's vocabulary turned hostile: the ident is interrupted, the schedule lies, the smile is wrong.
- **Cryptidcore** is creature-evidence photography: grainy handheld shots of beings in forests or roads, valued as specimens, usually without text bureaucracy. Analog Horror may show creatures, but frames them as tape or broadcast content with OSD burn-in, alert captions and institutional context; the creature is an intrusion into media, not a trophy in nature.
