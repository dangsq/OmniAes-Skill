# Realism

Realism is the art of the verifiable world. Born in France in the 1840s in the wake of the 1848 Revolution, it declared — with Courbet — that one cannot paint an angel having never seen one, and turned the machinery of grand-manner painting onto gleaners, stone-breakers, lace-makers, sailors, smiths, chess players and provincial parlors. It inherits its craft from the mimetic tradition of the Dutch and Spanish Golden Ages (dark grounds, a white tablecloth as a stage, bread and ceramic rendered as fact), and it hands its commitment to direct observation forward to Impressionism. What defines it is not technique alone but a moral position: truth and accuracy over beauty, the contemporary over the historical, the observed over the imagined.

## Visual Identity

**Palette.** A low-to-medium saturation earth band, wide in value but narrow in hue. Deep warm grounds and shadows sit near `#1C1712`–`#33291E`; umber and bark browns occupy `#4E3B27`–`#7A5C3E`; straw, ochre and worn wood run `#9C7A44`–`#C9A66B`; olive, moss and stubble greens `#4C5237`–`#77804F`; stone, putty and plaster greys `#6E675C`–`#A59C8F`; and linen, canvas and cloth whites `#DED5C2`–`#F1EBDD`, which carry the light wherever they appear. Accents are small and mineral or vegetable: brick and madder reds `#7E3527`–`#9C4A32`, dull slate blues `#4A5560`–`#6E7B85`. The rule is faithful local color under natural light: no hue may appear that the scene could not physically produce, and no color may be used for mere drama. Within this band the key may range from near-monochrome umber interiors to pale cream-and-green plein-air daylight; both extremes belong to the style.

**Motifs, ranked by textual prominence.** First, laboring bodies and hands: bent gleaners, kneeling stone-breakers, seated lace-makers, sailors hauling rope, smiths at the forge — figures absorbed in task, faces in profile, averted or shadowed, never posing. Second, scenes of everyday life: the meal table with its loaf, jug, glass of wine and knife; the chess game; the nursery in a public park; the charity visit in a bare parlor. Third, unromanticized land: stubble fields, mud roads, mossy forest floors with buttressed trunks and fallen timber, mist between vertical stems. Fourth, social commentary: institutional interiors, worn clothing against fine black cloth, the contrast of classes in one room. Fifth, the unidealized nude and the provincial bourgeoisie as portrait subjects — documented by the text as core motifs, though thinly represented in surviving visual samples. Sixth, work-clothes as still-life: coarse linen shirts with rolled sleeves, straw hats, wooden clogs, aprons, kerchiefs.

**Materials and surface.** Oil on canvas, matte to satin. Paint is loaded where light lands — impasto in cloth whites, straw, bread crust, foam and moss — and scumbled thin in mist and shadow. Contours of figures and objects stay firm and drawn; peripheral edges dissolve into warm dark. Glazed shadows keep their transparency; highlights stay opaque and chalky. The hand is visible but disciplined: description, not performance.

**Light.** Natural light treated as a fact of weather and hour: diffuse overcast sky, north window light, a single oil lamp, ember-glow from a forge, or soft open-air daylight in a park. One dominant source, directional but never theatrical; shadows opaque and warm, never violet or arbitrary. The white tablecloth, the white shirt, the white dress are the style's light-meters.

**Composition.** Ordinary subjects granted monumental scale and horizontal, frieze-like arrangements. Figures distributed by the logic of the task, not by academic symmetry; no central hero, no pyramidal apotheosis. The table functions as a stage holding a still life inside the genre scene. Landscapes either open and low-horizoned (harvest field) or enclosed and vertical (forest interior). Cropping feels witnessed but arranged — composed, never snapped.

## Mood & Values

Sober, patient, dignified. Fatigue is shown without pity, poverty without melodrama, kindness without sentiment. The prevailing feeling is the weight and rhythm of work and the quiet of uneventful hours. Underlying values are truth and accuracy, social consciousness, and the democratic claim that a peasant's day deserves the canvas size once reserved for saints and generals. Emotion is suppressed on the surface and accumulates underneath.

## Era & Origins

France, 1840s, post-1848; a reaction against Neoclassical idealization and Romantic emotionalism, and a challenge to the Academy's hierarchy of genres. Overlaps the Barbizon School in its open-air truthfulness toward forest and field; inherits tenebrist description from 17th-century Spanish and Dutch painting; spreads through the later 19th century into Russian, Finnish, British and Italian variants that keep the earth palette and the labor subject while localizing the costumes and light. Lays the groundwork for Impressionism by insisting on painting only the contemporary, observed world.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Paint only what could be stood in front of: no angels, no allegory, no myth.
2. Give the mundane subject serious scale, finish and attention.
3. Keep every hue inside the earth-and-mineral band; let linen whites carry the light.
4. Make light a fact of weather, hour and room — one source, honest shadows.
5. Let hands, posture and worn clothing narrate; keep faces absorbed, averted or in shadow.
6. Model solid mass under a matte oil surface; visible brushwork serves description.
7. Arrange, don't snapshot: asymmetry allowed, but every placement deliberate.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any contemporary ordinary scene — field harvest, port deck, forge, workshop, lace-room, domestic interior, café or chess table, charity parlor, park nursery, forest interior, shoreline, plain nude in a plain room, group of workers at rest. Composition: labor frieze, single absorbed figure, crowded interior with doorway light, open low-horizon field, enclosed vertical forest, table-piece with integrated still life. Lighting: overcast diffuse; single window or lamp; bright open-air daylight at the pale end of the range; forge or lamplight glow at the dark end. Color handling: from near-monochrome umber to cream-and-green daylight, with small muted red or slate accents. Mood: quiet fatigue, documentary calm, unsentimental tenderness, dry social irony. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Mythological or religious allegory and apotheoses; Romantic storm light, sublime sunsets, gloomy ruins; prettified peasants in costume-pageant dress; idealized musculature and heroic poses; sentimental gloss (rosy cheeks, pleading eyes); saturated arbitrary color — neon, candy tones, turquoise skies; broken optical brushwork and colored shadows; lens behavior — uniform sharp focus, bokeh, snapshot cropping, chromatic fringing; poster slogans, heroic worker silhouettes, graphic reds and blacks; anecdotal folkloric charm and picturesque local "types"; cozy candlelit domestic preciousness at small scale; airbrushed digital smoothness; academic symmetry and central-hero staging.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Costumbrismo** records local customs and costume with anecdotal, picturesque charm; Realism records labor and social fact with unsentimental weight. Work-clothes versus festival clothes; critique versus charm.
- **Impressionism** dissolves form into broken strokes and optical color, making light itself the subject and favoring leisure; Realism keeps drawn contour, modeled mass, earth pigment and working bodies.
- **Photorealism** reproduces lens behavior — even focus, depth-of-field blur, snapshot framing, hyperfinish; Realism is painterly, composed, and materially oil: glazed shadow, impasto highlight, visible hand.
- **Social Realism** (20th century) argues a political case with heroic or accusatory workers and poster clarity; Realism observes without slogan, granting dignity without heroization, in a somber rather than graphic palette.
- **Danish Golden Age** offers small, precise, candle-warm bourgeois interiors and domestic contentment; Realism ranges wider — fields, ports, forges, institutions — at monumental scale, with heavier earth tones and social scrutiny instead of hygge.
- **Barbizon School**, the closest overlap, is lyrical tonal landscape; Realism adds the figure as protagonist and the social stake.

Where evidence is thin, say so: the unidealized nude and the provincial-bourgeois portrait are textually core but scarcely sampled visually, and Daumier's lithographic looseness and Manet's flattened planes mark the style's loose and modern edges — legitimate extremes of its variation space, not deviations from it.
