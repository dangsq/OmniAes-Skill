# Raygun Gothic

## Overview

Raygun Gothic is the future as imagined from inside the American mid-century: a view of tomorrow painted between roughly 1945 and 1970, in the visual language of pulp science-fiction covers, comic books, theme-park brochures, diner signage and appliance advertising. It imagines a brightly coloured space-age utopia where atomic energy raises the standard of living, hover craft commute over Googie rooftops, spacesuits set fashion trends, and intergalactic adventure is a family pastime. The aesthetic runs on an intensity slider: at one end it is a merely stylized, slightly sci-fi mid-century suburbia; at the other, a full Tomorrowland of glowing moon cities and alien markets. It also runs under the names Populuxe and, in its architectural register, Googie. The term itself was coined retrospectively in 1981 by William Gibson in "The Gernsback Continuum" — "the tomorrow that never was" — which is exactly the mood to hold: a future that never arrived, preserved forever at peak promise.

## Visual Identity

### Colour palette

The style operates in three overlapping colour regimes, and a given image may sit in any one of them or blend two:

| Regime | Typical home | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Comic primaries | panels, cartoon posters, pulp covers | enamel red (#C8322B–#E4572E), atomic yellow (#F5C542–#FFD23F), sky and cobalt blue (#2E86AB–#3FA7D6), white smoke puffs, ink-black outlines |
| Populuxe pastels | suburbia, diners, postcards, Googie architecture | mint (#BFE3D6), turquoise (#4ECDC4), powder pink (#E8A7B8), lilac (#C9A7D8), butter cream (#F4E9D8–#F7E6A6) |
| Pulp painterly night | cover illustrations, city vistas | deep space blue (#16324F–#1B2A4A), teal shadow (#2E6E7E), hot orange (#F2762E–#FF8C42), magenta (#D64B8A), chrome silver (#C0C4C8–#E8ECEF) |

Constants across all regimes: polished chrome and silver highlights; neon glow accents (electric turquoise near #39FFF0, neon pink near #FF6EC7); cream or parchment paper grounds for poster, postcard and comic formats. The governing rule is saturation and clarity: colours stay candy-bright or jewel-bright, value contrast stays high, and black appears either as ink line-work or as the void of space — never as grime, never as mood. Muddy earth tones, desaturated grunge and matte charcoal fields break the style.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the style documents them

1. **Space travel and spacesuits** — finned rockets, disc-shaped saucers, ringed orbital stations, needle-prowed cruisers, bubble canopies, and skintight suits with globes, visors and chest consoles.
2. **Atomic-age graphics** — starbursts, orbiting-electron atom models, radar dishes, whip antennae, reactor domes and cooling towers treated as friendly civic icons.
3. **Chrome and grille language** — polished metal bodies, grille and grate patterns, stylized struts and support beams, chrome trim applied indiscriminately to rockets, cars and refrigerators alike.
4. **Aircraft-like fins and streamlining** — tailfins on everything that moves, curved noses, swept wings, teardrop cockpits.
5. **Hover technology** — craft floating without visible thrust, saucers tilted over plazas, vehicles parked in mid-air.
6. **Robots and automated appliances** — helpful humanoid robots, walking machines on spindly legs or treads, domestic devices that cook, clean and converse.
7. **Fantastical planets and aliens** — worlds ranging from monochrome moonscapes to outrageously candy-coloured terrains with crystal spires and double moons; aliens as brightly skinned mashups of Earth organisms, by turns mindless monsters to overcome and friendly altruists.
8. **Mid-century Americana** — diners, suburban streets, the nuclear family, theme-park pavilions and crowds, postcard greetings from atomic cities.
9. **Energy effects** — laser and energy beams, exhaust glow, neon tubing, light trails arcing across night skies.
10. **Geometric shapes and period graphics** — boomerangs, ovals, triangles, condensed masthead lettering, hand-lettered caption boxes, decorative poster borders.

### Materials and textures

Chrome and polished aluminium; bubble glass canopies; enamel-painted steel in candy colours; latex and smooth synthetic suit materials; neon glass; moulded plastic; lithographic paper grain. Painterly executions show gouache-like brushwork and soft airbrush gradients; comic executions show flat ink colour, crisp outlines and simple halftone shading. Surfaces are showroom-clean: waxed, gleaming, freshly unwrapped. Weathering, rust, dust and patina are foreign to the style.

### Recurring subjects

Vehicles above all — rockets, saucers, monorails, hover buses, finned cars, gyroscopic stations. Architecture as set-piece: Googie coffee shops, domed cities, moon colonies, theme-park plazas with spires and flying saucers overhead. People: square-jawed spacemen heroes, pinup-adjacent space-vixen fashion (skirts, boots, gloves, skintight suits; image evidence for fashion is thin — essentially sculpture and cover figures — but the textual record is explicit), commuting families, crowds marvelling upward. Robots and appliance characters. Aliens and alien flora. And format-subjects: posters, postcards, album covers, comic panels, magazine spreads, museum toy models.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Compositions favour heroic low-angle diagonal launches; cockpit close-ups framed inside a bubble canopy; wide plaza vistas where tiny crowds and layered flying craft build depth; vertical poster layouts with masthead lettering and taglines; postcard layouts with greeting scripts; comic panels with caption boxes; and neutral-ground product shots of model craft. Lighting comes from inside the world: glowing cockpits, neon signs, exhaust plumes, energy beams, starfields with multiple moons. Daylight scenes run high-key and pastel. Shadows are crisp and simple; chiaroscuro and moody noir lighting do not belong.

## Mood & Values

Endless technological advancement; abstracted architecture; pulp comic vibes; optimistic views. Heroism, discovery, the nuclear family and the suburban lifestyle are celebrated without irony. The atom is a friend. Technology solves problems. The mood is whimsical hope — an escape from the cynicism of the later twentieth century into a wonderland that never happened and never stops being promised.

## Era & Origins

United States, emerging in the late 1940s after the collapse of Dieselpunk, with a viewpoint window of circa 1945–1970. Visual roots lie in 1930s–40s pulp magazine covers and comics — Flash Gordon, Buck Rogers, and the painted covers of artists like Frank R. Paul — and in the real Space Age bookended by the 1946 V-2 launch and the 1972 Moon landing. Popularizers include Alex Raymond, Hugo Gernsback, Ray Bradbury, Arthur Radebaugh, and Walt Disney's Tomorrowland. The label arrived late, coined by Gibson in 1981; originally attached to Streamline Moderne, it now binds to Googie. Revivals run through The Jetsons, Colonel Bleep, Meet the Robinsons, Buzz Lightyear of Star Command, My Life as a Teenage Robot and Atomic Betty.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. **Optimism is the load-bearing wall.** Every element should read as a promise, not a warning.
2. **Chrome, curve, fin.** Machines are streamlined bodies with tailfins, bubble glass and grille details; nothing boxy, nothing brutalist.
3. **Pick a colour regime deliberately** — comic primaries, populuxe pastels, or pulp painterly night — and keep saturation high throughout.
4. **Glow as punctuation.** Neon, exhaust, beams and starbursts mark the futuristic accents.
5. **Speak period graphics.** Condensed mastheads, starburst logos, caption boxes and postcard greetings carry the voice.
6. **Keep surfaces new.** Wax, polish, enamel; no decay.

Variation axes: subject matter (rockets, saucers, stations, robots, appliances, diners in space, suburban hover-commutes, alien markets, spacesuit fashion, theme parks, postcards, album covers); composition (cockpit close-up, product shot, wide vista, poster vertical, comic panel, crowd scene); lighting (flat comic light, high-key pastel daylight, painterly starfield night); colour handling (any of the three regimes, blended or pure); medium (flat ink comic, gouache pulp painting, litho postcard, toy photography, sculpture, mid-century animation cel). A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A quiet pastel postcard of a hover-bus stop and a roaring pulp cover of a robot battle are equally valid Raygun Gothic.

Avoid: dystopia, cynicism, horror-gore and Cold War dread imagery (fallout anxiety belongs to Atompunk); rust, grime, weathering, decay; matte-black stealth shapes and camouflage; modern digital technology — smartphones, flat screens, LEDs, contemporary drones; beige boxy CRT computers (Cassette Futurism); brass, gears and steam (Steampunk); riveted diesel grit (Dieselpunk); rain-slick neon noir (Cyberpunk); brutalist concrete and minimalist white museum modernism; muted earth-tone palettes; post-1970 typography and sans-serif corporate minimalism.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Space Age** is the real-world historical design language of the race-to-space era: white and silver minimalism, documentary photography, actual hardware. Raygun Gothic is the illustrated, narrative, comic-saturated fantasy version with aliens, rayguns and heroics.
- **Atompunk** shares the atomic root but admits Cold War anxiety, fallout shelters, propaganda and irony, and tolerates drab olive-mustard weathering. Raygun Gothic never loses the smile; it is Atompunk's light-hearted cousin.
- **Diner** is grounded Americana — chrome counters, neon signs, checkered floors, tailfin cars at curb level, no space travel. Raygun Gothic borrows the chrome-and-neon vocabulary but launches it off-planet.
- **Googie** is built architecture: boomerang roofs, starburst signs, cantilevered coffee shops. Raygun Gothic uses Googie buildings as set dressing inside a science-fiction narrative; a coffee shop photograph alone is Googie, not Raygun Gothic.
- **Rocketpunk** is engineering-minded retrofuturism: riveted hulls, analog dials, slide-rule physics, Newtonian trajectories, often monochrome technical illustration. Raygun Gothic defies gravity with hover discs, glosses mechanics under chrome and glow, and keeps comic-book colour and pulp heroics.
