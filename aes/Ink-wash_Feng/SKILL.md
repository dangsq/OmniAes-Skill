# Ink-wash Feng (Shuimo Feng)

Ink-wash Feng is the contemporary design language that carries traditional Chinese ink painting (shuimo) into modern media. It is rooted in literati brushwork — ink gradation, xieyi freehand expression, and the philosophical use of emptiness — and was re-popularized for mass audiences by the mid-century ink-wash animated films of the Shanghai Animation Film Studio, whose nostalgic imagery still shapes how the style feels today. As a design aesthetic rather than a museum category, it appears everywhere: poster and packaging graphics, interior murals, folding fans, hanfu and dance costume, product rendering, and digital game key art. What holds all these applications together is not a subject list but a set of rules: monochrome carries the structure, void carries the composition, and the brush — wet, bleeding, streaky — carries the edges.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette is a monochrome ladder first, color second. Structure must always be legible in grayscale; hue is seasoning, never skeleton.

- **Paper and void:** warm-to-cool whites, `#F7F4EC` to `#FFFFFF`. The blank is an active compositional plane, not a background.
- **Ink gradation (the five tones):** mist gray `#DDE0DC`–`#C4C8C4`, middle gray `#9AA09C`–`#7A7F7B`, deep ink `#3A3E3C`–`#222624`, near-black `#101211`. Correct work uses several rungs of this ladder in soft wet transitions, not a single flat black.
- **Mineral accents (muted, small-area):** cinnabar/vermilion `#9E3A2F`–`#C8442C` (seals, sun discs, a dot of opera makeup); ochre and aged-paper beige `#CBB391`–`#E2D5BA` (paper tone, wood, mural grounds); indigo and dusk violet `#46507A`–`#6E6F92` (rare twilight variants); celadon-teal `#9FBFB8`–`#C2D8D1` (modern digital grounds); dusty rose `#D9A0AC` and sparse gold flecks `#B99646` (ribbon and energy accents in game-art variants).

Rule of economy: accents occupy a small fraction of the frame (roughly a tenth or less). Cinnabar is the only accent allowed full saturation, and only in tiny doses — a seal chop, a sun, a lip or eye mark. A beige-ground poster, a纯 monochrome scroll, an indigo dusk scene and a celadon-ground character illustration are all valid color handles of the same style.

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Negative space (liubai).** The defining motif: blank paper or mist left deliberately empty, set in strong contrast against solid ink masses.
2. **Landscape:** mountains and rivers, mist-wrapped peaks, floating clouds, waterfalls — the core theme inheritance.
3. **Bamboo and rocks:** slender bamboo stems and leaves, porous scholar's rocks.
4. **Flowers and birds:** the classic literati subject pair.
5. **Cultural punctuation:** ancient temples, small boats, bridges, pavilions — tiny human traces inside vast nature.
6. **Seals and calligraphy:** red name-seals and short vertical poetic brush-written lines used as decorative counterweights.
7. **Ink behavior as motif:** wet bleed halos, dry-brush "flying white" streaks, splatter dots, and — in modern digital variants — ink behaving as cloth, hair, water or energy, swirling into ribbons around figures.
8. **Gradient sheer fabric:** in fashion applications, translucent layered silks dyed in ink gradients from black hem to white shoulder.

### Materials and textures

Absorbent xuan (rice) paper with soft wet-on-wet diffusion; dry-brush streaks where the brush runs out of ink; spattered droplets; matte paper stock in packaging; paper folding fans; sheer organza and silk with a faint sheen; mist and fog as the atmospheric "texture" that separates depth planes. Even in 3D or digital rendering, surfaces should read as if brushed on absorbent ground — edges feather, they do not clip.

### Recurring subjects

Misty landscape scrolls; bamboo-and-rock studies; flower-and-bird vignettes; animals rendered with expressive economy (cats, deer, tadpole-and-frog lineage from the ink films); figures in hanfu or Neo-Chinese layered robes; dancers with fans and flowing gradient sleeves; mythic heroes and deities surrounded by ink-storm energy; minimalist cosmetics, tea and stationery packaging; interior mural walls; game and film key art. Subject matter is wide — the constant is the ink logic applied to it.

### Composition and lighting

Composition is asymmetric and void-led: the subject occupies a minority of the frame while emptiness holds the rest; mass concentrates in a corner or along one edge (the old "corner" manner of Southern Song ink painting) with a seal or calligraphy column balancing it from across the void. Formats follow scroll logic — tall hanging-scroll verticals and long handscroll horizontals — though square and poster ratios are fine when the void ratio survives. Depth is built by layered mist bands rather than linear perspective: planes fade from deep ink to gray to blank. Lighting is diffuse and mist-modulated: high-key white voids, mid-key fog, or low-key indigo dusk, but never hard directional light, never crisp cast shadows, never specular glare beyond a whisper of silk sheen.

## Mood & Values

The core value is **xieyi** — the freehand spirit that seeks verve and emotion over literal likeness. Emptiness substitutes for solid form and hands imagination to the viewer. The resulting mood is serene, implicit, natural: contemplative stillness even when the subject moves, because motion is expressed through flowing ink ribbons and drifting mist rather than action lines or frozen impact poses. There is also a layer of cultural nostalgia: the style consciously echoes the beloved ink-wash animated classics, so a faint poetic melancholy — distance, fog, memory — is native to it. Modern applications align it with minimalist, low-saturation, blank-layout design trends.

## Era & Origins

The lineage begins in the Tang Dynasty, when Wu Daozi and Wang Wei systematized ink-wash technique and established the style's formal foundation. During the Republic of China era (1912–1949), movements such as the Lingnan School and artists like Xu Beihong fused classical ink with Western realism, perspective and contemporary subjects, producing the hybrid sensibility the modern style inherits. After 1949, animators at the Shanghai Animation Film Studio — the circle associated with Wu Shanming — translated brush-and-ink into film (*Baby Tadpoles Look for Mama* 1960, *The Cowboy's Flute* 1963, *Deer Bell* 1982, *Feelings of Mountains and Rivers* 1988), creating national classics whose nostalgic pull still feeds the aesthetic; recent works such as *Yao Chinese Folktales* (2023) and films like *New Gods: Yang Jian* (2022) continue the thread. With digital tools the style crossed into graphic design, fashion, packaging, games and interiors worldwide, and in turn inspired the Neo-Chinese Style. ## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. **Void first.** Block out the empty areas before the subject; the blank must feel intentional and weighted, not leftover.
2. **Ink ladder, wet edges.** Use at least three tones of the monochrome ladder; transitions bleed and feather like ink on absorbent paper. Dry-brush streaks and splatter dots supply texture.
3. **Brush logic on every silhouette.** Even for characters, creatures or 3D renders, contours should taper, streak and fray like calligraphic strokes; drapery, hair and energy may literally become ink ribbons.
4. **Accent economy.** One or two muted mineral accents at most, small in area; cinnabar reserved for seal-scale moments.
5. **Mist as depth.** Separate foreground, middle and distance with fog bands or pure blank instead of perspective lines.
6. **Diffuse light only.** Soft, shadowless illumination; high-key void, mid-key fog, or gentle dusk.
7. **Punctuation, not ornament.** If calligraphy and seals appear, keep them sparse: a vertical brush-written line, one red chop, placed to balance mass.
8. **Verve over likeness.** Simplify forms toward expressive gesture; leave details for the viewer to finish.

**Variation axes (all of these may move independently)**

- **Subject:** landscape, bamboo/rock, flower/bird, animal, hanfu or dance figure, mythic hero, product packaging, interior mural, game key art — any of these, or none of the references' specific subjects.
- **Composition:** hanging-scroll vertical, handscroll horizontal, corner-mass with void diagonal, centered minimal product layout, vast field with a tiny focal figure.
- **Lighting:** high-key white, mid-key gray fog, low-key indigo dusk — always diffuse.
- **Color handling:** pure monochrome; monochrome plus cinnabar seal; monochrome on ochre/beige paper; indigo-violet dusk; celadon-teal digital ground with dusty rose and gold-fleck micro-accents.
- **Medium look:** scanned paper ink, photography of garments and interiors, matte packaging render, painterly digital ink, or monochrome 3D CGI with a single red accent.
- **Mood:** from meditative stillness to dramatic ink-storm energy, provided motion stays ink-borne.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — void dominance, ink gradation, brushed edges, accent economy — not any reference image. A minimalist soap-bottle layout, a dancing figure in gradient silk, and a battle scene dissolved in black ink swirls are equally valid members of this style.

**Avoid**

- Saturated full-color palettes, neon, candy tones, or color doing the compositional work.
- Hard vector edges, flat UI gradients without bleed, chrome, glass or glossy plastic sheen.
- Dense ornament, all-over pattern fills, heavy gold leaf (gold only as flecks), baroque symmetry or axial palace-style balance.
- Hard directional light with crisp cast shadows; photorealistic studio lighting.
- Comic-book outline linework and pop Guochao poster styling.
- Large flat fields of vermilion or any accent color; seals and suns stay small.
- Dense illegible pseudo-calligraphy dominating the layout; brush text should read as sparse ornament.
- Western one-point-perspective interiors rendered literally; depth comes from mist and tone, not converging lines.
- Japanese ukiyo-e flat color blocks and uniform outlines (different edge logic entirely).

## Discrimination Cues

Against its closest neighbor, **Neo-Chinese Style** (which Ink-wash Feng directly inspired): Neo-Chinese treats ink as one ingredient inside a broader modern design mix — it tolerates saturated accent blocks, gold line work, geometric lattice and cloud-pattern graphics, structured symmetry, glossy contemporary materials and urban or interior subjects. Ink-wash Feng keeps monochrome dominant, void dominant, and every edge brushed; its accents are mineral-muted and tiny. A practical test: convert the image to grayscale. If it still reads complete, balanced and atmospheric in grayscale, it is Ink-wash Feng; if its structure depends on color contrast, decorative density or symmetric geometry, it has drifted into Neo-Chinese or Guochao territory. Against antique scroll replication, the tell is application: Ink-wash Feng is a living design language, so paper ink logic appearing on packaging, fabric, murals, photography or digital game art is characteristic, not anomalous. Against Japanese sumi-e-adjacent minimalism, the tells are the cultural punctuation — red seals, vertical brush calligraphy, bamboo-and-rock and misty-landscape themes — and the wet, five-tone gradation rather than sparse single-stroke austerity.
