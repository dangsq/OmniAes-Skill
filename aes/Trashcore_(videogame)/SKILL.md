# Trashcore (videogame)

Trashcore names videogames whose visuals, palette or story are built to make the player feel uncomfortable, confused and out of their depth. It is the house style of the itch.io / independent-game underground: games that look assembled from half-finished projects, clashing asset packs and overcompressed screenshots, whether or not their creators labelled them "trash". The aesthetic runs from the early-2000s freeware mashups through the self-labelled itch.io text adventures of the mid-2010s to the breakthrough first-person shooters of the early 2020s. Its canonical artifact is the **screenshot**: a game viewport with its HUD, command menu, text box or window chrome still attached. Note that this is the videogame aesthetic only; the unrelated urban-pollution "Trashcore" of rust and litter is a different style.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is **two antagonistic colour families forced into adjacent regions of one frame**, at maximum contrast:

| Family | Hex range | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Acid neons | lime `#00FF00`–`#7FFF00`, magenta `#FF00FF`–`#FF4FD8`, hot red `#FF0000`–`#FF2A2A`, cyan `#00FFFF`, acid yellow `#FFFF00` | floors, skies, checkerboards, sprite fills, strobing backgrounds |
| Dingy desaturates | ash grey `#6E6E6E`–`#3A3A3A`, dung brown `#7B5B3A`, dark ochre yellow `#8A7A2E`, maroon `#6B1F1F` | walls, terrain, murky interiors, "miserable" counterweights |
| Void black | `#000000` | battle-screen and text-adventure backgrounds behind UI panels |

Either extreme may dominate a given image — a frame can be almost entirely neon strobe or almost entirely dingy murk punctured by one screaming red — but the *tension* between garish and miserable is the constant. Sub-rules: sets with very few colours are equally valid (e.g. red-and-blue dither on black); hue-shifted duplicates of the same tile or sprite produce rainbow banding; gradients, where present, are posterized into hard steps rather than smooth.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Harsh clashing palette** as above — the first and most constant trait.
2. **Mixed asset fidelity**: a photobashed greyscale photo cutout pasted beside a dithered pixel sprite beside a flat-shaded polygon mesh, each with its own resolution, shading density and source medium. Stretching, posterizing and overcompressing individual assets is part of the craft.
3. **Crude art and low-poly 3D**: lumpy paint-program humanoids, blob creatures, two-colour dithered monsters; untextured or bitmap-skinned polygons with hard facets and full-bright lighting.
4. **Broken tilesets**: repeating wallpaper, checkerboard or rosette tiles with visible seams, non-looping edges and uneven detail from tile to tile, producing visual disproportion in floors and walls.
5. **Unrealistic blood and gore**: red splatter decals, fanged fleshy maws, meat textures, characters in a permanent state of injury that never slows them down — gore proportioned to nothing in the scene.
6. **Strobing colour and limited animation**, which in stills appear as alternating complementary checkers, hue-shifted repeat sprites, stiff poses and duplicated two-frame figures.
7. **Game UI as texture**: black panels with thin white borders, monospace type, command menus (attack / skills / items lists), HP counters and floating damage numbers, window titlebars with close buttons, and text-adventure prose boxes of absurd second-person narration.
8. **Combat framing**: turn-based battle standoffs, top-down tile rooms, first-person corridors, projectile shots — violence as the default verb, delivered either as scripted turn fights or a single fired projectile.

### Materials and textures

Overcompressed JPEG blocks and colour bleed; dithered pixel grids; tiled bitmap wallpaper; photobashed photographic fragments; flat-shaded facets; paint-program airbrush squiggles; terminal-monospace UI chrome. The defining material fact is that **textures disagree with each other** — resolution, compression rate and medium change from one object to the next.

### Recurring subjects

Blob-bodied humanoid NPCs and player avatars; crude animal and fish creatures; suited pixel men; smiley-face and heart icons used as HUD or decoration; meat, maws and splatter; tiled rooms, corridors and overworld hills; title cards and prose screens with nonsensical place names.

### Composition and lighting

Framing is screenshot logic: 4:3 or letterboxed viewports, UI docked to the bottom or side third, optional window chrome at the top, or full-bleed viewport with seams showing. There is **no coherent global illumination**: every asset carries its own lighting — full-bright neon next to murky shadow next to unlit black void. Deliberate disproportion governs focus: one hyper-detailed element stranded in a field of crude ones, or one crude sprite pasted over a photographic background.

## Mood & Values

Discomfort, absurdism, confusion, violence, chaos. Trashcore values the intentionally broken as an honest statement: polish is distrusted, "good taste" is the enemy, and being literally or figuratively trash is a badge of honour. Its shock content ranges from completely unserious slapstick to vulgar excess, usually played deadpan. The intended feeling is not fear but disorientation — the sense of having booted something that was never meant for you.

## Era & Origins

Games in this mode exist from at least the early 2000s (freeware mashups of half-finished platformers and top-down shooters, circa 2007; cult RPG-maker-style journeys circa 2010). The term itself was coined on itch.io, first self-applied by a 2014 rainbow-shifting text adventure, and most early labelled work came from that scene; a 2021 first-person shooter of painfully bright colours beside miserably dingy ones brought the aesthetic its widest audience. Primary platforms: itch.io, Steam and independent distribution sites. It reacts against coherent commercial game art, asset-store uniformity and legible UI design.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Compose as a game artifact, not an illustration: include a UI layer (command menu, HP readout, prose box, titlebar, damage number) or commit to an in-engine viewport with visible tile/geometry seams.
- Force palette clash: place at least one acid neon region directly against a dingy grey-brown region or a black void.
- Mix at least two asset fidelities in one frame (pixel sprite + photo fragment + flat polygon + detailed bitmap).
- Break the tiling: seams, non-looping repeats, one tile shaded differently from its neighbours.
- Finish with compression damage: JPEG blocking, colour bleed, stretched pixels, posterized steps.
- Make gore disproportionate: splatter and maws that the scene's logic cannot explain.
- Keep figures stiff and crude: limited-frame poses, lumpy silhouettes, dithered fills.

**Variation axes**
- *Subject*: turn-based battle screen, top-down tile room, first-person corridor, text-adventure prose screen, title card, inventory/menu screen, overworld hill, dingy interior, poster-like cover — any videogame space qualifies.
- *Composition*: UI-heavy bottom dock, full-bleed viewport, letterbox, windowed frame with titlebar, collage of pasted assets.
- *Lighting*: flat full-bright, black void lit only by UI, murky dim punctured by neon, rainbow strobe.
- *Colour handling*: few-colour fixed set or maximal strobe; neon-dominant or murk-dominant, so long as the other family appears.
- *Mood*: silly-absurd, vulgar-shocking, or disorienting-dread.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**
- Unified tasteful palettes, cinematic grading, harmonious colour theory.
- Consistent fidelity across all assets; clean asset-store coherence.
- Seamless looping tile textures and even detailing.
- Smooth antialiased gradients, soft global illumination, PBR materials, ray-traced reflections, bloom.
- Modern clean UI: rounded glass panels, kerned sans-serif type, subtle drop shadows.
- Serious photoreal gore; trashcore gore is crude, pasted or cartoonishly excessive.
- Warm nostalgic pixel craft (that is 8-Bit), minimalist pastel low-poly (Low Poly), photo-based liminal dread (Weirdcore), or corrupted-interface dread alone (Digital Horror).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs 8-Bit**: 8-Bit celebrates coherent, skilled pixel craft with harmonious limited palettes and orderly retro UI; Trashcore pixels are crude, dithered, overcompressed and welded to non-pixel assets, with palettes that fight and menus that narrate absurdity in monospace.
- **Vs Digital Horror**: Digital Horror corrodes familiar interfaces toward dread, usually desaturated or monochrome with uncanny restraint; Trashcore is loud, garish and comedic, its horror unrealistically gory and its unease confusion rather than fear.
- **Vs Low Poly**: the Low Poly aesthetic treats minimal geometry as beauty — clean flat shading, pastel or restrained palettes, intentional simplicity; Trashcore low-poly is full-bright, skinned with clashing bitmaps and surrounded by mixed-fidelity junk and UI clutter.
- **Vs Weirdcore**: Weirdcore is built from amateur photography and lo-fi photo edits of liminal real spaces with overlaid text; Trashcore is engine-born — tiles, sprites, HUDs, polygons — and its disorientation comes from broken game artifacts, not from uncanny memory of places.

Where the record is thin: limited animation and strobing colour are documented traits that stills can only imply through repeated hue-shifted sprites and alternating checkers, and the style's vulgar or sexual content is common but explicitly optional — neither should be treated as a required ingredient.
