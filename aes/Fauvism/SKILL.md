# Fauvism

Fauvism was the first avant-garde movement of the twentieth century: a brief, explosive French episode (roughly 1904–1908, crystallized in the summer of 1905 at Collioure) in which Henri Matisse, André Derain, Maurice de Vlaminck, Raoul Dufy and Georges Braque liberated color from its job of describing the world. The name came from the critic Louis Vauxcelles, who, seeing these canvases beside a Renaissance-style sculpture at the 1905 Salon d'Automne, exclaimed "Donatello au milieu des fauves" — Donatello among the wild beasts — and the artists kept the label. The movement's revolution is simple to state and radical to execute: color is chosen by feeling and by pictorial structure, not by local truth; the picture is a flat surface organized into fragments of shape; and the brushstroke stays visible, thick and spontaneous. Its debt runs to Post-Impressionism (Van Gogh, Gauguin), to Gustave Moreau's Symbolist studio training, and to non-Western and decorative sources — Persian ceramics, African art, Moorish fabrics — whose flat pattern and arabesque line feed the style's ornamental rhythm.

## Visual Identity

### Color: the governing rule

Color is the subject, not the descriptor. Hues are used at tube purity, unmixed toward grey or brown, and placed in strong, dissonant contrasts that resolve into an overall decorative harmony rather than into naturalism. Skin may carry a green stripe or teal plane; water may be pink; a tabletop may be vermilion; a shadow may be cobalt. Brightness is carried by saturation, not by whitened highlights.

Working palette range (distilled rule, not a fixed scheme):

| Role | Typical hues | Approximate hex range |
|---|---|---|
| Hot anchors | cadmium red, vermilion, orange-red | #D93A2B – #E86A17 |
| Solar notes | cadmium yellow, gold ochre-yellow | #F2C230 – #E8A020 |
| Cool anchors | cobalt, ultramarine, prussian blue | #1F3A93 – #2B4FA0 |
| Greens | emerald, viridian, teal | #1F7A5C – #3AA6A6 |
| Dissonant flowers | magenta, rose pink, lilac, violet | #D96BA0 – #9B7EBD |
| Grounds and respirations | warm cream, off-white, pale straw | #F2E8D5 – #EFE3C8 |

Favored dissonant pairings: red against green, orange against blue, pink against green, yellow against violet, and large single-key fields (an entire room in red, an entire ground in deep blue-green) punctured by their complements. A canvas may be dominated by one key — red, blue, pink-lilac, yellow-green — so long as the accents strike against it.

### Signature motifs and subjects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Landscapes** — the infobox's first named motif: Mediterranean coasts and harbors with boats, olive groves, wooded banks, village streets, fields of broken color patches.
2. **Portraits and seated figures** — the second named motif: self-portraits, head-and-shoulder portraits with arbitrary facial planes, women in big hats, readers and sitters posed with hand to chin, figures in robes.
3. **Nudes, bathers and dancers** — schematic fleshy silhouettes against a saturated ground, standing bather groups, rings of dancing figures reduced to pure gesture.
4. **Interiors with patterned textiles** — rooms where wall, tablecloth and upholstery merge into one ornamental field of motifs (flowers, arabesques, stripes).
5. **Still lifes** — vessels, teapots, coffeepots, bowls and fruit (oranges especially) on a tilted, intensely colored tabletop.

### Materials, texture, brushwork

Oil paint on canvas, handled as if squeezed straight from the tube: thick, vigorous, visible strokes — dabs, dashes, short parallel taps, broad dragged scumbles. The surface reads as a mosaic of shape fragments rather than a blended illusion; Impressionism's light flickering touch is replaced by heavier, constructive marks. Backgrounds are often loosely scumbled with the warm cream ground left breathing through, so bare canvas participates in the composition. Edges between color planes are abrupt; a dark contour line may appear as an arabesque accent but never as descriptive outlining of volume. Vlaminck's mode dumps color with near-violent abundance; Matisse's mode distributes it with calculated harmony — both keep the stroke autographic.

### Composition, space and light

Flattened perspective is doctrinal: depth is compressed, tabletops and ground planes tilt up toward the picture plane, horizons sit high, and pattern denies recession. Forms are simplified to schematic silhouettes; anatomy is suggested, not modeled. Matisse's undulating arabesque rhythm — inherited from Symbolism and Art Nouveau — curves through branches, limbs and drapery. Lighting is even and ambient: there is no directional chiaroscuro and no grey cast shadow; shade is stated as a saturated hue (blue, violet, green), and sunlight is implied by overall chroma rather than depicted as an optical effect. Composition may be carefully balanced (Matisse) or tumultuous (Vlaminck), but the picture always declares itself a flat, decorated surface.

## Mood & Values

Joy, spontaneity and emotional candor. The Fauves valued color as feeling made visible — Matisse: "I do not literally paint that table, but the emotion it produces upon me" — and prized painterly spontaneity, the separation of color from descriptive reality, and a decorative equilibrium that lets even the wildest dissonance settle into pleasure. The mood ranges from serene, armchair-calm harmony to exuberant, beast-like energy, but it never turns to angst, satire or narrative sentimentality.

## Era & Origins

Paris and the French Mediterranean (Collioure), c. 1904–1908; named at the 1905 Salon d'Automne. It reacts against Impressionism's optical naturalism and light broken touch, and against academic modeled space, while extending Post-Impressionist color freedom (Van Gogh, Gauguin) to its logical extreme. By 1907–1908 the group dissolved as members turned toward Cézannesque structure and primitivism — Derain's sculptural bathers and Braque's Cézannian landscapes already augur Cubism — which fixes Fauvism's mature look firmly in the 1905–1907 window.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Choose every hue by emotion and pictorial structure first; local color is optional and usually wrong.
2. Keep chroma at tube purity; mix dissonant complementary pairs and let them balance decoratively across the whole canvas.
3. State shadows and shaded flesh as saturated color planes (green, teal, blue, violet), never as grey or brown modeling.
4. Flatten space: tilt tables and ground planes, raise horizons, let patterned textiles merge wall, cloth and figure into one field.
5. Simplify bodies and objects to schematic silhouettes; drive an undulating arabesque line through limbs, branches and drapery.
6. Keep the stroke visible and physical — dabs, dashes, scumbles, impasto; allow warm cream ground to show through in passages.
7. Light the scene evenly; brightness equals saturation, not highlight.
8. Let the picture confess flatness: fragments of shape assembled on a surface, not a window.

**Variation axes (all of these may move independently)**

- *Subject*: any landscape (coast, harbor, olive grove, village street, garden), any portrait or seated figure, nude or dance group, interior, still life — or any other ordinary motif (street, beach, café, boatyard) treated by the rules above.
- *Composition*: close-cropped head, full-figure interior, panoramic landscape, ring of dancers, tilted tabletop; centered or off-balance; calm or tumultuous.
- *Color handling*: pointillist-adjacent dabbed mosaic, broad flat planes, or loose scumbled patches; one dominant key (red room, blue ground, pink-lilac grove) or full-spectrum scatter.
- *Mood*: from Matisse's calculated decorative serenity to Vlaminck's violent color dumping.
- *Finish*: tight dark contour accents versus wholly contourless plane meeting plane.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A Fauvist harbor, a Fauvist flower bed, or a Fauvist stranger's portrait that shares none of the reference compositions is fully valid so long as color leads, space flattens, and the stroke stays wild.

**Avoid**

- Naturalistic local color, grey or brown shading, chiaroscuro modeling, cast shadows in neutral tone.
- Soft optical blending, feathery Impressionist touch, airbrushed digital gradients, invisible brushwork.
- Strict linear perspective, receding orthogonal space, volumetric academic anatomy.
- Muted earth-tone academic palettes or pastel desaturation.
- Cubist faceting, fractured planes, multiple viewpoints, ochre-grey-brown analytical palettes.
- Expressionist angularity, jagged nervous line, urban alienation and psychological grimace as the dominant tone.
- Art Nouveau whiplash ornament as graphic linework dominating flat color (painterly arabesque is fine; poster-line decoration is not).
- Photographic detail, smooth finish, narrative illustration, sentimental storytelling.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Fauvism |
|---|---|
| **Expressionism** | Shared arbitrary color, but Fauvism stays French, decorative and joyous: Mediterranean light, balanced harmony, constructive dabs. Expressionism turns dissonance into sustained abrasion — angular jagged contours, streaky nervous strokes, urban anxiety, acid-dark mood. If the picture broods or snarls rather than delights, it is Expressionism. |
| **Post-Impressionism** | Van Gogh and Gauguin are sources, not peers: their color still answers to observed structure (Cézannesque planes, cloisonnist outlines, symbolic but earthier tones). Fauvism pushes chroma to tube purity, space flatter, and abandons optical truth entirely; its dabs are heavier and its contrasts more deliberately dissonant. |
| **Impressionism** | Impressionism records light with small broken strokes and naturally mixed hue; Fauvism records feeling with thick unmixed color and flat shape fragments. Look for grey-lavender optical shadow (Impressionism) versus cobalt or emerald shadow planes (Fauvism). |
| **Art Nouveau** | Both love the arabesque, but Art Nouveau is line-first and design-bound: sinuous whiplash contour, stylized botanical ornament, poster and craft media. Fauvism is paint-first: color planes carry the image and the line only ornaments them. |
| **Cubism** | The 1907–08 border zone (sculptural bathers, proto-Cubist nudes) drains palette to ochre, grey and rose-brown and fractures form into facets. Fauvism keeps silhouettes whole and color at full chroma; once the palette desaturates and the object shatters, the style has left Fauvism. |
| **Modernism (general)** | Modernism is the umbrella; Fauvism is its first color-liberating wave, identifiable by the period handle (1905–1908 France) plus the specific combination of tube-pure hue, flattened pattern-space and vigorous autographic dash. |

Where evidence is thin — works on paper, gouache or watercolor practice, and precise lighting theory — the textual record speaks only of oil-scale painterly spontaneity; treat scumbled bare-ground passages as the style's documented surface behavior and do not invent further media claims.
