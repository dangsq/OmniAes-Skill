# Mandrake

Mandrake (regionally also Maloka, Cria, Nóia, Drake; feminine forms Mandraka and Funkeira) is a Brazilian social identity and visual style born inside the funk carioca scene of the favelas of Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo. It is the look of low-income teenagers and young adults projecting status, ambition and belonging: a fashion code the culture itself calls *chave* ("on point"), functioning as social armor and a declaration of worth in a society that marginalizes the poor. Everything visual in the style serves ostentation — not old money, but loudly branded, hard-won, displayed wealth — combined with community presence (the *tropa*, the crew) and a confrontational, self-possessed stare at the camera. The name is folk-etymologically tied to the sharp-dressed comic-strip magician Mandrake the Magician, but in Brazilian usage it means someone slick, resourceful and impressively stylish on the street.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The wiki is explicit that color "varies, often high-contrast and incorporating team colors," and the images confirm this: there is no fixed palette, only a palette *rule*. Build images from high-contrast club-color blocks — a deep jersey red (#C8102E–#E4002B), black (#0B0B0C–#1A1A1A), stark white (#F2F2F2), and burnt orange (#E8590C–#F26522) — set against saturated accent tones that recur across the culture's clothing and filters: turquoise/cyan (#00A9CE–#19D3E6), royal blue (#003DA5–#1F5EFF), and lens purple/magenta (#6A2C91–#C13AA0). Into this cloth matrix insert exactly the metallic punctuation the style is known for: chain gold (#D4AF37–#F0C75E), watch and chain silver (#B8BCC0–#D9DDE0), and the cold flare of mirrored lenses (yellow-gold, ice-blue, violet or red reflections). Backgrounds stay desaturated and mundane — grey concrete, beige plaster, dusty asphalt, cream car interiors — so the figure's color blocking and metal carry all the saturation. A correct image may lean monochrome-black (proibidão-adjacent street mood) or fully sun-bleached bright (cyan polo, white shorts); both sit inside the range as long as contrast between garment blocks and metal glint survives.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Brand-name sportswear.** Football club jerseys (the red-black and orange-black striped club shirts are typical), brand tracksuits, board shorts, athletic sneakers, polo shirts and snapback or fitted caps with club and brand logos, worn forward or backwards. This is the style's base layer.
2. **Gold and silver jewelry as wealth display.** Prominent chains (single or doubled), watches, earrings; the metal must catch light.
3. **Large mirrored sunglasses.** Aviator or wraparound sport shapes with reflective lenses in yellow, blue, violet or red; the single most reliable recognition cue, present even in indoor and night shots.
4. **Motorcycles and the *grau* stunt culture.** Customized bikes, wheelies, group rides — text-documented as a core communal activity even when a given frame shows none.
5. **Tattoos.** Forearm sleeves, neck and hand ink, displayed openly in poses.
6. **Baile funk and street gathering.** Massive street parties, corner crews, packed crowd frames; the communal backdrop of the style.
7. **Grooming codes.** Dyed hair, thin striped eyebrows (eyebrow slits), trimmed thin goatees and mustaches; grooming is text-ranked as part of the look, not an afterthought.
8. **Optional, image-observed but text-silent:** bandanas or cloth masks covering the lower face in some street and group frames, readable as proibidão-adjacent anonymity. Treat as a permissible variant, never as a required element.

### Materials and textures

Polyester jersey mesh with printed club crests, shiny nylon tracksuit faces, rubber sneaker soles and foam midsoles, cotton piqué polos, metal chain links with hard specular highlights, mirrored lens glare, screen-printed cap logos. Against these, the matte grain of the environment: rough concrete steps and curbs, plaster walls, asphalt, chain-link and rebar, car vinyl and beige upholstery, pool water. The texture story is glossy synthetic garment versus rough public infrastructure.

### Subjects

Teens and young adults of the favela and periphery: solo male Mandrake ("Faixa Preta"), solo female Mandraka/Funkeira ("Faixa Rosa") in corset tops, jeans, tattoos and sunglasses pushed into the hair, mixed or male crews (*tropa*), party crowds in matching printed shirts. Poses are confident and declarative: direct stare, chin up, hand signs, thumbs-up, crouch on a curb or step, arms crossed over chains, leaning on a bike or a car door.

### Composition, framing, medium and lighting

The style's native cameras are the phone and the music-video rig. Expect arm's-length selfie angles with slight tilt and wide-lens face proximity; candid crew line-ups and crouched group clusters on steps, curbs and street corners; vertical social-media crops; promo-flyer frames with bold sans-serif artist names and track titles overlaid; and KondZilla-grade video stills with saturated color grading and shallow depth of field. Lighting ranges from harsh tropical noon sun with hard shadows, to on-camera flash in night parties and indoor rooms, to dusk streetlight, to deliberately tinted phone-filter casts (cyan or magenta washes). Studio softbox lighting, seamless backdrops and editorial fashion framing are foreign to this style.

## Mood & Values

Ostentation as resistance: wealth display is a claim to respect in an exclusionary system, so the mood is proud, declarative and unapologetic rather than cozy or ironic. Defiance and street smarts sit under every stare; community identity surfaces in crew frames and packed bailes funk; ambition and social mobility animate the jewelry and brands. There is also play — party energy, grins, thumbs-up, poolside ease — so the style spans from confrontational cool to celebratory joy, but never to melancholy, nostalgia or quiet refinement.

## Era & Origins

Funk carioca itself emerged in Rio's Black communities in the 1970s–80s by localizing American Miami Bass; the Mandrake style cohered in the 2000s, when Brazil's economic growth widened working-class access to consumer goods and spawned Funk Ostentação (peak 2011–2014), whose lyrics fetishize brands and luxury as symbols of overcoming poverty. Later subgenres — Funk Proibidão, Funk Mandelão, Funk Automotivo, 150 BPM funk — keep the visual culture current today, disseminated through KondZilla's high-production videos and social-media selfie practice. The style defined itself partly against contemporaneous middle-class alternative scenes (emo, goth, punk), and it remains stigmatized by mainstream Brazilian society, which reads it through crime stereotypes — a prejudice the style answers with confrontational self-presentation.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Dress the figure in logo-loud sportswear: a club jersey or branded tracksuit/polo plus cap; never unbranded minimal clothing.
- Include at least one metallic ostentation cue (chain, watch, earring) and at least one mirrored-lens glint; these two accents carry recognition more than any garment.
- Block color at high contrast using club palettes; keep the environment grey, beige or dusty so the figure saturates the frame.
- Set the scene in mundane urban Brazil: concrete steps, street corners, favela lanes, car interiors, party yards, domestic rooms — infrastructure, not décor.
- Shoot in the style's native camera languages: phone selfie, candid crew snapshot, flash-lit party frame, flyer with bold type, or saturated music-video still.
- Pose for declaration, not narrative: stare at camera, show the chains, show the ink, stand with the tropa.
- Add grooming truth: eyebrow slits, dyed hair, thin goatee, sunglasses pushed into the hairline for female subjects.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be solo male, solo female, or a crew of any size within teens-to-young-adults. Setting may range across favela street, baile funk crowd, motorcycle wheelie or parked bike, car interior, pool or house party, bookshelf-lined room, dusk corner with a bus passing. Composition may range from extreme selfie close-up to wide crowd frame to typed promo flyer. Lighting may range from noon hard sun to night flash to tinted filter cast to video grade. Color handling may range from black-dominant street mood to bright cyan-and-white sun mode to full club red-black or orange-black. Mood may range from defiant to celebratory to relaxed party ease. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a frame with no sunglasses crowd, no curb, no bookshelf can still be perfectly Mandrake if the sportswear-metal-mirror logic and street realism hold.

**Avoid.**
- Carnival costume codes: feathers, sequins, glitter, body paint, confetti, samba-school regalia.
- Brazilcore patriotism: national-team canarinho yellow with green-blue flag waves and flag-print garments.
- Quiet luxury and old-money codes: beige tonal layering, unbranded tailoring, silk scarves, minimalist studio editorial lighting.
- Emo, goth and punk signifiers (the style's historic rival scenes): black fringes, band tees, flannel, studs, pale makeup.
- North American gangsta-rap scenery transplanted whole: lowriders with hydraulics, US franchise jerseys, desert suburbia.
- Phonk edit aesthetics: neon magenta-purple glitch, VHS scanlines, anime overlays, drift-car night neon, anonymous hooded silhouettes.
- Formal wear: suits, ties, dress shoes, graduation or office styling.
- Reducing the culture to crime stereotype: centered firearms, police-raid staging; the style's power display is cloth, metal and posture.
- Pristine interiors, boutique hotels, runway catwalks, softbox beauty light.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Paty de SP:** the feminine São Paulo funk-dance sibling centers young women mid-passinho, in crop tops, short shorts, styled edges and extensions, with dance-move energy as the subject. Mandrake overlaps (Mandraka/Funkeira exist) but its canonical core is ostentation gear — jersey, chain, mirrored shades — plus crew, bike and street-party context; if the frame is primarily a feminine SP dance-fashion statement without the metal-and-mirror ostentation logic, it is Paty de SP.
- **Brazilcore:** national palette (yellow, green, blue), flag motifs, football-fan patriotism and middle-class editorial or nostalgic streetwear. Mandrake uses *club* colors rather than national colors, adds jewelry and mirror lenses, and keeps favela concrete realism; Brazilcore never ostents, it waves.
- **Brazilian Carnival:** feathers, sequins, bare skin, floats, confetti, parade night. Mandrake parties too, but covered in synthetic sportswear and metal on concrete; shared energy, opposite costume code.
- **Brazilian Phonk:** a sound-edit visual language — dark neon grading, glitch and VHS artifacts, cowbell-drift car imagery, masked anonymous silhouettes, aggressive display type. Mandrake is a lived social identity photographed in daylight or flash with visible faces, real brands and community; if the image reads as a dark neon music edit rather than a person showing off among their tropa, it is Phonk, not Mandrake.
- **Gangsta Rap (related, not identical):** shares ostentation values, but swaps Brazilian club jerseys, grau motorcycles and baile funk crowds for US lowriders, grillz and North American suburbia; keep the geography Brazilian.

*Evidence note:* mirrored sunglasses, caps, chains and club jerseys are overdetermined across both text and images; motorcycles and eyebrow slits are text-documented but underrepresented in the sampled frames, so treat them as licensed elements rather than required ones; face-covering bandanas appear only in images and remain an optional variant.
