# Reggae

Reggae began as a music genre in Kingston, Jamaica, in the late 1960s — ska and rocksteady slowed down until the bass became the lead voice — and grew into a global visual language of resistance, spirituality and Pan-African pride. Its imagery was born in the street: hand-painted sound-system posters, murals on concrete, silkscreened record sleeves, and the clothing of Rastafari adherents. Everything in the style carries meaning: color is symbolism (red for blood and struggle, gold for the sun and wealth, green for the land and Africa), objects are shorthand (peace sign, lion, ganja leaf, the African continent), and the worn, handmade surface is itself a statement against polished commercial culture. The style is simultaneously relaxed and militant — "laid-back but quietly defiant."

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The core is the Rastafari tricolor — red, gold (yellow), green — plus black as structural ink and earthy neutrals as mediation. Hues stay saturated and sunlit; pastels and desaturated minimalism do not exist here.

| Role | Hex range | Meaning / use |
|---|---|---|
| Red | `#A6192E` – `#E4342A` | Blood, struggle, protest; flag bands, sunburst rays |
| Gold / yellow | `#F2A900` – `#FFD200` (ochre drop `#C98A1B`) | Sun, wealth; middle band, lettering, glow |
| Green | `#007229` – `#3AAA35` | Land, Africa, ganja leaf; top band, leaf patterns |
| Black | `#0B0B0B` – `#262626` | Anchor, stencil ink, silhouettes, background fields |
| Earth neutrals | hemp `#C9B08A`, burlap `#A9834F`, wood `#6B4A2B` | Handmade accessories, worn paper, cotton |

Color architecture rules: the tricolor appears as horizontal or vertical flag-like bands, as a smooth green→yellow→red gradient, or as radial sunburst rays; black either grounds the field (black background with tricolor type/shapes) or cuts into it as silhouette. White survives only as paper, canvas or lettering ground. Cool blues and purples are absent from graphic work; in photographic modes sky and sea may appear but are usually warmed or tinted toward the palette.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how centrally the text documents them)

1. **The red-gold-green tricolor with black** — the defining key-color system, repeated in flags, garments, knit caps, jewelry and backgrounds.
2. **Dreadlocks** — embodied fashion rejecting Eurocentric grooming; the single most identifying human feature, often voluminous and shoulder-length or longer.
3. **The peace sign** — on wristbands, pendants, mural marks and poster corners; paired with the words PEACE and LOVE.
4. **Bob Marley likenesses** — the iconic figure, usually rendered as a high-contrast stencil or silhouette portrait rather than a photograph-realistic face.
5. **The lion (Lion of Judah)** — tied to Rastafari's messianic reading of Haile Selassie; frequently shown in profile with a mane tinted or rimmed in the tricolor.
6. **Ganja leaves** — as scattered silhouettes, repeating watermark patterns or emblem shapes.
7. **The Jamaican flag and the map of Africa** — flag stripes or the gold-saltire flag as motif; Africa as silhouette or word-cloud shape expressing reverence for the homeland.
8. **Bold hand-painted / distressed lettering** — slogans and names set in painted, stencilled, arched or grunge display type on posters, murals and sleeves.
9. **Rasta dress and handmade accessories** — knitted or crocheted tams big enough for dreadlocks, loose cotton T-shirts and dresses, beaded and wooden jewelry, leather wristbands, woven belts, natural-fiber bags, sandals and desert boots.
10. **Sun, sunrise, land and sea** — visual echoes of the color meanings (gold as sun, green as land); golden-hour horizons over water.
11. **Sound-system culture objects** — boomboxes, speaker stacks, vinyl sleeves and studio/dub haze; documented in the genre's history more than its iconography, so treat as secondary.

### Materials and textures

Screen-print ink with slight misregistration; worn, creased and sun-faded paper; visible canvas weave; spray paint and roller marks on concrete or brick; halftone dots and photocopy-collage edges in mixed posters; crocheted wool, hemp and cotton cloth; seed beads, carved wood, tooled leather, burlap. Surfaces should look handled, layered and weathered — never pristine.

### Recurring subjects

Dreadlocked musicians and singers; couples and communities in Rasta dress; festival and yard crowds; lions; ganja-leaf fields or patterns; African continent shapes; sunsets and sunrises over sea or hills; hands wearing peace-sign bracelets and leather bands; murals covering walls; square record sleeves; typographic protest sheets.

### Composition and lighting

Composition tends toward the emblematic: a centered portrait, lion, map or word set against a tricolor field (bands, gradient or sunburst), with pattern or rays filling negative space. Collage grids of photos plus type, arched lettering over a bust, stacked three-panel strips, and documentary candid framing are all native formats. Lighting splits by mode: graphic work is flat ink light with no modelling; photographic work uses warm tropical daylight, hard noon shadow on walls, golden-hour flare over water, or dusk warmth; dub-derived haze, smoke softness and occasional neon rim-glow outlines appear in poster treatments.

## Mood & Values

Peace and love expressed as active protest, not passivity. Racial pride, black nationalism, anti-colonialism, anti-capitalism and criticism of political systems sit beside spiritual devotion (Jah, Selassie, Zion) and mourning for struggle (the red). The emotional register ranges from celebratory festival joy to meditative roots solemnity to militant defiance to tender romance ("one love"). Communal, handmade, sun-warmed, unbossed.

## Era & Origins

Late 1960s Jamaica, from the deceleration of ska into rocksteady and then reggae (the term coined by Toots Hibbert via "Do the Reggay," 1968). Haile Selassie's 1966 visit galvanized the Rastafari movement that supplies the palette and symbols; the 1970s carried Bob Marley and The Wailers from Kingston's ghettos to global stages; the 1980s split into dub and dancehall. The visual style reacts to urban poverty, colonial legacy and oppression, and speaks through cheap, public, reproducible media: posters, murals, sleeves.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** (1) Tricolor system: red-gold-green present as a coordinated system, usually all three, anchored by black or earth neutrals. (2) Black as ink: silhouettes, stencils and heavy outlines, never gloom. (3) Handmade print: bold painted or distressed display type, misregistered ink, worn paper/canvas/wall grain. (4) Embodied culture: when people appear, dreadlocks, tams, loose cotton, beads, wood, leather. (5) Symbol shorthand: peace sign, lion, ganja leaf, Africa, sun. (6) Message as iconography: slogans (ONE LOVE, PEACE, LOVE, JUSTICE, ROOTS, ZION, resistance lines) set as design elements. (7) Mode-consistent light: flat ink for graphics, warm tropical or golden-hour light for photography, haze or rim-glow for dub-flavored posters.

**Variation axes.** Subject: portraits, crowds, landscapes, animals, maps, still lifes of accessories, pure typography, murals, sleeves. Composition: emblematic center, banding/gradient/sunburst fields, collage, arched type, panel strips, candid documentary. Lighting: flat print, hard noon, golden hour, dusk, haze, glow outline. Color handling: pure flat tricolor, blended gradients, tricolor as tint over photography or over a lion's mane, tricolor as small garment accents on earthy neutrals, black-dominant fields with tricolor type. Mood: celebratory, meditative, protest, romantic. Medium: silkscreen poster, painted mural, photograph, collage, textile, sleeve. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Pastel or desaturated minimal palettes and clean corporate vector finishes without grain; dominant cyan/magenta/neon-rave color and smiley faces (Acid House); black-and-white checkerboards, pork pie hats, thin ties, sharp mod tailoring (2 Tone, Rude Boy); tie-dye swirls, daisies, fringe suede, hazy soft-focus bloom (Hippie) unless the tricolor system clearly dominates; ikurriña red-green-white schemes, anarchist stars and xerox punk zine monochrome (Basque Radical Rock); luxury logos, chrome, glossy consumerism (contradicts the style's anti-capitalist values); gloom-metal occult imagery; and mis-drawn flags — the Jamaican flag is a gold saltire on green and black with no red or blue; the red belongs to the Rasta tricolor, not the national flag.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Their tell | Reggae's tell |
|---|---|---|
| Rude Boy | Sharp crombie coats, pork pie hats, thin ties, cool monochrome street menace | Tricolor system, dreadlocks, spiritual symbols, relaxed defiance |
| 2 Tone | Black-and-white checkerboard, mod suits, waltzing duo logo, nervy pop-art line | Red-gold-green everywhere, hand-painted type, Rasta iconography |
| Acid House | Yellow smiley, strobe neon, cyan/magenta, orbital geometry | No smileys or strobe; earthy wear, lion/leaf/Africa symbols |
| Basque Radical Rock | Ikurriña red-green-white, anarchist punk xerox collage, zine roughness | Same protest energy but Rasta palette, dreadlocks, peace sign, Jamaica/Africa references |
| Hippie | Tie-dye spirals, daisies, paisley, sun-bleached haze, fringe | Peace sign shared, but reggae adds the tricolor system, tams, lion of Judah, ganja leaf, stencil portraits and print-worn surfaces |

The peace sign is the great shared symbol; the discriminators are the red-gold-green-plus-black system, the Rasta-specific emblem set (lion, ganja leaf, Africa, Selassie/Marley stencils), the knitted tam and dreadlocks, and the handmade, sun-worn print surface.
