# Tiki (Polynesian Pop)

Tiki is an invented paradise: an American commercial fantasy of the South Pacific built inside bars, restaurants, home dens, and poster art. Originating in 1930s Hollywood and booming after World War II, it stages "the jungle" and "island life" as hospitality design — carved Polynesian-style idols, bamboo and rattan, ropes and netted glass floats, hibiscus and orchids, torches and volcanoes, and elaborate rum cocktails served under deliberately dim light. Its visual DNA is theatrical tropical kitsch: earthy olive and khaki greens grounded by wood brown, warmed by flame reds and oranges, and cut by a cool aqua-turquoise counterpoint, delivered in vintage color photography, mid-century screen-printed ephemera, or flat stylized pattern fields.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule: a base of dried-plant and wood neutrals (khaki, olive, thatch straw, deep brown), warmed by flame accents (vermilion, marigold orange, amber lamplight), and cut by one cool counterpoint of aqua/turquoise — lagoon water, cocktail glass, glazed ceramic, printed bamboo walls. Cream paper carries the graphic lane.

- Khaki / olive: `#8C7B4A`–`#B0A179`; olive green `#55603A`–`#7A8B4F`
- Wood and thatch: `#5C3A21`–`#8B5E34`; straw `#C9A66B`
- Flame: vermilion `#B33A2B`–`#D1495B`; orange `#E07B28`–`#F2A03D`; lamp amber `#FFAE5E`
- Aqua / teal: `#7FD8D0`–`#35B8AC`; deep teal `#1F6F6B`
- Paper and ink: cream `#F0E6CE`, aged brown-black

Saturation may run high in the print and animation lanes; the photo lane keeps saturation vintage-muted with a warm cast. Cool blue-white grading is off-style in every lane.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how strongly the style documents them)

1. **Dim lighting** — the style's first-listed visual trait; light is pooled, practical, low.
2. **Carved Polynesian-style idols ("tikis")** — wood or ceramic figures standing sentinel at entrances, bar fronts, and columns.
3. **Bamboo and rattan** — pole ceilings, woven pendant lamps, cane furniture, thatch roofing.
4. **Nautical rig** — ropes, rope nets, netted glass fishing floats, buoys, portholes, fish hooks, maps.
5. **Flowers** — hibiscus and orchids as leis, hairpieces, textile prints, and drink garnish.
6. **Palms and fronds**, jungle foliage as backdrop and as repeat print.
7. **Fire and weather** — torches, fire pits, volcano iconography; tropical storms (monsoon rain, thunder, lightning) as the dramatic variant.
8. **Cocktails and vessels** — elaborate rum drinks, sculptural tiki mugs, glassware crowned with flowers.
9. **Printed tropical pattern** — stylized bamboo lattice, fronds, and blossoms in flat repeat: textiles, wallpaper, Hawaiian shirts, batik, animation backgrounds and title cards.
10. **Mid-century ephemera** — hand-lettered menus, luau posters, signage, and diagrammatic infographics on cream stock.

In-family but thinner: indoor lagoons and pools with thatched huts and boat bars (documented in historic venues); the fashion lane (floral print dresses, sarongs, caftans, Hawaiian shirts, Panama and fez hats) is text-rich but image-thin; volcanoes and monsoon storms are listed motifs but rarely depicted — treat them as accents, not defaults.

### Materials and textures

Carved and grained wood; split bamboo; woven rattan and cane; dry thatch; coiled rope and knotted netting; smooth glass floats; glazed ceramic mugs; vinyl banquettes; nubby carpet; paper and fiberglass lanterns; screen-print ink on cream stock; condensation on glass.

### Recurring subjects

Bar and restaurant interiors (including home bars); cocktail and mugs still lifes; memorabilia arrangements (mugs, vintage menus, floats); posters, menus, and title cards; all-over pattern swatches; luau and party scenes; torchlit night exteriors; lagoon water features; stylized jungle and volcano illustration; figures in Hawaiian shirts, leis, and floral dresses.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Interiors are maximalist and ceiling-heavy: hanging lanterns, netted floats, and lamps fill the upper third while wide eye-level views recede into warm dim depth, with a glowing bar back or water feature as the focal horizon; a carved idol often anchors a frame edge as foreground sentinel. Still lifes sit on dark grounds under a single warm key. Print and animation lanes go flat: all-over pattern fields, or centered hand-lettered type surrounded by sparse vignettes. Light sources are visible practicals — woven pendants, paper lanterns, string bulbs, torch flames, backlit bottles — and water glows turquoise from within. The photo lane carries a vintage color cast: warm shift, faded saturation, gentle grain.

## Mood & Values

Escapism and relaxation are the stated values: a purchased ticket to a fantasized Pacific. The mood is hospitable, abundant, playfully theatrical — kitsch worn sincerely, leisure elevated to ritual through the slow cocktail and the luau. Beneath runs postwar armchair exoticism; today the style also signals collector devotion and affectionate revival.

## Era & Origins

United States, 1930s: Don the Beachcomber (Hollywood, 1933), Trader Vic's (Oakland, 1936), later Mai-Kai (Fort Lauderdale, 1956). Popularized after World War II by American fascination with the South Pacific, peaking mid-century and sustained by collectors, conventions, and authors such as Sven Kirsten. It reacts against ordinary climate and routine — winter, office, suburb — with permanent artificial summer. Note honestly: this is an American commercial invention borrowing Polynesian forms, not indigenous Polynesian art; its carvings are décor and its jungle is set design.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

1. **Assemble, don't decorate.** Layer carved wood + bamboo + rattan + thatch + rope/net + floats + flowers until the space looks collected over decades.
2. **Keep light low and sourced.** Every glow needs a visible practical: lantern, torch, bulb, bottle, or water.
3. **Honor the palette rule:** olive/khaki/wood base, flame accent, aqua counterpoint; cream paper for graphic work.
4. **Pick one lane and stay in it:** vintage photo (patina, dim), mid-century print (cream stock, limited inks, hand-lettering), flat animation/pattern (even light, stylized flora), or dark-ground still life.
5. **Carry at least one core motif** (idol, bamboo/rattan, netted float, torch, hibiscus, tiki mug) so the style reads even in sparse compositions.
6. **Let kitsch be sincere.** Abundance and theatricality are features, not irony.

Variation axes — a correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image:

- **Subject:** interior, still life, graphic ephemera, fashion figure, storm/volcano landscape, pattern field, animation background.
- **Composition:** crowded wide interior; idol-foreground framing; ceiling canopy of hung objects; tight dark-ground still life; flat repeat; centered-type poster.
- **Lighting:** dim amber practicals; torchlit night; turquoise water glow; flat even light in print/animation; soft daylight only in illustrated poster work.
- **Color handling:** vintage-muted photo cast; saturated print inks on cream; flat animation color; near-monochrome olive pattern field.
- **Mood:** cozy escapism, playful kitsch, exotic theatrics, monsoon drama, collector nostalgia.

Avoid:

- Modern minimalist tropical resort looks: white linen, pale oak, infinity pools, bright daylight.
- Cool blue-white grades, HDR crispness, north-window daylight in photo lanes.
- Generic beach-and-palm photography with no tiki artifacts — that is Tropical or Surf, not Tiki.
- Neon cyberpunk, vaporwave pastel, blacklight fluorescents (Lowbrow territory).
- Painterly peril: jungle expeditions, heroes versus beasts (Adventure Pulp).
- Flash-lit snapshot banality, golf shirts, beige motels (Vacation Dadcore).
- Active surf sport gear as subject: boards, wetsuits, beach parking lots (Surfer / Surf Crush).
- Ethnographic documentary framing of actual Polynesian ritual; Tiki is mid-century American fantasy décor.
- Pure monochrome, desaturated grunge, or sleek digital cleanliness without patina in photo lanes.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Lowbrow:** both are pop-culture kitsch, but Lowbrow speaks in underground-comix line work, hot-rod and pinup iconography, airbrushed surrealism, and candy-fluo or blacklight color. Tiki speaks in interior design and hospitality artifacts — carved wood, bamboo, rattan, dim amber light — with an earthy olive/khaki/aqua palette. If it reads as a comic poster or custom-culture painting, it is Lowbrow; if it reads as a room you could drink in, it is Tiki.
- **Vs Vacation Dadcore:** Dadcore is the undesigned snapshot — flash flattening, beige condos, grills and golf shirts, accidental framing, 1980s–90s family-album banality. Tiki is a deliberately designed environment with theatrical dim light and carved ornament, and its vintage surface is mid-century color print or photo, not point-and-shoot flash. Snapshots of tiki venues belong to Tiki because the iconography, not the snapshot manner, carries the style.
- **Vs Adventure Pulp:** Pulp takes the same jungle and volcano and adds peril — painterly cover art, expedition narrative, muscles and monsters, saturated primary drama. Tiki removes threat: its jungle is wallpaper and its volcano a menu icon. Leisure versus danger is the fastest test.
- **Vs Surf Crush:** Surf Crush is daylight coast romance — pastel haze, sun flare, wet skin and boards, soft-focus longing in pink-blue grades. Tiki is shade and lamplight, wood and thatch, seated rather than swimming; its aqua is lagoon water glowing inside a dark room, not sunlit surf.
- **Vs Surfer:** Surfer is contemporary sport culture — boards, wetsuits, vans, lineups, action photography. Tiki is the drink after the surf: mid-century artifacts, ceramic mugs, carved idols. If the board is the subject, it is Surfer; if the mug is, it is Tiki.
