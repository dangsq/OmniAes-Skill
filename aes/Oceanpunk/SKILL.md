# Oceanpunk

Oceanpunk is a science-fiction aesthetic centred on the technology humanity uses to explore, colonise and traverse the sea, and on our relationship with an ocean that is sometimes the whole world left — apocalyptic or post-apocalyptic water over drowned land. It is steampunk's sibling transposed onto the water: wooden ships and buildings, Victorian-flavoured machinery, rivets and rope, but equally at home with modern engines and outright sci-fi submersibles. Where steampunk romanticises the engine room, oceanpunk romanticises the hull, the harbor and the habitat — the machine that keeps living possible on or under water. Its emotional core is colonisation and exploration: not war at sea, but settlement of it.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The colour language is cool aqueous dominance punctured by warm structural wood and metal, with tiny amber light accents. As a rule: roughly two-thirds of any canvas should sit in the water family, the built world supplies the warm mid-tones, and glowing lamps occupy only slivers.

| Family | Range (hex) | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Deep water / abyss teal | `#0D3B42` → `#1E5F5B` | underwater depth, shadow side of hulls, murk |
| Mid sea-green / sea-glass | `#2E7D74` → `#4FB3A9` | open water, wave bodies, patina |
| Shallow turquoise / foam / pale sky | `#7FD4C1` → `#D7EDEA` | lagoons, wake, horizon sky, cel-shaded water |
| Hull grey / storm grey / paper grey | `#8A8F8C` → `#5C6663` → `#A9ADA4` | steel hulls, overcast washes, background mass |
| Weathered wood / driftwood | `#6B4A2F` → `#9A7B57` | planking, decks, stilt piles, shanty walls |
| Rust waterline / oxide red | `#8C4A3C` → `#A0522D` | boot-topping stripes, streaked corrosion |
| Bronze / brass / lamp amber | `#B08D57` → `#C9A227` → `#E8B84B` | submarine shells, fittings, porthole glow, lanterns |

Allowed handling varies from flat cel fields to granular watercolor washes, but the ratio holds: cool water carries the image, wood and bronze build the subject, amber only glints.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how firmly the text documents them)

1. **The sea itself** — surface, depth, or flood. Water is never backdrop only; it is the ground plane, the sky's mirror, or the medium the whole scene suspends in.
2. **Ships and boats**, up to and including flotillas: vessels lashed hull-to-hull into towns, tankers converted into islands, masts crowded into a single skyline.
3. **Ocean architecture / colonisation structures** — stilt platforms, harbor towers, piling settlements, underwater habitats and domed colonies; buildings that stand in water because there is no land.
4. **Pirates and sailors** — the human inhabitants of this world; crews, rigging-climbers, harbor crowds. (In surviving imagery figures are small or absent; treat them as scale-givers and life-signals rather than mandatory portraits.)
5. **Submarines and submersibles** — the Verne lineage: brass-shelled boats, periscopes, propeller shrouds, portholes lit from within.
6. **Anachronistic technology stacking** — Victorian/steampunk era machinery (boilers, cranes, riveted plate), modern technology (engines, antennas), and futuristic/sci-fi technology (glowing pods, pressure habitats) coexisting on the same structure without comment.
7. **Post-apocalyptic salvage** — patched plate, repurposed hulls, crane-jib gangways, improvised additions; scarcity expressed as ingenuity, not ruin-porn.

### Materials and textures

Weathered plank and tarred seam; riveted steel with rust streaking from every fitting; bronze and brass with salt patina; rope, chain, canvas and net; corrugated salvage sheeting; barnacle crust and kelp fronds clinging to waterlines; glass portholes and lantern glows. Water itself takes texture from the medium: stylised scalloped wave-crests in graphic renderings, soft granulating washes in painterly ones, caustic light-nets underwater.

### Recurring subjects

Ship-cities and fleet-towns; lone working vessels (trawler-submarines, armed catamaran rafts, turret-boats); underwater colonies with glowing windows beneath kelp canopies; pirate harbors of crowded masts and lantern towers; flooded or drowned architecture reclaimed as foundation; cranes, gangways and bridges stitching vessels to walls or to each other.

### Composition and lighting

Compositions favour the establishing vista: a low horizon with water occupying the lower third to half, and vertical accumulation above it — towers, masts, stacked decks pressing against a pale or overcast sky. Underwater scenes invert this: a vertical column of habitat beneath a luminous surface, light raking down as god-rays or diffuse ceiling glow. Vessels read best in profile or three-quarter, long hulls cutting flat turquoise. Scale storytelling is constant: immense structures carrying tiny human traces — planter boxes, laundry, lantern strings, a figure on a gangway. Lighting modes: bright open daylight with near-white skies; marine overcast as flat grey-green wash; underwater shafts and caustics from above; dusk or murk lit by warm window and lamp amber.

### Medium and finish

The style tolerates several hands: granular watercolor/gouache on toothy paper with stylised wave scallops; clean ink line with flat cel shading (anime concept-art lineage); loose painterly digital with soft edges and visible brush weathering. Finish is free; palette discipline and subject logic are not.

## Mood & Values

Oceanpunk values **colonisation and exploration** — the sea as frontier and as home. Its mood is lived-in ingenuity: communities afloat, gardens on decks, lamps lit in murk, machines kept running past their intended lifespan. Wonder and hazard coexist; the deep is beautiful and indifferent, sometimes openly post-apocalyptic, but the tone is settlement rather than despair. Humanity is tethered to water by necessity and by affection, and every rust streak is evidence of staying power.

## Era & Origins

The concept originates in 1870s America with Jules Verne's *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas* (1872); the term and genre were popularised in the 1990s through films such as *Waterworld* (1995) and *Deep Blue Sea* (1999), with a mid-century lineage in *20,000 Leagues Under the Sea* (1954) and *Voyage to the Bottom of the Sea* (1961/1964), and later revivals in *Atlantis: The Lost Empire* (2001), *The Marvelous Misadventures of Flapjack* (2008), *Suisei no Gargantia* (2013), and games like *BioShock*, *Subnautica* and *Sunless Sea*. It reacts to and borrows from steampunk's machine-romance while rejecting landbound Victorianism; it is explicitly **not** Seapunk, the 2000s Tumblr sea-fashion aesthetic.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Water is ground truth: every composition acknowledges the sea as surface, depth, or flood.
- The focal subject is a built artifact for water — hull, habitat, platform, submersible — never bare nature.
- Stack eras: show at least two technology generations sharing one structure (sail rig + boiler crane + glowing sci-fi pod).
- Build by accretion: structures grow by addition — lashed hulls, stilt extensions, mast-cranes, shanty towers on decks.
- Salvage honestly: rust streaks, patch plates, mismatched planking — but everything functions and is inhabited.
- Keep palette discipline: aqueous cool dominance, wood/bronze structural warmth, amber glow as accent only.
- Let light behave like water: shafts, caustics, surface shimmer, or flat marine overcast.
- Seed human life at small scale: rigging, plants, lanterns, figures optional but implied occupancy mandatory.

**Variation axes.** Subject: surface fleet-town, lone working vessel, underwater colony, pirate harbor, drowned-city foundation, deep-trench habitat. Composition: wide horizon vista, vertical stack against sky, underwater looking up toward surface light, deck-level closeness. Lighting: noon glare, god-rayed depth, dusk lantern-glow, storm overcast. Colour handling: flat cel, watercolor wash, painterly digital. Mood: exploratory wonder, scarcity-driven ingenuity, quiet deep-sea eeriness. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.**
- Seapunk/vaporwave signifiers: holographic dolphins, chrome palms, magenta-cyan neon grids.
- Pure historical maritime realism with no speculative or anachronistic technology.
- Magic-first content: mermaids, sea gods, spell effects, enchanted creatures.
- Engineered flesh and wetware: ships grown from tissue, organic biotech hulls.
- Supernautical horror: ghost ships, cursed fog, occult iconography, gore.
- Decay without function: pollution-only murk, abandoned wrecks with no machine running and no life aboard.
- Clean minimal futurism: seamless white chrome, glass domes without rivets, seams or salvage.
- Landlocked settings: deserts, plains, cities with no water contact.
- Tropical postcard saturation: resort imagery, pink skies, leisure yachts, marinas, jet skis.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Nautical Fantasy:** fantasy leads with myth — merfolk, sea deities, enchanted vessels; oceanpunk leads with machinery and colonisation infrastructure. If the solution to a problem is a spell, it is fantasy; if it is a boiler, a crane and a patched hull, it is oceanpunk.
- **Vs Biopunk:** biopunk's submarines and cities are engineered biology — wetware, grown tissue, genetic horror. Oceanpunk's underwater worlds keep rivets, seams, brass and glass; kelp and barnacles are encrustation on built artifacts, not the artifact.
- **Vs Dark Nautical:** dark nautical is dread — desaturated storm, ghostly silhouette, supernatural menace. Oceanpunk spans bright daylight and settlement optimism; even its post-apocalyptic darkness is scarcity with lamps still lit, not haunting.
- **Vs Nautical:** plain nautical is historical or coastal realism — accurate rigging, navy-and-white cleanliness, no speculative layer. Oceanpunk always adds the anachronistic technology stack and colonisation architecture.
- **Vs Ocean Grunge:** ocean grunge fixates on pollution, rot and lo-fi murk for their own sake. Oceanpunk's decay is purposeful salvage in service of habitation and voyage — functional machines, inhabited decks, bronze warmth and aqueous clarity surviving the grime.

**One-line recognition test:** a salt-worn, era-mixed machine or settlement standing in teal water, built by hand from wood, steel and brass, glowing faintly amber from within — that is oceanpunk.
