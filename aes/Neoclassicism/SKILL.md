# Neoclassicism

Neoclassicism is the visual arm of the Age of Enlightenment: a deliberate turn away from Rococo frivolity toward the "noble simplicity and calm grandeur" that Johann Joachim Winckelmann praised in Greco-Roman art. Fueled by the excavations at Pompeii and Herculaneum, it treated antiquity not as decoration but as moral argument — clarity, reason, civic virtue and idealized form made visible in paint, stone, bronze and cloth. Its range runs from built architecture (temple-front porticoes, domed pavilions, pale stair halls) through history painting of Roman oaths and sacrifices, to intimate salons with painted grotesques, engraved paper architecture of utopian spheres and domes, and hand-colored fashion plates of the new antique-inspired dress. Across all of these the same discipline holds: symmetry, spareness, archaeological citation, and light used as a metaphor for reason.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette (the rule, not a swatch list).** Every Neoclassical image sits somewhere on a line between two poles: a *stone-and-print pole* (plaster white, limestone cream, warm beige, cool grey, ink black, grey wash on laid paper) and a *muted pigment pole* drawn from antiquity and the upholsterer's silk shop (Pompeian red and terracotta, ochre and matte gold, verdigris green, pale blue-grey, crimson rose). Saturation stays low to medium; value contrast is deployed for legibility, never for moodiness. Rough hex territory: plaster `#F4F1E9` through limestone `#E3D9C2` to shadowed stone `#C9BDA4`; grey wash `#A9A79F`; burin ink `#141414`; gilt bronze `#B08D3E`–`#C9A961`; Pompeian red `#8E3B2F`–`#A65E2E`; ochre `#C9973F`; verdigris `#4E8F82`; silk crimson `#9E4356`; pale blue-grey `#A9B8C2`; checker-floor black `#262421` against marble white. Rococo pastels — powder pink, celery, candy blue — are explicitly refused by the style.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the tradition documents them.** First, classical mythology and history as subject: togas, shields, fasces, oath-taking arms, grieving women, idealized bodies posed like statues. Second, the architectural citation: Greek and Roman orders, colonnades, triangular pediments (often with sculpted relief), domes, coffered ceilings and rosettes, ashlar masonry, plain white walls at grand scale. Third, the interior ornamental vocabulary absorbed from Pompeii: painted garlands and swags, medallions and rondels, grotesques and arabesques, urns, tripods, lyres, sphinxless candelabra — ornament confined to friezes, bands and panels, never all-over. Fourth, austere settings: bare walls, empty floor planes, niches holding a single statue. Fifth, the dress reform of the period — women in high-waisted walking dresses with corsets, petticoats, reticules, gloves and rectangular shawls or a tailored spencer; men in tall boots, waistcoats, tight fall-front pantaloons, watch fobs, gloves, canes and top hats. Sixth, the visionary branch: pure geometric masses — immense spheres, drums, domes and arches — with an oculus admitting a single shaft of light or a star-filled sky. Seventh, the picturesque counterpart: English landscape gardens with Palladian bridges, lakes and temple follies set in composed nature.

**Materials and textures.** Honed limestone ashlar and smooth painted plaster; veined marble floors, including black-and-white checker and circular inlay medallions; polished mahogany and fruitwood furniture of rectilinear profile; gilt bronze (ormolu) on balustrade tips, lanterns and chair frames; wrought-iron balusters; silk taffeta and damask hangings; matte laid paper carrying fine burin lines in engravings or delicate hand-coloring in fashion plates; oil paint worked to an invisible, enamel-smooth surface with emphatic drawing.

**Recurring subjects.** Exterior architecture (temple-front porticoes, domed central pavilions, street façades with wrought-iron balconies); interior architecture (stair halls with niche statuary and checker floors, curving stairs with gilt railings, salons and enfilades, cabinet rooms with display cases); history painting of civic sacrifice; full-length portrait and fashion plate; engraved paper architecture; the landscape garden; decorative-arts rooms and shopfronts treated as miniature temples.

**Composition and lighting.** Bilateral symmetry and axiality are the default grammar: frontal elevations, one-point perspectives down enfilades, centered domes and spheres, figures arranged frieze-like on a shallow stage plane behind arcades. Geometry itself — sphere, cylinder, cube, cone — may be the hero. Negative space is generous; walls stay largely empty so that ornament reads as citation. Light is strong, clear and even: tall French windows flooding pale stone, diffuse daylight as the image of reason. History painting narrows this to a raking, theatrical spotlight that isolates statuesque figures against shadowed vaults. The visionary branch pushes to a single source — an oculus, a pinpoint starfield — inside vast darkened voids. Interiors allow warm gilt points from chandeliers and sconces, but the overall key remains lucid, not atmospheric.

## Mood & Values

Order, reason, restraint, solemnity, morality, patriotism, civic duty, clarity, idealism. The style feels composed before it feels beautiful: stillness, upright posture, moral weight, a sense that the room or the gesture is part of a republic of citizens rather than a court of favorites. Even its intimate interiors keep their composure — silk and gilt are held inside white walls and straight lines. Grief, when present, is classical grief: controlled, grouped, sculptural.

## Era & Origins

Emerging c. 1760 in Rome and flourishing to c. 1830, Neoclassicism reacts directly against Rococo ornament and Baroque motion, armed by archaeology (Pompeii and Herculaneum from 1748 onward) and theory (Winckelmann; Laugier's 1753 claim that "architecture owes all that is perfect to the Greeks"). It spreads to Sweden and Russia, becomes Regency and Adam-style classicism in Britain, Federal style in North America (c. 1780–1830), Biedermeier middle-class classicism in German lands, and — as its late, imperial phase — French Empire style. Canonical names: Jacques-Louis David, Ingres, Canova, Robert Adam, Winckelmann, with Étienne-Louis Boullée as the unbuilt visionary conscience. As revolutionary fervor cools after 1800, its solemnity opens toward Romanticism.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build with geometry and axis first: symmetry, centered masses, receding enfilades, frieze-plane figures.
2. Cite antiquity archaeologically — a correct order, a real pediment, a Pompeian grotesque — rather than inventing free ornament.
3. Keep surfaces smooth and outlines firm: invisible brushwork, clean burin line, honed stone.
4. Light means clarity: even daylight, a raking stage light, or a single oculus; never diffuse gloom or storm.
5. Hold the palette between stone neutrals and muted antique pigment; reserve gilt for points, not fields.
6. Confine ornament to bands, friezes, medallions and panels; let large planes stay empty.
7. Give figures posture before emotion: statuesque, grouped, morally legible.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any of: built exterior, stair hall or salon interior, history painting, portrait, fashion plate, engraved visionary project, landscape garden with classical folly, shopfront or decorative-arts object. Composition may run from dead-front elevation to deep one-point perspective to shallow theatrical stage. Light may be diffuse daylight, directional spotlight, or oculus drama in a dark void. Color handling may be fully monochrome (ink and grey wash), stone-neutral, Pompeian polychrome, or silk-accented interior. Mood may span civic solemnity, domestic calm, and visionary sublimity. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a stark engraved sphere and a crimson silk bedroom are equally Neoclassical when they obey the grammar above.

**Avoid.** Rococo asymmetry, shellwork, C-scrolls and pastels; Baroque swirling motion, diagonal ecstasy and heavy curvilinear gilding; Gothic pointed arches and tracery; Romantic weather, wild brushwork and emotional distortion; visible impasto or painterly texture; cluttered, off-axis arrangements; saturated or neon color; modern materials (curtain glass, steel frames, plastics, signage); photographic snapshot casualness; imperial eagles, bees and Egyptian sphinxes in quantity (that is Empire, a later phase); caricatural exaggeration of the antique dress (that is the Incroyables edge).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Classicism.** The older 17th-century tradition (and the Central/Eastern European name for this same movement) keeps Baroque warmth, golden atmosphere and painterly softness; Neoclassicism is archaeologically literal — harder contour, cooler stone palette, Greek orders and Pompeian ornament quoted correctly.
- **Empire Style.** The 1804–1820 French phase thickens the vocabulary: massy mahogany, black lacquer, deep crimson silk, heavy ormolu, imperial eagles, laurel and sphinxes. Neoclassicism proper is paler, lighter, garland-and-medallion rather than trophy-and-emblem.
- **Gustavian.** The Swedish reading cools to grey-blue and white painted wood, faux-marble, tiled stoves and simpler country-scale furniture; domestic and homely where Neoclassicism is civic and grand.
- **Hellenic / Greek Revival.** Greek-only severity: Doric triglyphs, meander keys, white marble monochrome, no Roman arch, vault or dome. Neoclassicism freely mixes Roman engineering — arches, domes, coffering — with Pompeian color.
- **Incroyables and Merveilleuses.** The same dress reform pushed into dandyish exaggeration: impossible collars, oversized cravats, satirical pose. Neoclassical fashion imagery is poised, proportionate and serious.
- **Rococo and Romanticism**, the reaction poles: asymmetry and pastel play on one side, storm and subjective feeling on the other. Any trace of either breaks the style.
- **Postmodern neoclassicism** reuses the vocabulary at commercial scale or with ironic color; anachronistic context or wink is the tell against the genuine article's moral straightness.

*Evidence note:* the surviving record sampled here is rich in architecture, interiors, prints and fashion plates; freestanding sculpture appears mainly as niche statuary and relief, so sculptural claims rest on the written tradition more than on images.
