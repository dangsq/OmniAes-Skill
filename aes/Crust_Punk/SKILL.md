# Crust Punk

Crust punk — also called crust, stenchcore or stretchcore — began as a music form in early-1980s England, fusing English punk rock with extreme metal, and carrying dark, pessimistic lyrics about political and social collapse. Around that sound grew one of punk's most materially explicit dress codes: a DIY garb branch in which clothing is black, buried under sewn-on patches, studs and spikes, kept dirty on purpose, and sometimes built outright from trash. The "crust" is the point. Filth, repair and accumulation are not neglect but statement — an anti-consumerist archive worn on the body, where every patch names a band and every hand-stitched seam refuses the clothing industry. Visually the style sits between anarcho-punk's black uniformity and grunge's thrifted dirt, but it is denser, heavier and more armored than either: a walking collage of denim, canvas, metal hardware and grime.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)

The palette is **black anchored by earth decay**. Roughly eighty to ninety percent of any crust image should sit in soot and soil tones; saturated color is permitted only as a small accent (tartan, dyed hair, a painted patch), never as a field.

- Soot black (base layer, jackets, vests, jeans): `#0D0D0C` – `#1E1D1B`
- Sun-faded, grime-faded black drifting to charcoal-olive: `#2B2A26` – `#3B3A33`
- Olive drab, moss, surplus canvas: `#4B523B` – `#6C7250`
- Earth brown, rust, leather belts and straps: `#5A4531` – `#8A4B2A`
- Camo khaki, bleached bone cotton, photocopy-white patch ground: `#8C7F5C` – `#C9C1AC`
- Faded denim slate and grey-indigo: `#4C525A` – `#6E7680`
- Accent only: tartan / blood red `#7A1E1E` – `#A32B2B`; rare hair flash of crimson-magenta `#B01E4A` – `#C2185B` or bleached bone blonde `#D6C79E`

Rule of thumb: if an image could be described as "colorful," it is not crust. If it reads as black plus mud, moss, rust and bone, with at most one shout of red or dyed hair, it is in range.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them

**Tier 1 — definitional (text-documented).**
1. Black clothing as the default ground, shared with anarcho-punk.
2. Denim jackets and hooded sweatshirts covered in sewn-on patches.
3. Vests ("battle vests") covered in studs, spikes and band patches.
4. Trousers covered in band patches, often to the point of full patchwork where the original fabric disappears under squares of canvas, camo and denim.
5. Dreadlocks, worn sometimes rather than always.
6. Deliberate dirt and trash-born construction: fraying, staining, repair from scavenged material. The crust *is* the aesthetic.

**Tier 2 — frequent in the photographic record.**
Combat boots (often with folded socks or cloth leg wraps below the cuff); tartan or flannel shirts and bonded tartan fragments as under-layers; olive and camouflage surplus fabric woven into patchwork; hand-painted or photocopied screen-printed patches with band logos, occult and political imagery; safety pins, straps, buckles and heavy leather belts holding the accumulation together; sleeve tattoos; patched caps and beanies; the electric guitar as prop in gig-context shots.

**Tier 3 — occasional accents.**
Bright dyed hair (crimson, magenta, bleached blonde) against the black field; pale stone or brick walls and institutional halls as backdrop; squat-hall furniture (folding chairs, wooden tables) intruding into frame; animals or bicycles in group snapshots.

### Materials and textures

Heavyweight denim and cotton twill; screen-printed and photocopied patch cloth with frayed cut edges; visible hand-stitching in coarse thread, uneven and functional; metal pyramid studs, round spikes, safety pins and buckles with dull, handled patina; battered leather belts, straps and boot leather; surplus camo canvas; wool tartan; matted dreadlocks and unkempt hair; grime as a finish — dust, mud spatter, sweat staining, sun-bleaching. Nothing should look machine-new. Every surface carries evidence of wear, repair and re-repair.

### Recurring subjects

Solo full-body or three-quarter outfit portraits; pairs and small groups standing or sitting in loose rows on curbs, benches, plaza edges and grass; candid hall and gig snapshots mid-gesture (shouting, gesturing, instrument in hand); garment documentation — patched trousers or vests laid flat or worn, shown front and back as an object record; street and squat exteriors against masonry. The subject is almost always the *assembled garment* and the body wearing it, not a face or a product.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Amateur documentary framing: center-weighted, unstyled, slightly awkward, with environmental clutter left in frame. Full-body or three-quarter crops that let the patch accumulation read head to toe. Flat-lay or front/back garment shots on plain ground function as object documentation. Lighting is snapshot-grade: direct on-camera flash in dim interiors producing harsh foreground highlights, black shadow falloff and faded color casts; or flat overcast daylight outdoors; or hard midday sun with strong shadows on asphalt and grass. Medium registers as 35mm snapshot film, faded prints, xerox zine reproduction, or lo-fi digital grain. There is no studio lighting, no seamless backdrop, no glamour retouching anywhere in this style.

## Mood & Values

Crust punk looks the way its lyrics sound: pessimistic, militant, unsparing. The mood is defiance worn as squalor — dirt as a refusal of respectability and of consumer fashion, repair as anti-capitalist practice, patches as a bibliography of allegiance. Beneath the grimness runs communal warmth: groups slumped together on a curb, friends in a hall, garments traded and re-stitched. Gallows humor surfaces in hand-painted patch lettering and mid-shout grins. The style values honesty of material over beauty of finish: a torn, stained, hand-sewn garment outranks anything bought new. Anger at political and social ills is the emotional engine; exhaustion and endurance are its visible residue.

## Era & Origins

Crystallized in early-1980s England from late-1970s precursors (Discharge, Amebix, Antisect, Sacrilege), with Hellbastard credited as creator of the term's musical core; the sound splices English anarcho-punk and D-beat with extreme metal's weight. It spread fast and hard: Sweden's d-beat/krust scene (Anti Cimex, Mob 47, Disfear, Wolfbrigade), the United States (Nausea, Dystopia, His Hero Is Gone, Tragedy), Finland, Japan, the Netherlands, Germany. A neo-crust wave (Tragedy, Fall of Efrafa, Agrimonia, Svalbard) carried the look into the 2000s and 2010s, which is why the visual record spans faded 80s flash snapshots and sharper modern digital documentary of the same garment logic. The style reacts against Thatcher-era Britain, mainstream consumerism, and the polished end of punk itself: where commercial fashion cleans up, crust deliberately corrodes.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the outfit as an accumulated archive, not a designed look: black base layer, then dense layers of patches, studs, straps; the original garment should nearly vanish under its history.
2. Treat dirt as a material: grime, dust, fraying, sun-faded blacks drifting toward charcoal and olive.
3. Show the hand: visible coarse stitching, uneven patch edges, hand-painted lettering, safety pins doing real structural work.
4. Keep hardware dull and handled — studs and spikes with patina, battered boot leather, never chrome-new.
5. Place bodies in real, unglamorous space: squat halls with folding chairs, curbs, plazas, brick and stone walls, field edges, asphalt.
6. Light like a witness, not a studio: direct flash in dim rooms, flat overcast day, hard sun on pavement; allow grain, fade, blown highlights.
7. Pose anti-glamour: slouch, sprawl, defiant stare, mid-shout gesture, loose group rows. No fashion posture.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a solo portrait, a duo, a seated group, a gig candid, a street scene, a camp or field gathering, or a flat-laid garment record. Composition may run full-body, three-quarter, tight crop on vest or trouser patchwork, or top-down flat-lay. Lighting may be flash-in-darkness, overcast flat, harsh sun, or dim warm interior. Color handling may swing from near-monochrome all-black, through olive-and-brown earth patchwork, to camo-dominant or tartan-accented builds, with or without a single dyed-hair flash. Mood may be defiant joy, grim exhaustion, communal warmth or open anger. Era register may be 80s film snapshot or present-day documentary digital. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A photograph of boots and patched trousers on a kitchen floor, or a group under a bridge at dusk, is fully crust if the palette, material logic and handmade grime hold.

**Avoid.**
- Clean, new, retail-crisp clothing; unworn denim; machine-perfect seams.
- Brand logos, commercial graphics, fast-fashion prints, sponsor-style type.
- Studio lighting, seamless backdrops, editorial posing, retouched skin or fabric.
- Saturated color fields, neon, pastels, candy tones; more than one small accent of bright color.
- Salon grooming: styled blowouts, tidy beards, manicured looks (spiked or matted hair is fine, *clean* hair styling is not).
- Luxury materials: silk, satin, glossy leather, jewelry as ornament rather than hardware.
- Minimalist composition, generous negative space, product-shot isolation on white.
- Orthodox 1970s punk shorthand alone (mohawk + safety-pin earring, bright tartan suit) without patch accumulation and grime.
- Pure metal shorthand (black leather and spikes with no patches, no denim, no dirt).
- Folk props (banjo, accordion, suspenders, newsboy caps), grunge casualness (flannel and ripped jeans with no patches or studs), or scavenged-homelessness imagery lacking subcultural signifiers — crust's squalor is chosen, coded and literate in band patches.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Decisive difference |
|---|---|
| **Gutter Punk** | Gutter punk reads as street survival: rainbow dread extensions, plastic bags, carts, cardboard and blanket improvisation, color chaos of scavenging. Crust keeps a coherent black-and-earth patchwork armor, stud hardware and band-patch literacy — squalor as uniformed ideology, not destitution. |
| **Anarcho-Punk** | Anarcho-punk is cleaner and tidier black: hoodies, stenciled slogans, political badges, pacifist severity, little metal influence. Crust adds studs and spikes, extreme patch density, camo/olive surplus, dreadlocks and deliberate filth. |
| **Basque Radical Rock** | Rock radical vasco leans street-rock and festive: ikurriña and red-green-white colors, band tees, leather jackets, sneakers, bandanas, conventional rock staging. No patchwork armor, no stud density, no earth-decay palette. |
| **Folk Punk** | Folk punk softens into thrift warmth: acoustic instruments, banjo and accordion, suspenders, cardigans, floppy hats, mustard-rust-cream palette, whimsical hand-sewn motifs. Crust stays electric, studded, black-based and severe. |
| **Grunge** | Grunge shares dirt and thrift but drops ideology: flannel shirts, plain ripped jeans, band tees, cardigans, sneakers, slacker apathy, no patches, no studs, no dreads. Crust's grime is armored with patch and stud accumulation and political intent. |

When in doubt, apply the load-bearing test: **black base + dense sewn-on patch and stud accumulation + visible handmade repair + deliberate grime + snapshot lighting.** All four present: crust. Missing the patch-stud armor: grunge or anarcho. Missing the grime and handwork: orthodox punk or metal. Missing the black base and coherence: gutter or folk.
