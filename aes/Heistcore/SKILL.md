# Heistcore

Heistcore is the visual culture of the elaborate theft: the staged, high-skill robbery of money, art, jewels, historical documents and data, as imagined in espionage, crime and detective media. It borrows the trappings of wealth because wealth is the mark — gilded frames, marble, velvet, Persian rugs and whiskey decanters appear either as the setting being infiltrated or as the loot being carried out. Where a con is grubby, a heist is framed as high-class craft, so the imagery celebrates competence, secrecy and anonymity over violence: gloved hands, masked faces, mechanical lockwork and paper plans instead of guns and struggle. Its register runs from the 1920s Gentleman Thief and Cat Burglar, through mid-century caper cinema, to sleek contemporary heist fiction, keeping an analog, tactile soul — paper, brass, leather, glass — even when the subject matter is modern.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The governing rule: a dark, deep, metallic ground carrying saturated jewel accents, with cream paper as the counterpoint note. Never pastel, never warm-orange dominant, never fully desaturated.

| Role | Hex range | Typical carriers |
|---|---|---|
| Ground black | #0B0B0C–#1C1C1E | catsuits, leather gloves, void backgrounds, night interiors |
| Steel & silver | #6E7681–#C9CED6 | vault doors, locking bolts, watch cases, security hardware |
| Gold & gilt | #8C6A1D–#D4AF37, highlights to #E8C86A | picture frames, bullion, cuffs, clasps, champagne |
| Paper cream & tan | #C9B08A–#E9DFC7 | blueprints, banknote stock, antique letters, dossiers, ID cards |
| Jewel red | #5E0B15–#8A0303 | rubies, velvet curtains, lipstick |
| Jewel blue | #082567–#0F52BA | sapphires, deep evening suits |
| Jewel green | #013220–#046307 | emeralds; the muted gallery-green wall tone (~#6F8F7A) sits in this family |

A correct frame holds at least one metallic family (gold or steel) plus either jewel saturation or paper cream. A single gold accent on near-black is as valid as a full jewel-toned spread.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

- **Tier 1 — the core lexicon:** gold bars, coins and statues; paper money in high denominations; diamonds and brightly colored jewels (rubies, sapphires, emeralds); bank vaults with elaborate locking mechanisms; oil paintings in gold frames; antique letters, books and maps.
- **Tier 2 — strongly documented:** wall safes and hidden compartments; security cameras; the leather catsuit with neckline-to-waist zipper and matching gloves; rubber masks bearing the faces of American presidents or celebrities; disguise uniforms (cleaners, guards, clergy, art experts) and black-tie ball gowns and suits; champagne and coupes; fur coats; bold makeup — winged eyeliner, red lipstick; marble statues; clocks and watches; Persian rugs; red velvet curtains; Fabergé eggs; whiskey decanters; diamond-shaped glass paperweights; Eastern-origin artifacts.
- **Tier 3 — text-listed but low-frequency:** exotic pets (peacocks, sharks, alligators), Vespa scooters, Louboutin heels. These are legitimate but optional; nothing depends on them.
- **Document layer (image-corroborated extension of the disguise and map motifs):** forged ID badges and credentials, architectural floor plans and blueprint schematics, dossiers — the paper trail of planning. Likewise the scattered loot pile and the gloved hand as protagonist recur across the visual record.

### Materials & textures

Brushed and polished metal (vault steel, bolt work, watch cases); supple matte leather and faux leather (catsuits, gloves); crisp paper and cardstock (plans, notes, banknotes); faceted stone and cut glass (gems, paperweights); gilded wood and plaster (frames, ornament); cold marble; deep-pile velvet and fur; matte rubber (masks); silk and satin (evening wear). The style lives on texture contrast: hard machine against skintight leather, crisp paper against gleaming stone, soft fur against cold steel.

### Recurring subjects

Loot (bullion, cash, jewels, art, artifacts, documents); the security apparatus that guards it (vault doors, wall safes, cameras, vintage locks); the thief's kit (catsuit, gloves, mask, heels, fur); the wealthy setting (museum gallery, mansion room, auction house, casino, vault anteroom); the paper trail (blueprints, maps, forged badges); the disguised figure moving through a service or black-tie role; occasionally the getaway (scooter) or the villain's exotic pet.

### Composition & lighting

Composition tends toward object-forward still life: a single precious object isolated on a dark void, or a chaotic scatter pile of notes and gems filling the frame. Hands stand in for people — gloved hands clasped, cuffing, or cupping loot — while faces stay masked, cropped or turned away. Hidden-compartment reveals (a hinged painting swung open on a safe, a panel ajar) and frontal, near-symmetrical portraits of vault machinery are signature structures; documents read as flat lays or overhead scans. Lighting is controlled and deliberate, ranging across: even, shadowless gallery or museum light; hard direct flash against black, like an evidence photo or paparazzi snap of loot; low-key single-source chiaroscuro raking leather and metal; cool institutional fluorescent for vault corridors and back-of-house spaces. The wiki text does not prescribe lighting; this range is distilled from the visual record and all four modes are in-style.

## Mood & Values

Competence, control and secrecy. Heistcore romanticizes skill — lockpicking, gymnastics, forgery, disguise, planning — and treats transgression as elegance rather than brutality. Wealth is ambivalent: admired as craft (the gilded frame, the cut stone) and indicted as ill-gotten, echoing the genre's recurring question of who really owns looted art. Anonymity is glamorous: the mask and glove confer identity without a face. The emotional temperature is cool, poised and often playfully caper-like — tension without gore, triumph without cruelty.

## Era & Origins

Dated to the 1920s: the opening of King Tut's tomb and the ensuing Egyptomania, with European grave robbers trafficking artifacts to aristocrats and museums, fixed the style's obsession with looted antiquities and contested ownership. Its lineage reaches back to medieval Robin Hood folklore and sideways into the Golden Age of Detective Fiction's Gentleman Thief and Cat Burglar; its modern voice was shaped by caper cinema from the 1960s onward and by real-heist folklore — the Mona Lisa robbery (1911), the Amber Room (1941–45), D.B. Cooper (1971), the Gardner Museum (1990), Hatton Garden (2015). The result is a retro-modern register: analog tools, mechanical locks and paper plans even in contemporary settings.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Stage value: every frame contains an object of obvious monetary or cultural worth, or the machinery that guards it.
2. Anonymize the human: hands, gloves, masks, silhouettes, cropping; identity is costume, not face.
3. Keep the ground dark and metallic; let gold, jewels or cream paper carry the light.
4. Treat wealth doubly: Old Money décor as environment *and* as target/loot.
5. Include the paper trail where plausible: plans, maps, badges, dossiers.
6. Make security architectural: vaults, safes, cameras, locks as set pieces, not props.
7. Light with intent: gallery evenness, hard flash, or low-key spot — never ambient warmth.
8. Contrast textures: metal/leather/paper/stone in at least two families per frame.

**Variation axes**

- *Subject:* any loot class, security device, thief garment, disguise role, wealthy interior, document type, getaway vehicle or exotic pet within the ranked lists above.
- *Composition:* centered machine symmetry, hidden-compartment reveal, overhead flat lay or scatter, tight hand close-up, figure-in-disguise environmental shot, isolated object on void.
- *Lighting:* diffuse gallery, hard flash on black, chiaroscuro single source, cool institutional fluorescent.
- *Color handling:* full jewel saturation; near-monochrome black/steel with one gold accent; cream-paper-dominant document scenes; muted gallery-green walls.
- *Mood:* cool infiltration, playful caper, forensic aftermath, post-heist triumph, elegant black-tie deception.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A frame of a single forged badge on cream cardstock under flat light is as valid as a vault door under flash.

**Avoid**

- Street-crime grit: graffiti, broken glass, drug paraphernalia, poverty cues — heists are high-class by definition.
- Weapons-forward framing, blood, or physical struggle; stealth and craft over force.
- Pastel, boho, sun-bleached or matte-Scandinavian palettes; LED-strip modern minimalism.
- Neon cyberpunk holograms, VR interfaces, glowing HUDs; even data heists stay tactile (server halls of brushed steel, cloned keycards, badge forgeries).
- Pirate-treasure register: sunlit sand, cartoon X-maps, spilling doubloon chests.
- Full sepia or desaturated monochrome — that surrenders the jewel tones that define the style.
- Influencer-style smiling direct-to-camera portraits; faces appear in character, masked, or cropped.
- Military tactical gear, camouflage, assault rifles.
- Loud modern branding, plastic packaging, fast-fashion clutter.
- Golden-hour outdoors, beaches, sky-dominated frames.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Film Noir:** Noir is monochrome or near-desaturated, rain-slick streets, fedoras and trench coats, venetian-blind shadows across anguished faces, moral despair. Heistcore keeps saturated jewel tones and gold, puts objects of value — not fatal women or doomed detectives — at center, and feels gleeful competence rather than doom.
- **Golden Age of Detective Fiction:** That style investigates: drawing rooms, tweed, pipes, clue boards, country-house murder paraphernalia. Heistcore perpetrates: vaults, loot, catsuits, masks. Shared tan-and-antique surfaces differ in intent — the parlor becomes the vault, the detective becomes the thief.
- **Old Money:** Old Money celebrates inherited wealth at rest — equestrian prep, patinated interiors, soft daylight, no tension. Heistcore borrows the same markers but frames them as target or spoil, and always admits the transgression hardware: safes, cameras, gloves, masks, flash-lit cash. Old Money never shows a vault door.
- **Playboy:** Playboy is mid-century bachelor hedonism — smoking jackets, boudoirs, warm pop saturation, overt pin-up sexuality. Heistcore shares champagne and fur but centers theft apparatus and anonymity; the catsuit is functional stealthwear, the palette darker, metallic and jewel-toned rather than lounge-warm.

**Quick tell:** dark metallic ground + a guarded or stolen object of great value + an anonymous human trace (glove, mask, crop) = Heistcore.
