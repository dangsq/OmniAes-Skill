# Retro Gamer

Retro Gamer surrounds the object-world of video games: the hardware, the packaging, the rooms and the venues where play happened between the 8-bit boom and the turn of the millennium. It is an aesthetic of affection for machines — controllers worn shiny on the sticks, cartridges piled in heaps, CRTs humming as the only light in a carpeted room, arcade cabinets standing in receding rows under their own marquees. Where neighboring aesthetics borrow pixel grids or neon palettes as pure graphics, Retro Gamer keeps insisting on the *thing itself*: plastic, paper labels, rubber cables, particleboard shelves, and the domestic or arcade space that houses them. Its emotional register is comfort, contentment, euphoria and nostalgia — the feeling of returning to a saved game.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette rule is a base-plus-emission structure: most surface area belongs to the machines and their surroundings, while saturated color arrives as small areas or as light leaking out of screens and marquees.

- **Base neutrals (majority surface):** console grey `#A9A9A9–#C8C8C8`, age-yellowed plastic `#D8D2B8`, charcoal and black plastic plus dark-room shadow `#141414–#2E2E2E`, domestic beige carpet and wood shelving `#B99B6B–#D9C7A7`.
- **Greens (signature accent):** pea-green Game Boy screen and PCB green `#8BAC0F–#9BBC0F`, soft mint `#A8E6CF`.
- **Pastels (secondary family):** shell pink `#F4A7C3`, baby blue `#A9D6F5`, lavender `#C7B5E6` — handheld shells, craft homages, packaging gradients.
- **Neon emission (accent light, not base paint):** magenta `#FF2BD6`, cyan `#00E5FF`, electric purple `#6A0DAD`, marquee orange `#FF8A00`, CRT glow white-blue `#DCEBFF`.

A correct image keeps grey/black/beige as the structural ground and lets green, pastel and neon behave as buttons, labels, screens, glow and washes. Pastel-dominant variants (candy handhelds, crocheted cartridge crafts, lavender box-art gradients) are legitimate, but they retain at least one grey or black hardware anchor so the image reads as gaming culture rather than pure kawaii.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Controllers and accessories** — the infobox's first motif: chunky pads with three-pronged or two-grip silhouettes, concave analog sticks, colored letter buttons, directional crosses, coiled and tangled cables.
2. **Consoles and handhelds** — cartridge-slot bodies, clamshell portables with small non-backlit screens, console faceplates with brand badges.
3. **Televisions, specifically CRTs** — curved glass, thick bezels with printed logos, scanlines, the screen acting as the room's light source.
4. **Arcade cabinets** — lit marquee headers, angled control panels with joysticks and buttons, painted side art, cabinets arranged in rows.
5. **Game cartridges and discs** — labeled grey or colored shells, stacked, shelved, or piled in heaps.
6. **Gaming magazines and packaging** — newsprint pages, bold mastheads, box art with heavy sans-serif logotypes and Japanese katakana product text, paper manuals.
7. **PCs and headsets** — documented in the text but thin in the visual sample; treat as optional period props.
8. **Period screen graphics** — 8-bit pixel scenes, low-poly worlds and wireframe graphics appearing *as content on a screen*, not as the image's own rendering language.
9. **Collector architecture** — cube shelving, wall-to-wall console displays, staircase risers covered in console logos, bins and boxes; collection-as-interior-design.

### Materials and textures

Matte and gloss ABS plastic with UV yellowing; rubberized cable coils; paper cartridge labels with slight peel; newsprint and coated magazine stock; CRT glass with curvature, scanlines and dust; carpet pile and particleboard; plastic storage bins; coin-slot metal and tempered marquee acrylic; satin souvenir jackets with embroidered logos; occasional handmade craft textures (crocheted or beaded cartridge homages) as fan tributes. Wear is affectionate: shine on thumbsticks, scuffed shells, tangled wires — never museum-glass sterility.

### Recurring subjects and scenes

Domestic play spaces (CRT on a low shelf over beige carpet, consoles lined beneath); maximalist collector rooms and shelf walls; dim arcade rows and pinball corners; hands holding handhelds or pads in intimate close-up; packaging and box-art graphic layouts; pixel game screens presented as artifacts; overhead cluttered flat-lays of consoles, cables and manuals; occasional merchandise extensions (jackets, crafts).

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Composition modes range from **hero-object centering** on a flat graphic gradient field (packaging logic: one controller, bold logotype, corner badge) through **receding rows** (cabinets, shelf lines), **dense grid maximalism** (wall of consoles and screens), **overhead cluttered flat-lay**, to **hand-held close-up** and the **single glowing screen anchoring a dark room**. Lighting modes: domestic daylight or on-camera flash snapshot with falloff and grain; dark-room illumination supplied entirely by CRT or marquee glow; saturated neon washes (magenta, purple, cyan) flooding objects; flat even print lighting for graphic and packaging work; illustrative modes add sparkle glints and cel-shaded highlights on plastic. Scanlines, slight grain and color casts are welcome surface signatures.

## Mood & Values

The style invokes comfort, contentment, euphoria and nostalgia. It values physical media and preservation (cartridges saved, shelves curated), collection as an act of care, tactile play over abstract subscription services, childhood memory, and the communal memory of arcades. Clutter is pride, not mess; wear is proof of love. Euphoria appears as glow, sparkle and saturated screen-light — never as speed, violence or sleek futurism. The accompanying cultural voice is playful and referential: game-obsessed musicians and novelty electro tracks about arcade fever signal the style's tongue-in-cheek devotion.

## Era & Origins

The objects originate in the 1980s–2000s: 8-bit home consoles and arcade golden-age aftermath, through the cartridge-to-disc transition and the handheld boom, up to sixth-generation hardware. The aesthetic itself is a retrospective internet-aesthetic culture that gathers these artifacts into a coherent nostalgic language, reacting against flat-panel minimalism, digital distribution and disposable modern tech by insisting on weight, slots, cables and labels. Its graphic ancestry includes period packaging design (bold sans logotypes, Japanese katakana product names, gradient fields) and period screen graphics (pixel, low-poly, wireframe).

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. **Artifact-first:** every image must contain or strongly imply a physical gaming object or gaming space from the 1980s–2000s — hardware, media, packaging, cabinet, or the room that houses them.
2. **Base-plus-emission palette:** ground the frame in grey/black/beige; deploy green, pastel and neon as buttons, labels, screens, glow and washes rather than as wall paint.
3. **Machine-emitted light:** in dark scenes, the light source is the machine — CRT phosphor, marquee acrylic, LEDs — casting colored pools onto carpet and plastic.
4. **Wear honesty:** allow yellowing, scuffs, cable tangles, dust and paper age; pristine sterility breaks the nostalgia.
5. **Density spectrum:** both a single hero object on a graphic field and a maximalist shelf wall are correct; choose deliberately, not accidentally.
6. **Period typography as prop:** bold sans-serif logotypes, katakana product text, magazine mastheads and bezel badges may appear as diegetic graphics.
7. **Mood check:** the finished image should feel like returning home to a saved game — comfort, contentment, euphoria, nostalgia.

**Variation axes (allowed ranges)**

- **Subject:** any gaming artifact or space of the era — console, handheld, controller, cartridge, disc, CRT, arcade cabinet, PC, headset, magazine, box art, collector shelf, arcade row, pixel/low-poly/wireframe screen content, craft or fashion tributes.
- **Medium:** snapshot or catalog photography, packaging/print graphic, pixel art, cel/anime illustration, craft photography.
- **Composition:** hero-object centering, receding row, dense grid wall, overhead flat-lay, hand-held close-up, screen-anchored dark room.
- **Lighting:** daylight or flash snapshot → CRT-dark glow → neon wash → flat print light → illustrative sparkle.
- **Color handling:** grey-dominant, pastel-dominant with a hardware anchor, or neon-washed; all valid within the base-plus-emission rule.
- **Mood:** quiet domestic comfort at one end, euphoric glowing celebration at the other.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**

- Modern flat-panel TVs, curved esports monitors, thin-bezel displays, RGB-lit gamer rooms, streaming overlays, mechanical-keyboard close-ups of current gear.
- Current-generation console silhouettes (glossy white towers, matte monoliths) and touch-screen phones as the subject.
- Whole-image pixel or low-poly rendering with no hardware or culture frame (that is 8-Bit or Low Poly, not Retro Gamer).
- Fractal swirls, UV mandalas, rave psychedelia (Cyberdelia).
- Uniform kawaii pastel polish with no grey/black hardware anchor, no wear, no carpet (Cybertwee).
- Arcade-venue kitsch — ticket counters, prize shelves, carnival signage — with the machines absent or backgrounded (Arcadecore).
- Vaporwave props without games: marble busts, palm grids, chrome gradients.
- Holograms, sci-fi HUDs, cyberpunk cityscape gloom, grimdark tones.
- Non-gaming retro (vinyl, cassettes, diners) as the primary subject.
- Sterile museum-vitrine presentation; the style is lived-in.

## Discrimination Cues

- **8-Bit:** 8-Bit is a rendering language — chunky pixels, sprite logic, limited palette — applied to any subject. Retro Gamer borrows pixel graphics only as period screen content or artifact within a hardware culture frame. Test: pure pixel scene with no machine, room, package or hand = 8-Bit; pixels glowing on a CRT beside a console = Retro Gamer.
- **Arcadecore:** Arcadecore is the venue as social space — ticket counters, prize walls, neon carpet, token machines, carnival signage. Retro Gamer includes arcades but centers the cabinets and the act of play, and treats home setups, shelves and handhelds as equally core. Test: remove the games and if the image still works as a kitschy venue, it is Arcadecore.
- **Cyberdelia:** Cyberdelia is psychedelic trance culture — UV fractals, acid swirls, transcendent rave euphoria. Retro Gamer's neon is emitted by machines and sits in domestic or arcade space; its euphoria is nostalgic, its geometry rectangular. No fractals, no mandalas, no chemical transcendence.
- **Cybertwee:** Cybertwee is kawaii pastel tech — soft pink gadgets, cute interfaces, sweet feminine polish with no grime. Retro Gamer may show pastel handhelds and candy accessories but anchors them in grey plastic, cable tangles, beige carpet and CRT gloom; it tolerates wear and clutter that Cybertwee forbids.
- **Low Poly:** Low Poly is an early-3D rendering technique — faceted flat-shaded polygons — practiced as an end. Retro Gamer treats low-poly only as a game running on period hardware, viewed through glass; the image itself is photographic, illustrative or graphic, not a faceted 3D scene.

Where evidence is thin, say so: wireframe graphics, PCs and headsets are text-documented but scarce in the visual record, and fashion extensions (satin logo jackets) appear only occasionally — treat all three as optional garnish, never as defining motifs.
