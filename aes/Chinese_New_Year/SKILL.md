# Chinese New Year (Chunjie / Spring Festival)

Chinese New Year is the most important holiday of Chinese culture, timed by the lunisolar calendar and celebrated with remarkable consistency across China and the diaspora. Because its activities are highly traditional and its decorations industrially mass-produced, the holiday reads as a specific, reproducible aesthetic: a festive atmosphere intertwined with vibrant consumption, unified from offline bazaars to online shops. Its visual core is simple and non-negotiable — saturated, sharp red carrying gold — while its subject matter spans food, doorways, markets, lantern fairs, dance processions, family interiors and winter streets.

## Visual Identity

### Color language
Red is not an accent here; it is the atmosphere, the ground, the field in which everything else sits. Gold is the second voice, applied as stamping, glitter and foil rather than as flat paint.

| Role | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground red | vermilion `#E23A2E` → festival red `#C8102E` → deep lacquer `#8E0E00` | saturated and sharp, never brick-dusty or terracotta-muted |
| Gold | foil `#C9A227` / `#D4AF37` → bright metallic `#FFD700` | reads as stamped, glittered, embossed; modern commercial norm for calligraphy and ornament |
| Ink black | `#1A1A1A` | traditional brush calligraphy on red paper; night sky silhouette |
| Night indigo | `#0B1026` → `#1A2151` | backdrop for fireworks and lantern glow |
| Lantern amber | `#F5A623` → `#FFB347` | warm point-source glow from paper skins |
| Porcelain / snow white | `#F7F3EE`, cool white | plates, steam, snow, fur trim — accent only |
| Citrus orange, foliage green | `#F28C28`, `#2E5E3A` | oranges, vegetables, pine and plum branches |

Pastel or cream grounds appear only in the style's media layer — zodiac infographics, sweet charts, poster templates — where red-gold motifs sit on softened backgrounds. In photographic and decorative contexts, desaturation breaks the style.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence
1. **Chunlian (spring couplets)**: a set of three red paper strips — a short horizontal hengpi of four hanzi over the doorframe, flanked by two long vertical shulian bearing couplets of wishes. Traditionally bold black brush calligraphy; modern commercial versions are gold foil-stamped. Both are correct.
2. **The doufang of '福'**: a square red paper sheet, often presented diamond-oriented, pasted upright on the outside of the front door and upside-down indoors (the pun "fortune arrives"). Modern versions add 3D relief, flocking, glitter and gold stamping.
3. **Lanterns**: the 'big red lantern' (oval body, vertical ribs, yellow tassels) hung in pairs under eaves, on lampposts and in rows; gong-deng palace lanterns (hexagonal or octagonal wooden frames, painted silk or glass panels, corner tassels); spinning zouma-deng; lotus, rabbit and fish lanterns, the latter two often handheld by children on sticks; and monumental lantern art sculptures (qilin, dragons, architecture-scale pieces) at yuanxiao lantern fairs, plus LED string lights in lantern shapes.
4. **The New Year's Eve dinner**: a round table crowded with dishes — whole fish, dumplings shaped like ingots, whole chicken, nian gao, eight treasure rice, tangyuan — plus bamboo steamers, porcelain plates and rising steam; snacks like the togetherness tray, citrus, spring rolls and tanghulu.
5. **Wealth objects**: hongbao red packets (bank-note sized, opening on the wide side), yuanbao gold ingots, money trees, round coins with square holes.
6. **Fireworks and firecrackers**, from rooftop bursts to children's sparklers.
7. **Chinese knots** with long tassels; **chuanghua** red paper-cut window flowers; **nianhua** New Year paintings.
8. **Loong (dragon) and lion dancing** processions through crowds and temple courtyards.
9. **Winter views**: snow on roofs and branches, breath in cold air, fur-trimmed clothing.
10. **Places**: temple fairs (miao-hui), decoration markets, ancestral halls of southern China, traditional architecture, the countryside.
11. **Pattern vocabulary**: the year's zodiac animal; auspicious creatures (loong, fenghuang, qilin); gods (Menshen, Caishen, Zhaowangye); plum blossom, pine, magpie, crane.
12. **Greeting gestures**: kowtow and zuoyi, elders handing hongbao to children.

### Materials and textures
Red paper matte and lacquered; gold foil stamping, glitter, flocking and 3D relief on characters; silk and satin for lantern skins and qipao; knotted cord and rayon tassels; bamboo steamers and lattice wood; painted glass; porcelain; paper-cut edges; and, because the festival turns from winter to spring, woven wool, down, faux fur and thick cotton in clothing. Steam, spark trails and falling snow are the style's atmospheric textures.

### Composition and lighting tendencies
Recurring structures rather than fixed scenes: frontal symmetry around a doorway (doufang centered, couplets flanking, lanterns paired); corridors of hanging verticals — couplet strips, lantern strings — creating rhythmic depth in markets and fairs; overhead radial spreads of dishes on a round table; crowd-depth diagonals following a lion or dragon through a street; radial firework bursts against night; centered object still lifes of knots, ingots or packets. Lighting falls into four registers: bright even daylight or market fluorescence with glossy saturation; night warmed by lantern glow and firework point-sources against indigo black; soft-flash nostalgic interior snapshots with slight film fade for family-memory scenes; and cold overcast snow daylight in which red pops hardest.

## Mood & Values

The style expresses reunion as the highest form of happiness: the world's largest human migration resolved into one crowded, complete table, presented with impeccable completeness even when reality is messier. It celebrates wealth and abundance through ritual consumption, auspiciousness through puns and symbols, and Confucian ritual order through seating arrangements, elder-junior greeting sequences and ancestral rites. Festivity licenses non-daily roles — dressing up, decorating beyond utility — and the whole construct affirms a shared Chinese identity across regional cultures and overseas communities. The tone is sincere, warm and boisterous; irony has no place here.

## Era & Origins

Ancient in root (at least 1000 BC): red embodied yang, used from pre-Qin through Han to subdue yin and dispel the outgoing year's misfortune. Tang dynasty red paper replaced peach wood for blessing inscriptions; the Ming Hongwu Emperor's decree that every household paste red-paper couplets made red the definitive New Year symbol. The name "Spring Festival" dates to the Republic of China; the canonical big red lantern was designed in 1949. Crucially, today's gold-heavy look is modern: gold only became ubiquitous after industrial gold foil stamping, and manufacturing and logistics unified the aesthetic into a reproducible, commercialized festivity. The style is therefore folk-traditional in grammar and mass-commercial in surface.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Make red the ground: most of the frame's area or atmosphere should be saturated red; gold stamps it rather than competing with it.
2. Keep ritual grammar correct: couplets come in flanking vertical pairs with a four-character horizontal strip; the Fu square centers a door (upside-down only indoors); lanterns hang in pairs or rows; fish are served whole; hongbao pass from elders to children.
3. Use real, legible hanzi in bold brush or foil-stamped forms — never pseudo-calligraphic strokes.
4. Favor auspicious density over restraint: pile symbols, dishes, lanterns, crowds. Abundance is the message.
5. Choose one lighting register consciously (market daylight, lantern/firework night, nostalgic flash interior, snow daylight) and keep saturation high in all of them.
6. Show warmth in clothing when people appear: silk qipao or tangzhuang, Ming-style hanfu with fur-trimmed bijia, red down jackets, gold-thread mamian skirts, braids with red ribbon, updos and buns.
7. Let texture carry the modern folk surface: foil, glitter, flocking, tassels, paper-cut edges, steam.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any node of the festival: feast table, market aisle, door facade, lantern fair, dance procession, family greeting, child with handheld lantern, snow portrait in hanfu, object still life of packets/ingots/knots, firework rooftop night, dumpling-making kitchen, or a graphic media artifact (poster, zodiac infographic, gala key visual). Composition may range from frontal symmetry to overhead radial to crowd diagonal to centered still life. Lighting may range across the four registers above. Color handling keeps the red-gold core but may admit ink black, porcelain white, snow, indigo night, citrus orange and foliage green; cream or pastel grounds are acceptable only in the infographic/poster media layer. Mood may run from boisterous crowd energy to intimate family warmth to serene snow stillness. Medium may be glossy photograph, retro film snapshot, product render, paper-cut vector, lantern-skin painting or embroidered motif. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.**
- Minimalist negative space, ink-wash zen emptiness, muted earth-tone or boho palettes.
- White or black as the dominant field (mourning associations); white headwear; white only as snow, fur trim, porcelain or infographic ground.
- Aggressive or rebellious styling, unnatural hair colors, ironic or satirical tone.
- Illegible pseudo-hanzi calligraphy.
- Non-Chinese festival signifiers: kadomatsu, torii, hanbok, kimono motifs, Christmas trees or Santa despite the shared red-gold.
- Neon, teal or cyan palettes and streetwear-collage graphics as the dominant language (Guochao items may appear only as clothing accents inside a festival scene).
- Summer settings, lush tropical greenery, beach light; the festival turns from winter to spring.
- Decay, grime, gloom, or sparse single-object zen arrangements (neutral studio backgrounds are fine only when the object itself is a red-gold festival item).

## Discrimination Cues

**Against Guochao:** Guochao is a brand-driven, youth-consumer remix of heritage signs — flat vector illustration, bold typographic layouts, irony, sneakers and printed tees, palettes that freely include teal, black or neon, and no festival requirement. Chinese New Year shares motifs but is festival-bound, folk-commercial and ritual-grammatical: red as ground, foil gold, family, food, winter night. Test: if the image could sell a sneaker any month of the year, it is Guochao; if it needs the lunar new year to make sense, it is this style.

**Against Neo-Chinese Style:** Neo-Chinese is a quiet spatial and fashion minimalism — ink-wash greys, warm wood, lattice screens, generous negative space, red reduced to a single punctuation mark (one knot, one couplet on a calm wall), scholarly stillness. Chinese New Year inverts every one of those: red is the field, gold glittering on top, density instead of void, crowds and feasts instead of silence. Test: remove the red — if the composition still holds as a serene interior, it is Neo-Chinese; if removing red destroys the image, it is Chinese New Year.
