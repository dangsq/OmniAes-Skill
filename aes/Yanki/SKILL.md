# Yanki

Yanki (ヤンキー, also yankii) is Japan's home-grown delinquent look: teenagers and young adults who dye their hair blond or orange, gel it into pompadours, shave their eyebrows, alter their school uniforms, smoke and drink in public, and refuse the strict manners of Japanese society. The term doubles as an umbrella for Japanese delinquency in general — sukeban, gyaru, bosozoku and teamer groups can all be counted as yanki — but as a specific aesthetic it has its own grammar: the modified gakuran uniform, the embroidered jacket, the squat, the group, the banner, the cigarette. It is a poor-student, working-class style that treats trashiness as honesty and loudness as loyalty, and it survives today mostly as a retro code in manga, anime and B-movies.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The core triad is **black, white and yellow-gold**, with red as the stitching and ink accent. Treat this as a rule of proportion rather than a fixed scheme: black dominates the clothing mass (wool gakuran, leather jackets, night backgrounds), white appears as cloth surfaces (banners, white tokku-fuku coats, T-shirts, print borders), and yellow-gold plus red live in the embroidered line work and calligraphy that cuts across both.

- Base blacks and charcoals: `#0b0b0d` – `#232323` (uniform wool, leather, night voids behind flash-lit subjects)
- Cloth whites and bone: `#e8e4da` – `#f7f5f0` (banner fabric, white coats, sun-bleached print edges)
- Embroidery golds and mustards: `#c9a227` – `#e8c547` (sukajan satin, thread work, brass buttons)
- Stitching and ink reds: `#a61b25` – `#d0342c` (kanji columns, oni/hannya emblems, crest details)
- Hair spikes of peroxide blond and orange: `#caa64f` – `#e0c06f`, `#b45a1f` – `#d97b2f`
- Optional saturated satin accents on sukajan and jackets: royal blue `#1d3f8f`, vermilion `#c0392b`, teal-green `#2e6e4e`

Saturation is allowed to concentrate in exactly two places: hair dye and embroidery/satin. Everything structural stays black, white or gray concrete. A fully saturated rainbow image is not yanki; a black-clad group whose only color is one blond head and a red-stitched banner is.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them

1. **Altered school uniforms.** The chouran (長ラン): a lengthened, high-collared black gakuran jacket, worn unbuttoned and draped on the shoulders to exaggerate its size, frequently embroidered with kanji. Shortened or otherwise mutated gakuran, and for women altered sailor uniforms with miniskirts.
2. **Hair and brows.** Pompadour / regent styles and punch perms, gel-thick and glossy in black, or dyed blond, orange or red. Shaved or drastically thinned eyebrows on both genders. The hair silhouette is the single fastest recognition cue.
3. **Sukajan.** Satin souvenir jackets descended from Yokosuka base-town souvenir wear, covered in raised embroidery — tigers, dragons, waves, maps, kanji.
4. **Tokku-fuku.** Long military-style coats carrying vertical columns of kanji, shared with bosozoku.
5. **The squat.** The "popping squat" (sonkyo-style crouch) on curbs, asphalt and gravel; a resting posture, not an action.
6. **Cigarettes and drinks.** A lit cigarette between fingers, canned drinks, convenience-store plastic bags; smoke as atmosphere.
7. **Tattoos and the manji crest.** Glimpsed irezumi and the Buddhist manji used as a gang emblem on jackets and cloth (a traditional crest, not a political symbol).
8. **Machines at the edge of frame.** Modified motorcycles, old boxy sedans, chrome and exhaust metal; present as lifestyle props rather than the subject.
9. **Group banners and flags.** Large cloth banners heavy with kanji calligraphy and a painted crest (oni masks, chrysanthemum-like mon) held behind or above the group. The written text documents kanji embroidery on garments; the photographic record extends that calligraphic impulse onto flags, so treat banners as a strong but secondary motif.

### Materials and textures

Wool serge of the gakuran against glossy satin of the sukajan; raised, slightly clumsy machine embroidery; stiff leather jackets; hair lacquered to a hard shine with gel; cotton and canvas banner cloth with painted ink; gravel, cracked asphalt and concrete underfoot; chrome, exhaust pipes and old car paint. Over everything sits the texture of the medium itself: 35mm film grain, flash blowouts, white print borders, faded dye casts, or — in the anime/manga iteration — flat cel color with bold outlines and impossibly tall hair silhouettes.

### Recurring subjects

Teenage and young-adudent men and women in tight-knit groups formed in middle school; solo portraits of a leader type; mixed-gender crowds; construction-site workwear as an adult afterlife; teen mothers as the documented female stereotype; rockabilly dancers in 50s dress (poodle skirts, polka dots, florals) in the rockabilly branch; Shibuya-casual teamers in American leather jackets in the 90s branch; and, in the revival era, cosplayers and anime characters carrying the same code.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Groups arrange themselves in horizontal arrays in front of a banner, or in clustered knots with a squatting front row and standing back row; hierarchy is visible — the loudest hair and longest coat at center. Solo shots favor a low angle that stacks the pompadour against sky or black night. Candid crowd frames catch subjects ignoring the camera, smoking, mid-argument. Lighting splits into two families: **night flash** — harsh on-camera flash, subjects gleaming, background collapsing to black void — and **faded daylight** — overcast or flat sun on gravel lots, riverbanks and parking areas, with washed-out film color. Fluorescent convenience-store spill and dusk blue hour sit between them. The anime iteration uses flat, hard-shadow cel lighting with saturated hair color.

## Mood & Values

Rebellion is the declared core value, but it is rebellion as social style rather than politics: loud, rude, deliberately trashy, allergic to the strict manners of Japanese society. Beneath the swagger sits a strict internal code — fighting rules that limit violence, loyalty to a group formed in adolescence and kept into adulthood, working-class pride (the construction-site stereotype, the regular job after high school). The mood is confrontational boredom: squats, cigarettes, stares at the camera, nothing to do and everything to prove. In its afterlife the style also carries Showa/Heisei nostalgia, a remembered toughness rendered affectionately in anime and B-movies.

## Era & Origins

Born in the 1950s–60s from American GIs' rock 'n' roll: Yokosuka base-town "sukaman" imitated US soldiers and popularized the sukajan (later cemented by the film *Pigs and Battleships*), and Greaser hair gave yanki its pompadour. The name arrived in the 1970s, borrowed from "Yankee"; the subculture flourished through the 1980s and 1990s, faded from street life after the 2000s, and persists in manga and anime (*Tokyo Revengers*, *JoJo's Bizarre Adventure*, *Cromartie High School*, *Bucchigiri?!*). The chouran descends from prewar Bankara student roughness filtered through Tokyo cheer-team leaders — a lineage worth remembering when discriminating styles. Branches: Japanese Rockabilly (50s music and dance, black greaser jackets, huge pompadours, women in poodle skirts) and Teamers (90s Shibuya Center-gai street crews in American casual leather, adjacent to kogal gyaru).

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Hair and uniform read before the face: if the silhouette (pompadour or punch perm, altered gakuran or embroidered jacket) fails, the image fails.
2. Kanji is texture: vertical stitched or painted columns on coats, banners and cloth are structural elements, not captions.
3. The group is gravity: even a solo portrait should imply a crew just out of frame — a second cigarette glow, a parked bike, a banner edge.
4. Deliberate trashiness: garments too long, too loud, ill-fitting, hand-altered; nothing tailored, nothing branded-luxury.
5. Analog witness: film grain, flash blowout, print borders, B-movie flatness — or, in illustration mode, bold-outline cel flatness with exaggerated hair volume.
6. Ground level: curbs, gravel, asphalt, squat height. The camera meets the subject at street level or below.

**Variation axes.** Subject: solo to groups of a dozen; male, female or mixed; teens to young adults; rockabilly dancers, teamers in street leather, cosplayers, anime characters. Setting: night street corners, convenience-store fronts, parking and gravel lots, riverbanks, school roofs and classrooms, arcades, pachinko parlour exteriors, dusk embankments. Composition: banner-backed array, squat line at a curb, low-angle solo, candid off-center crowd, interior fluorescent snapshot. Lighting: night on-camera flash, flat overcast, faded daylight film, blue-hour dusk, fluorescent spill, flat cel light. Color handling: black/white/gold-red core; or saturated sukajan accents against gray concrete; or dyed hair as the only saturated element; or monochrome film. Medium: 35mm snapshot, B-movie still, anime cel, flat cartoon illustration. Decade flavor: 50s rockabilly, 70s punch perm, 80s tokku-fuku peak, 90s teamer casual, post-2000s anime revival.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A girl in an altered sailor uniform with a miniskirt and shaved brows, smoking under fluorescent light with no banner anywhere, is exactly as valid as a night group posed behind a kanji flag.

**Avoid.**
- Clean studio lighting, seamless backdrops, influencer polish, luxury logos, minimalist styling.
- Untouched natural hair and full groomed eyebrows on focal subjects; the style alters both.
- Western punk hardware (mohawks, safety pins, tartan) or modern hypebeast sneaker culture as the focal note.
- Adult yakuza formality: dark suits, ties, montsuki kimono, family-crest pins — yanki is teenage delinquency, not organized-crime dress.
- Kawaii gyaru glamour: pastel palettes, platform boots, brand-cute accessories, tanned polish.
- Prewar Bankara props: tattered capes, geta, antique sepia tone.
- Bosozoku machine-fetish as sole subject: neon jumpsuits, helmets, towering exhaust pipes, convoy shots with no pedestrian uniform group.
- Modern anachronisms: smartphones, vapes, LED screens, current-model cars (unless intentionally staging the post-2000s revival).
- Sanitized costume-party brightness: keep fabric worn, ink slightly clumsy, fit deliberately wrong.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Their tell | Yanki's tell |
|---|---|---|
| Bankara | Prewar/early-Showa rough student: ragged gakuran or hakama, capes, geta, headbands; no dye, no pompadour, no embroidery; antique tonality | Postwar code: gel pompadour or punch perm, bleached hair, shaved brows, kanji-embroidered chouran; flash-snapshot or cel rendering |
| Bosozoku | Machine-first: customized motorcycles, tall exhausts, convoys, helmets, neon or reflective jumpsuot-like tokku-fuku, flags mounted on bikes | Pedestrian and uniform-first: hair and altered gakuran carry the image; tokku-fuku appears but bikes stay peripheral props |
| Sukeban | Female-only crews; long, ankle-length altered sailor skirts; monogender framing | Mixed-gender groups; women in miniskirts per the yanki code; male hair silhouettes usually present |
| Greaser | American 1950s: white tee, cuffed jeans, leather jacket, black pompadour; no kanji, no gakuran, no sukajan embroidery | Same hair ancestry plus Japanese uniform grammar: kanji stitching, chouran silhouette, manji crests, louder blond/orange dye |
| Gyaru | Feminine glamour: tanned skin, styled bleach, brand fashion, platforms, kawaii accessories; cute as currency | Trashy uniform-derived dress on both genders, shaved brows, confrontational rather than cute; gyaru elements appear only as teamer girlfriends, never as the core |

Because yanki also functions as an umbrella term, overlap is expected: when an image mixes bosozoku bikes or sukeban skirts with a pedestrian uniform group, altered hair and squatting posture, classify it yanki. Note honestly that the surviving photographic record skews to male groups and night flash; female yanki visual evidence is thinner (miniskirts, the sukeban split, rockabilly poodle skirts), and banners, while ubiquitous in photographs, are documented in writing mainly through the kanji-embroidery tradition they extend.
