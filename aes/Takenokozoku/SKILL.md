# Takenokozoku (Bamboo Shoot Tribe)

Takenokozoku was a Japanese street fashion and dance subculture active in Harajuku, Tokyo, from the mid-1970s until 1981. Teenagers — mostly junior and senior high school students of the affluent "no hunger" generation — gathered on Sundays in Yoyogi Park and on Harajuku streets to perform choreographed dances to rock music blared from large portable radios, dressed in costumes bought or assembled from Boutique Takenoko, the shop that gave the tribe its name. The look is maximalist, gaudy and deliberately unrefined: a loud, loose, color-saturated rejection of conservative 1970s Japanese dress, worn as a group uniform that still allowed each member individual flair. It is regularly cited as a predecessor of Gyaru, and was briefly revived from 2019 by the group KEKENOKO.

## Visual Identity

### Palette
The color rule is high-chroma candy saturation, never muted or earthy. Core hues: hot magenta-pink (roughly #E83A8C–#FF5FA8), crimson-scarlet red (#D92B3E–#E8465A), and violet-purple (#6E4AA8–#A678D8). Supporting hues that recur across the corpus: turquoise-teal (#1FA8A0–#39C0C4), marigold yellow (#F2C230), tangerine orange (#F0782A), plus off-white/cream (#F2EDE2) as a grounding panel color and occasional black piping or sash. The structural rule is color-blocking: a single costume combines two to four of these hues as contrast panels, sashes, hems, piping and trim, so clashing brights sit adjacent on the body rather than being blended. Whole groups read as a swarm of saturated color against grey pavement and green park foliage.

### Signature motifs and garments (ranked by how strongly the source documents them)
1. **The harem suit** — the defining garment: a shapeless jumpsuit or wide-legged harem pants with a matching top, voluminous through hip and thigh, often gathered at the ankle. Silhouette conceals the body's outline entirely.
2. **Ribbons, bows and fringe** — the named surface adornments, applied abundantly at chest, shoulder, sleeve and hem.
3. **Wide, theatrical sleeves** — dolman/batwing or kimono-derived furisode-like wings, frequently in a contrast color or gradient, which flare open when arms are raised in dance.
4. **Sash belts** — broad obi-like bands cinching the loose volume at waist or hip, usually in a clashing hue.
5. **Maximalist accessory abundance** — badges, buttons, pendants, feather ornaments, visor caps; more is correct, restraint is not.
6. **Portable stereos / large portable radios** — the music prop of the subculture, plus equipment cases carried to the Sunday gathering.
7. **Group formation** — matching-but-individual costumes: a shared palette family and silhouette with per-person color variants, worn in rows, clusters or dance lines.

### Materials and textures
Cheap, lightweight synthetics with a costume sheen: polyester satin, jersey, chiffon-like panels, fine pleats (as on pleated skirt-halves and batwing tops). Fabric should read slightly crinkled, soft and unstructured — handmade or small-boutique construction, never tailored. Fringe and ribbon trim add movement; satin catches daylight with flat glossy highlights.

### Subjects and props
Dancing teenagers in full costume; posed dance troupes mid-routine with arms raised or angled; proud standing presentations of a single costumed figure; groups crossing streets or massed on park promenades; bystanders in ordinary conservative clothing as an involuntary contrast chorus; boomboxes, radio stacks and flight cases; trees, paved plazas, pedestrian crossings, shopfront streets of Harajuku.

### Composition and lighting
Full-body or three-quarter framing that preserves the loose silhouette is the norm; the costume's volume is the subject. Groups arrange in loose rows, diagonals or clusters rather than tight symmetrical grids. Candid snapshot energy — slight tilt, imperfect cropping, mid-gesture bodies — is characteristic. Lighting is outdoor daylight, typically harsh midday sun casting crisp shadows on pavement, or open park shade. Two valid medium registers: (a) original-era 35mm snapshot — grain, faded chroma, warm cast, slight overexposure, printed-photo softness; (b) modern revival documentary — crisp digital clarity, saturated greens and clean shadow detail. Hair and makeup are not prescribed by the written record; period imagery shows natural dark hair in feathered, fringed or bobbed cuts, minimal or pale stage makeup, and simple white sneakers or flat shoes as footwear — treat these as period-typical, not mandatory.

## Mood & Values

Ebullient, defiant joy. The style expresses individualism and freedom of expression through deliberate gaudiness, while simultaneously asserting group identity — a tribe in matching loudness. Its values are play, public occupation of space, and quiet rebellion against rigid social conformity: teenagers claiming a park on a Sunday afternoon with blaring rock and self-made splendor. The mood should feel celebratory and slightly amateur in the best sense — earnest, exuberant, unpolished, unashamed.

## Era & Origins

Mid-1970s to 1981, Harajuku and Yoyogi Park, Tokyo; born from Boutique Takenoko's costume racks and the post-war prosperity cohort that rejected its parents' selflessness-and-hard-work ethic. It reacts against conservative, disciplined mainstream Japanese fashion of the 1970s and prefigures later street movements such as Gyaru. A 2019 revival (KEKENOKO) restages the look with modern fabrics and digital-era photography, which keeps the style legible in two temporal registers.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Build the silhouette first: volume without body-con. Wide harem legs, shapeless jumpsuit bodies, huge sleeves, one cinching sash.
- Color-block at least two clashing saturated hues per outfit; trim, piping and sashes carry the contrast.
- Load the surface: ribbons, bows, fringe, badges. Maximalism is the baseline.
- Keep fabric cheap-looking and light: satin sheen, jersey drape, fine pleats, soft crinkle.
- Stage it publicly in daylight: park promenade, plaza, crossing, street — with ordinary-clothed bystanders optional but effective.
- Prefer group grammar: unified palette family, individual variants per wearer.
- Put the body in choreographed motion or proud presentation: raised arms, flared sleeves, angled poses.
- Choose one medium register and stay in it: faded vintage film snapshot, or clean revival documentary.

**Variation axes**
- Subject: solo dancer, duo, full troupe, costume detail close-up, radio/equipment prop study, crowd crossing scene, backstage-style gathering.
- Composition: full-body, three-quarter, candid tilt, posed row, elevated view of a crossing or park circle.
- Lighting: harsh noon sun, open shade under trees, bright overcast — always outdoor daytime.
- Color handling: any combination inside the saturated range; pink/magenta/red/violet as core, teal/yellow/orange/cream as support; gradient sleeves and contrast panels allowed.
- Mood: joyful defiance, playful spectacle, earnest rehearsal, communal pride.
- Era register: 1970s–81 film snapshot or post-2019 revival digital.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**
- Body-conscious silhouettes: tight dresses, pencil skirts, mini-skirts, fitted tailoring.
- Muted, neutral or monochrome palettes; beige minimalism; earth tones.
- Luxury logos, designer branding, refined couture finish, crisp suiting.
- Gyaru markers: bleached or blonde hair, deep tans, white eyeliner, false lashes, platform boots.
- Bodikon markers: clingy one-piece dresses, gold jewelry, teased curls, nightclub settings.
- Night scenes, neon club lighting, indoor studio glamour.
- Authentic formal kimono with correct kitsuke — the wide sleeves here are synthetic costume quotation, not traditional dress.
- Modern streetwear coding: hoodies, techwear, logo sneakers as the focal point.
- Polished fashion-editorial staging in the vintage register; snapshot imperfection is part of the look.

## Discrimination Cues

| Cue | Takenokozoku | Bodikon | Gyaru |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silhouette | Shapeless, voluminous harem suit; body concealed | Body-conscious, clingy one-pieces | Fitted casual brand fashion, mini-skirts |
| Palette | Saturated costume satin: magenta, red, violet, teal | Bubble-era black, red, gold accents | Beige/brown/white or neon accents, tanned skin contrast |
| Hair & face | Natural dark hair, minimal makeup | Teased, curled hair, tanned skin | Bleached hair, tan, heavy white-liner makeup |
| Setting | Daylight park/street, Sunday dance gathering | Clubs, night streets, late-80s urban | Shibuya streets, arcades, cafés |
| Props | Portable radios, sashes, fringe, bows | Gold jewelry, heel boots | Brand bags, purikura, platform boots |

Fast tells: if the garment hides the body in loose color-blocked satin with harem legs and flaring sleeves, and the scene is a sunlit public dance gathering, it is Takenokozoku. If the fabric clings and the room is a nightclub, it is Bodikon. If the tan, bleached hair and branded casual wear lead, it is Gyaru — the descendant, not the tribe.
