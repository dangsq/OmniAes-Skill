# Minimal Wave

## Overview

Minimal Wave names a body of obscure, self-published electronic music from roughly 1978–1985 — cheap portable analog synthesizers and drum machines, pre-MIDI, recorded in isolation by amateurs — and, just as importantly, the archival aesthetic built around it decades later by reissue labels, compilers and fans. The visual style is therefore double-exposed in time: it mixes the genuine DIY artifacts of cassette culture (xeroxed J-cards, scrawled lettering, grainy band photos) with a revival-era curatorial layer (monochrome reissue sleeves, fan edits pairing tracks with Cold War-era film footage). The result reads as austere, melancholic, technically minded and deliberately uncommercial: a photocopied memorial to music almost nobody heard the first time.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The base is strictly monochrome: deep blacks (#050505–#1a1a1a, often lifted by grain rather than truly pure), xerox whites and paper whites (#e6e6e6–#f5f5f5), and a dominant middle register of murky, underexposed greys (#4a4a4a–#9a9a9a) that gives film-still imagery its sodden tone. This restraint is frequently broken by exactly one accent, handled in one of three licensed ways: (a) a flat desaturated pastel field behind black xerox art — pale cyan (#9fc4cf), mint grey-green (#cfdcd4), blush or lavender-pink (#e3c9d6, #e6d2e0); (b) a small saturated stroke on the monochrome base, such as a marker-green scrawl or a thin printed stripe; (c) a global warm sepia shift of the whole monochrome image (#b7a99a over #8a7d6f shadows). Rule: never more than one accent hue, never gradients, never a full-spectrum palette. A correct image may also be purely black-and-white with no accent at all.

### Typography

Type carries much of the design weight. Two voices dominate: oversized or edge-pinned stacked sans-serif capitals in a plain grotesque (Helvetica/Univers family), tight-led, set white-on-black or black-on-white/pastel, often shrunk to a small block in a top corner so that empty black field becomes the composition; and loose hand-scrawled marker script — signatures, vertical scribbles, speech-bubble text — that preserves the handmade tape-era character. A plain serif caps line or a small italic script subtitle appears occasionally as a secondary voice. No drop shadows, no chrome, no gradient fills, no ornamental display faces.

### Motifs and objects (ranked by documented prominence)

1. **Monochrome imagery** — the governing condition of nearly everything else.
2. **Stark typography** as described above.
3. **Tape grain / noise** — analog grain, photocopy degradation, and literal noise fields (squares of TV static or hiss texture set as an image panel).
4. **Geometric abstraction** — angular triangles, wireframe perspective grids, tilted rectangular fragments, mesh zigzags; lineage from futurism and constructivism.
5. **Vintage film footage** — grainy stills from 1960s–1980s, often Cold War-era or B-movie science fiction films, presented letterboxed with black bars; a revival-era fan convention now central to the look.
6. **Oscilloscopes and technical apparatus** — named key motifs; in surviving artwork this surfaces more often as technical line diagrams (reel-to-reel tape machine schematics, panel drawings) and wireframe grids than as literal glowing scopes, so treat "laboratory/electronics diagram" as the working form.
7. **Architectural imagery** — subway platforms and tunnels, coffered ceilings, colonnades (including classical column illustration), shot or drawn in severe one-point perspective.
8. **Tight close crops** — a single face, a pair of eyes, a pair of hands (hands holding a cigarette or a cable plug recur), preferred over full portraits.
9. **Cassette-culture objects** — cassettes, reel-to-reel machines, mail-art/fanzine ephemera.
10. **Organic or hand-drawn counterweights** — reeds, foliage, sketched line figures, speech bubbles — paired against severe geometry so the frame never feels purely clinical.

### Materials and textures

Photocopied/xeroxed print is the reference medium: crushed contrast, halftone dot, paper-scan flatness. Analog film grain and VHS softness cover the photographic side; tape hiss is visualized as static or noise texture. Matte vinyl-sleeve and folded J-card surfaces, slight misregistration, and visible seam artifacts (film-strip joins, double-exposure overlays) are all in-period credentials. Nothing should look like a clean digital render.

### Subjects

Deadpan amateur musicians and non-models; cropped eyes and gloved or cigarette-holding hands; costumed figures from old films; mannequin-still poses and flat theatrical gesturing; urban transit spaces; scientific and technical diagrams; starkly photographed organic matter; abstract geometric constructs. Faces are unsmiling, made-up in heavy dark eye makeup or utterly plain, addressed to the camera with detachment or not at all.

### Composition and lighting

Recurring structures: the letterboxed film still (4:3 image inside black bars); a vast black field with one small centered cluster of tilted photo fragments; a diagonal cascade of rectangular image shards; a grid of cropped facial features; one-point perspective corridors and platforms; a flat frontal "scanner" plane inherited from photocopying; asymmetric type blocks surrounded by generous void. Lighting is flat frontal flash with harsh falloff, murky available light with underexposed greys, blown highlights on a face against black, or a soft circular vignette. There is no rim-light glamour, no golden hour, no colored gel lighting — accent color arrives as graphic field or ink, never as illumination.

## Mood & Values

Detachment and existentialism first: melancholic, deadpan, somber, alienated, preoccupied with technology and urban loneliness. Underneath sits a value system of DIY ethos, anti-commercialism, lo-fi authenticity and sincere amateurism — "electronic pop gems from the underground" — plus a layered nostalgia for cassette culture and for a past that the revival partly invented. The image should feel archived, overlooked, and quietly earnest rather than ironic or slick.

## Era & Origins

Music originates 1978–1985 across North America, Europe (notably Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, the UK) and Japan, in a pre-internet mail network of isolated musicians (the CLEM fanzine community), influenced by Kraftwerk, Yellow Magic Orchestra, John Foxx, early OMD and Depeche Mode, and overlapping new wave, post-punk, cold wave, dark wave, synthpop, EBM, new beat and industrial. The name and the cohesive visual aesthetic arrive later: coined in 2005 by Veronica Vasicka with the Minimal Wave label, consolidated by 2010s reissue compilations and fan video edits. Visual lineage reaches back to constructivism and futurism and to science-fiction and existentialist literature.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
- Build monochrome first; add at most one accent, as flat field, thin stroke, or global sepia shift.
- Degrade the surface: grain, xerox contrast, halftone, static noise, letterbox bars, seam artifacts.
- Let type do structural work: stacked grotesque caps pinned to an edge or corner over empty field, or hand-scrawl; never decorative type effects.
- Pair one severe geometric or architectural element with one organic or hand-drawn element.
- Keep subjects deadpan and amateur; keep lighting flat, murky or harshly frontal.
- Cite an analog medium implicitly: photocopy, J-card, sleeve scan, film still.

Variation axes: subject (portrait, eyes, hands, architecture, apparatus diagram, film still, pure geometry, organic matter); composition (letterboxed still, flat scan, fragment cascade, feature grid, one-point perspective, void-with-small-center); color handling (pure mono, cool grey mono, warm sepia mono, mono plus pastel field, mono plus single saturated stroke); lighting (flat flash, underexposed available light, blown highlight on black, vignette); mood (melancholic, deadpan, eerie, wistful, clinically detached). A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a wireframe diagram on navy, a sepia domestic snapshot, or a pastel-field xerox collage are all equally valid though none resembles the others.

Avoid:
- Multi-hue palettes, gradients, neon glow, chrome or Y2K gloss, saturated fashion-editorial color.
- Ornate typography: blackletter, Victorian flourish, chrome script, gradient fills, drop shadows.
- Smiling, glamorous or sexualized posing; professional-model lighting.
- Pristine vector minimalism with no grain or print degradation.
- Punk chaos: ransom-note cut letters, safety pins, splatter, aggressive collage.
- Goth ornament: candles, crosses, lace, fog, graveyards.
- Industrial brutality: rust, meat, heavy machinery, sepia-brown corrosion textures.
- EBM club corporeality: leather, muscle, sweat, stencil militarism.
- Modern anachronisms: smartphones, current UI, contemporary logo design, crisp DSLR bokeh.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Minimal Wave |
|---|---|
| DIY Punk | Punk shares xerox grain but is loud, cut-and-paste, confrontational and splattered; Minimal Wave is cold, geometric, deadpan, with restrained type and vast empty field. |
| Electroclash | Electroclash is 2000s, glossy, kitsch-neon, chrome and sexual glamour; Minimal Wave is desaturated, grainy, austere, amateur. |
| Electronic Body Music | EBM imagery is club-bodied: leather, muscle, sweat, military stencil, often red/orange heat; Minimal Wave is bedroom-introverted, grey, technical-diagram minded. |
| Goth | Goth keeps romantic ornament — blackletter, religious iconography, fog, candles, purple and blood red; Minimal Wave is secular, sans-serif, anti-ornament. |
| Industrial | Industrial leans on rust, corrosion, machinery violence and brown-sepia noise; Minimal Wave's noise is tape hiss and photocopy, its geometry clean and its mood melancholic rather than menacing. |

When in doubt, apply the core test: does the image read as a photocopied, grainy, monochrome artifact of obscure early-1980s cassette electronics — detached, geometric, typographically stark, faintly mournful — with at most one accent color? If yes, it is Minimal Wave.
