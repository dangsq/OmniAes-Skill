# LOLcore

LOLcore — "LOL" welded to "-core" — is the sound of the mid-2010s fashion industry laughing at itself. Coined by fashion commentators and documented mostly in newspapers and magazine editorials, it describes a maximalist, humorous mode of dressing that quotes Pop Art and consumer culture directly and literally: fast-food uniforms and doll clothes recut as high fashion, cartoon faces blown up across bodycon dresses, candy shops treated as couture salons. It was a catwalk phenomenon first and a street style second, brief but loud, peaking in autumn 2014 as a deliberate counterattack on the understated seriousness of Normcore and early-2010s minimalism. Because it lived and died on runways and in styled editorials, nearly all surviving images are staged — runway, editorial, red carpet, window display, illustration — and casual candid registers are essentially undocumented; treat the red-carpet snapshot as the outer edge of its informality.

## Visual Identity

### Palette
Chroma before harmony. Every hue sits at or near maximum saturation with mid-to-high value; the working core is signal red (#E60023–#FF3B30), traffic yellow (#FFD400–#FFC72C), hot pink (#FF4FA3–#FF8FCB), tangerine (#FF6A13–#FF7F00), cobalt/royal blue (#0047AB–#26428B) and violet (#7B3FF2). Paper white (#FFFFFF) and ink black (#101010) appear only as print grounds, comic outlines, card-suit marks, or neutral studio/runway backdrops — never as the overall mood. Clashing is structural, not accidental: put complements or near-complements side by side (yellow against violet, orange against blue, red against pink) and refuse to mediate them with grey, beige or any desaturated peacemaker. The documented record leans red–yellow–pink–orange–blue–violet; green survives mostly as packaging accent rather than garment ground. A single saturated hero color carrying a whole look is acceptable only when that color bears a printed joke (a sponge-yellow dress with a face on it), never as plain monochrome elegance.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them
1. **Pop-culture prints on cloth** — cartoon characters, manga and comic-panel graphics with speech bubbles, monster faces. Prints cover entire garments edge to edge, not a small chest placement.
2. **Fast-food and consumer-brand references** — fry boxes, soda cups with straws, snack packaging, service uniforms, doll-style branding; McDonald's-, SpongeBob- and Barbie-coded vocabulary without requiring literal trademark fidelity.
3. **Oversized childlike accessories** that break body proportion: giant lollipops, plush mascot-head backpacks (lion, tiger), cartoon-character gloves, disc or helmet headpieces, enormous hoops.
4. **Clashing pattern layering** — animal print stacked with comic print stacked with patchwork and graphic stripe in one look; "organized chaos" as a method.
5. **Literal object-dresses** — outfits that become playing-card suits, gift wrap, candy wrappers, astronaut disc shells; doll clothes scaled up to couture.
6. **Consumer-abundance sets** — supermarket aisles, candy-shop counters, donut and cookie piles, sticker-plastered walls, store-window displays with packaging sculptures.
7. **Signature silhouettes** — bodycon mini dress, culottes, colorful faux-fur coat, patent-vinyl outerwear; platform shoes and white socks as the childish footnote.

### Materials and textures
Glossy patent vinyl and lacquered plastic (discs, shorts, raincoat sheen); transparent PVC or organza shells layered over solid color; plush faux fur and mascot pile; printed satin and cotton jersey carrying the graphics; foil wrappers, cardboard packaging and candy gloss in the sets; flat matte ink when the piece is an illustration. Shine and plush are the two tactile poles — nothing matte-rustic, nothing handwoven.

### Subjects, composition, lighting
The subject is almost always a dressed figure: runway model, celebrity on a red carpet, editorial model playing in a shop, or a group cast in matching branded uniforms; secondary subjects are the merchandise themselves (window displays, packaging sculptures, illustrated rooms hung with parody frames). Poses are goofy or deadpan — prop held to the mouth, wink, slouch, sprawl — never composed haute-couture gravitas. Composition fills the frame edge to edge with shelves, candy, stickers and print so that negative space reads as a mistake: centered full-body or three-quarter figures, straight-on runway shots, overhead sprawls on candy piles, one-point aisle perspectives, flat illustrated interiors. Props are pointed at the camera so the joke lands first. Lighting is bright, even and saturated: hard editorial flash with a warm overexposed glow in shop interiors, shadowless high-key studio white, runway spots on neutral grey, or no modeled light at all in flat illustration. Low-key, moody, dusk or golden-hour light does not exist in this style.

## Mood & Values
Humor, silliness, anti-minimalism, playful consumerism, self-mockery. LOLcore wears luxury as a cartoon to prove that seriousness was always optional: kitsch embraced on purpose, bad taste wielded as a critique of austerity, joy and irony ranked above refinement. It celebrates mass consumption while winking at it, turning everyday objects and pop icons into luxury goods exactly to expose how arbitrary the luxury was.

## Era & Origins
Emergent around 2014, peaked autumn 2014, gone soon after — a mid-2010s flash. It reacted directly against Normcore's deliberate averageness and the minimalist mood of the early 2010s. Jeremy Scott's Moschino collections (McDonald's, SpongeBob, Barbie) are its quintessential documents, with Kenzo, House of Holland, Meadham Kirchhoff, Olympia Le-Tan and Junya Watanabe in orbit, and bold-style celebrities — Katy Perry, Miley Cyrus, Rita Ora, Anna Dello Russo, Lily Allen — as its amplifiers.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Start from one joke told literally: choose a pop-culture or consumer object and build the garment as if that object were couture.
- Saturate first, harmonize never: place clashing complements adjacent and keep every hue near full chroma.
- Stack at least three loud elements: a clashing print, a solid high-chroma block, and one oversized childlike prop.
- Break proportion exactly where the comedy lives — the accessory should be absurdly large or absurdly literal.
- Set the figure inside abundance (aisles, candy, stickers) or on a neutral stage (studio white, runway grey) that makes the color scream.
- Keep the face in on the joke: goofy, deadpan or self-mocking, never reverent.
- Light flat and bright; let saturation do the modeling.

**Variation axes**
- Subject: runway model, red-carpet celebrity, editorial figure in a shop or aisle, uniformed group cast, flat illustrated woman, window-display still life, accessory still life.
- Composition: centered full-body, overhead candy sprawl, aisle one-point perspective, crowded group, illustrated interior, tight crop on prop plus print.
- Lighting: hard warm flash, shadowless studio, runway spot, flat graphic no-light.
- Color handling: all-over multi-clash, or one hero color carrying the look with smaller clashes inside it; white-ground print dresses are equally correct.
- Medium: glossy editorial photograph, runway photograph, candid red carpet, flat magazine illustration, retail-display photograph.
- Mood register: goofy, deadpan-ironic, or sweet-kitsch.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — chroma, clash, branded kitsch, oversized play, filled frame — not any reference image. A still life of a fry-box clutch on patent vinyl, or an illustrated room of parody frames, is as LOLcore as a candy-shop editorial.

**Avoid**
- Muted, earthy or desaturated palettes; beige, oatmeal, sage; any Normcore or quiet-luxury restraint.
- A single small logo on an otherwise plain garment; here the logo is the whole cloth.
- Moody chiaroscuro, dusk light, film-fade nostalgia, VHS grain.
- Pastel sugar dreamscapes with no branding and no punchline (that drifts to Candycore).
- Toy-clutter sincerity without couture construction (drifts to Kidcore).
- Ornate abundance with no humor and no pop-culture reference (drifts to Maximalism).
- Meme-graphic digital collage randomness (drifts to Internet Awesomesauce).
- Serious sexualized glamour poses, austere negative space, handcrafted rustic textures, or neutral monochrome outfits.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | How LOLcore differs |
|---|---|
| Candycore | Candycore is pastel, soft-textured, sweet-dreamy; LOLcore keeps candy at primary chroma, branded and worn as fashion with a punchline — candy is clutter and print, not atmosphere. |
| Snackwave | Snackwave is 80s–90s snack nostalgia in vaporwave dusk and VHS haze; LOLcore is 2010s, flash-bright, clean digital saturation, runway-bred, quoting logos for laughs now rather than mourning them. |
| Internet Awesomesauce | Awesomesauce humor is meme overlays and random internet graphics; LOLcore's humor is wearable and catwalk-born — garments, props, physical sets. |
| Kidcore | Kidcore's primaries carry childlike sincerity, crafts and casual clothes; LOLcore cuts its primaries into couture silhouettes (bodycon, faux fur, patent) with adult irony and designer provenance. |
| Maximalism | Maximalism piles abundance for its own sake, often ornate and vintage; LOLcore's abundance always cites pop culture or consumerism and lands a joke — maximalism is the method, not the message. |
| Pop Art | Pop Art is flat fine-art graphics with gallery lineage; LOLcore is Pop Art's vocabulary tailored into clothing and staged inside commerce. |
