# Figuration Narrative

Figuration Narrative arose in Paris in the early 1960s as a figurative, narrative counter-attack on two fronts: the institutional dominance of abstract painting, and the flood of American consumer imagery arriving with Pop Art. Its artists — Eduardo Arroyo, Bernard Rancillac, Hervé Télémaque, Valerio Adami, Erró, Gérard Fromanger, Jacques Monory, Peter Klasen, Gilles Aillaud, Peter Saul, Antonio Recalcati, Equipo Crónica among many others — kept the mass-media vocabulary of comics, cinema, advertising and press photography, but refused Pop's "art for art's sake" neutrality. Every image is meant to *tell*: to depict duration inside a static canvas, to quote history and headline, and to critique consumerism, war and dictatorship from a left-wing position that crystallized around May 1968. The movement consolidated through the exhibitions "Mythologies quotidiennes" (1964) and "La Figuration narrative dans l'art contemporain" (1965), and its core activity spans roughly 1960–1972.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The colour rule is **bold, contrasting, flat**: at least two high-saturation hues collide inside one field, and neither is modulated toward naturalism.

- Signal vermilion / red: `#D5382B` – `#E4572E` (often the entire ground)
- Cobalt / ultramarine blue: `#1E4FA3` – `#2A6BC9`
- Cadmium yellow: `#EFCC34` – `#F4D03F`
- Olive / military green: `#4C6B3C` – `#7A8A4A`
- Grounds and neutrals: paper off-white `#F2EFE6`, cream `#E7DCC3`, solid black `#121212`
- Cinematic register: monochrome value scales of grey and blue-black (`#3A3A3E` – `#8E8E93`) carrying a single saturated accent
- Minor poetic register: dusty pink and teal (`#E89AA6`, `#86C6C4`) for sparse object constellations on pale grounds

Saturation stays even from edge to edge; there is no atmospheric fading, no gradient sky, no local-colour drift. Black or dark contour lines separate planes.

### Signature motifs and subjects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Anonymous human figures in urban space** — streets, boulevards, staircases, shop windows, department-store fronts; crowds of repeated near-identical silhouettes; single isolated figures (a back, a pair of legs, a standing woman) against empty ground.
2. **Political imagery and social commentary** — soldiers and marines (Vietnam, the Antilles), dictator portraits (Franco), Chile, Cold War signage; the protest-poster inheritance of May 1968; hazard diamonds, traffic lights, flags and stencilled emblems used as political punctuation.
3. **Comic-strip syntax** — panel divisions, outlined bodies, truncated captions; the movement was explicitly tied to bande dessinée in its 1966 museum exhibition.
4. **Film stills and cinematic sequencing** — paintings organised like film sequences or single stills, sometimes with a painted "voice-over" of accompanying text; close-up insert shots (an eye, a hand, a shoe in a circular vignette) cut into wider scenes.
5. **Press photography and advertising** — magazine photographs superimposed on canvas, halftone fragments pasted into painted fields, consumer goods and logos quoted from the "everyday mythologies" of advertising.
6. **Polyptychs and collective narrative** — multi-panel works whose hinges break the story into episodes; the movement's manifesto was itself a three-handed polyptych on the "tragic end" of Marcel Duchamp.
7. **Duration devices** — simultaneous depiction of multiple spaces, superposition of planes, combination of different framings in one field, repeated offset outlines of a body to mark movement through time.
8. **Historical and art-historical quotation** — repainted old masters and modern masters, Lenin, Mayakovsky, a new form of history painting addressing contemporary politics through historical costume.
9. **Critical-realism subjects** — caged animals, fenced enclosures, exile rendered as discontinuous narratives of floating domestic objects arranged like constellations on a neutral ground.
10. **Everyday and cultural scenes** — fashion figures, mannequins, travel gear, musical iconography (jazz instruments, drum kits, double basses appear in the surviving corpus more than in the written record; treat music as a legitimate but minor subject).

### Materials, surface, texture

Matte oil or acrylic on canvas with a **printed-poster surface**: flat unmodulated planes, hard edges, crisp dark contours, no impasto and no gestural brushwriting. Photomechanical elements are welcome inside the paint: halftone dot grain, the cut edge of a magazine clipping, photographic reportage transferred to sensitized canvas, stencilled letterforms. Letterpress and headline typography is treated as a material — painted words, sliced slogans, partial sentences ("CAISE", "CHILI", fragmented verse) sit in the composition as objects, not as captions. The overall hand-feel is sign-painter and screenprinter, never painterly skin.

### Composition and lighting

- **Panel logic**: irregular grids, polyptych hinges, vertical band divisions; adjacent panels may clash in source and scale (a photographic eye beside a painted face beside a hazard sign).
- **Superposition**: planes stacked like pasted posters; objects overlap without sharing a perspective system; drawn scaffolds, grids and armatures may expose the construction of space.
- **Cropping**: cinematic — figures sliced by the frame edge, insert close-ups, wide shots interrupted by vignettes.
- **Ground**: either a single saturated field (vermilion, cobalt) or a neutral paper cream/grey; large empty ground around one isolated figure is correct, as is horror-vacui collage density. Both extremes belong to the style.
- **Depth and shadow**: no modelled light. Shadows are flat solid-colour silhouettes offset from the body; depth comes from overlap, scale jumps and panel sequence, not from perspective or chiaroscuro.
- **Lighting default**: even, shadowless poster illumination. Alternate register: cinematic monochrome noir (greys, blue-blacks) with one saturated accent, for film-still narratives. Impressionist or naturalistic light never appears.

## Mood & Values

The mood is **critical, literary and cool**: reportage distance mixed with parody, irony over pathos, melancholy in the anonymity of crowds and masked or blanked faces. The declared values are storytelling, freedom of expression, rebellion, anti-consumerism and social critique. Following Marcuse, the movement held that subversion lives in the *aesthetic* construction — montage, sequence, collision of sign systems — rather than in slogan-shouting; so even its most political images keep a formal wit, a chess-game cleverness, a novelist's pleasure in structure. Painting is treated as writing: Arroyo called his approach inherently literary, and Fromanger gave canvases a textual "voice-over."

## Era & Origins

Paris, 1962–1972 core. Narrative structures emerge around 1962 in Klasen and Télémaque; the name crystallizes with "Mythologies quotidiennes" (Musée d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, 1964) and Gassiot-Talabot's 1965 theorization, which defined narrative art as any representational work depicting *duration* — movement within the canvas, sequential imagery, polyptychs. The term itself predates the decade, borrowed from Peter Foldès' sequential animation. The movement forms in reaction to abstract painting's dominance and to the mediated arrival of American Pop (Sidney Janis 1962, Lichtenstein at Sonnabend), absorbing Pop's formal language while rejecting its political indifference; it participates directly in May 1968 through protest posters and slogans.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Paint flat: matte saturated planes, hard edges, dark contours; zero impasto, zero gradient.
2. Make the image *narrate*: imply before-and-after through sequence, panel division, repeated offset figures, superimposed planes, or clashing framings (close-up + long shot in one field).
3. Source from mass media: comic outlines, film-still cropping, halftone photo fragments, headline typography painted into the field as object-text.
4. Keep people anonymous: silhouettes, backs, masked or blanked faces, repeated crowd bodies; individuated psychological portraiture is out of register.
5. Charge the scene politically or socially — war, exile, consumer critique, dictatorship, street protest — but deliver it through parody, montage and aesthetic construction, not through realist sermonizing.
6. Set it in the mediated city: streets, shop windows, traffic signals, signage, staircases, boulevards; or in the interior of consumer myth (advertising glyphs, fashion, travel goods).
7. Let shadows be colour: cast shadows as flat solid silhouettes, never as tonal gradation.
8. Allow text: truncated letterpress words, slogans, place names, fragments of verse, functioning visually and as voice-over.

**Variation axes (what may change)**

- *Subject*: street crowd, soldier at war, dictator parody, caged animal, isolated legs or back, floating exile objects, chessboard of props, jazz instruments, fashion mannequins, repainted old master, protest march. Any of these — or any other everyday or political scene — is admissible.
- *Composition*: single cinematic frame; two- or three-panel polyptych; dense irregular collage grid; sparse constellation of objects on cream ground; horizontal film-strip sequence.
- *Colour handling*: full-primary saturation on white or black; monochrome vermilion field; grey/blue cinematic monochrome with one accent; cream ground with red and black; rare pastel constellation register.
- *Lighting*: flat poster light (default) or monochrome noir value drama; never naturalistic.
- *Mood*: ironic parody, cold reportage, poetic melancholy, agitatory urgency.
- *Density*: from one isolated figure in empty space to horror-vacui panel collage.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — flat bold colour, mass-media syntax, narrative duration, critical stance — not any reference image.

**Avoid**

- Gestural brushwork, impasto, drips, visible painterly stroke (the handling is printed, not painterly).
- Celebratory or neutral consumer imagery with no critical or narrative inflection (that is Pop, not this).
- Naturalistic modelling, chiaroscuro, academic finish, sentimental individuated faces (Social Realism / salon register).
- Uniform Ben-Day dots and a regular comic-page grid as the *whole* look (Four Colors pastiche); here comics are one source among photo, film, text and art history, and paneling is irregular.
- Real three-dimensional objects, accumulations, torn-poster assemblage materiality (Nouveau Réalisme); this style translates the city into painted/printed image.
- Gradients, glossy digital rendering, airbrush blending, soft-focus bokeh.
- Pure abstraction with no figuration or no narrative device.
- Decorative pattern or ornament for its own sake; every plane should carry story or critique.
- Impressionist light, sunset skies, atmospheric perspective.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | Shared surface | Figuration Narrative tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Pop Art** | Flat bold colour, comic and ad sourcing | FN adds narrative duration (sequence, polyptych, superposition), painted text as voice-over, and explicit political subjects (war, dictators, exile); Pop stays celebratory or deadpan-neutral about consumption, FN critiques it |
| **New Figuration** | Return to the figure after abstraction | FN is narrower: graphic, mass-media-derived, story-driven; New Figuration may be painterly, gestural, existential, with distorted bodies and no narrative or media syntax |
| **Nouveau Réalisme** | Urban consumer detritus as subject | NR keeps the *actual object* (accumulations, compressions, torn posters); FN repaints and reprints the city as flat image and subordinates it to a story |
| **Social Realism** | Political commitment, workers and crowds | SR models bodies naturalistically with empathetic chiaroscuro in an academic lineage; FN is flat, ironic, collage-built, quoting comics and cinema instead of observing life directly |
| **Four Colors** | Comic line and primary palette | Four Colors simulates vintage comic printing consistently (dots, uniform line weight, regular panels, genre fiction); FN mixes photo, halftone, letterpress text and painted planes in irregular panels, with critical content and matte painterly flatness |

Quick recognition checklist: flat matte planes in clashing saturated hues with black contours; anonymous or masked figures in urban or political space; panel/polyptych or superimposed construction implying time; painted typographic fragments; parody and critique delivered through montage. If all four fire, it is Figuration Narrative.
