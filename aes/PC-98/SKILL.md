# PC-98

PC-98 names the screen art of NEC's PC-9800 series, the personal computer that owned Japan from the early 1980s into the late 1990s because IBM-compatible machines could not set Japanese type. Its games — visual novels (the term was coined here, by *Shizuku*), RPGs, strategy titles, and a notorious library of eroge — developed a pixel art grammar unlike anything in the West: relatively high resolution for its time (up to 640×400), a screen palette of only 16 to 256 colors pulled from a 4096-color master, and dithering used obsessively to fake gradients, depth and blush. The result is an intimate, melancholy, hand-placed look: anime faces rendered pixel by pixel inside game screens framed by ornate borders and dialogue bands. Today it survives as a nostalgia object — emulation circles, doujin developers still shipping for the hardware, and descendants from Vaporwave to *World of Horror* and *16bit Sensation*.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** Every color sits on a quantized grid: pick a small set (as few as 16, at most 256) from a 4096-color master and never exceed it. Gradients never blend; they step through two to four banded tones bridged by checkerboard or Bayer-pattern dither. Recurring families, any of which may dominate a given image: machine cool (deep petrol teal `#0e3b46`, mid cyan `#2e8b94`, pale mint `#9fd8d4`) for cockpits, circuitry, uniforms and metal; romance and UI hot (magenta `#c2266e`, rose `#e86ea0`, blush `#ffb3d1`) for menus, portraits and love scenes; sunset vermilion (brick `#8a1e1e`, orange `#d94f30`, amber `#ff9a3c`) for backlit dusk; pastel fantasy (lavender `#b9a7e0`, mint `#a8e0c8`, cream `#f2e8dc`) for outdoor and dream sequences; and the constants — pure ink black `#000000` for voids, borders and dialogue bands, bone white `#f5f0e6` for bitmap text and eye highlights. Saturated mid-tones over black grounds are the norm; desaturated filmic grading is not.

**Pixel grammar and texture.** Square, countable pixels on a 640×400 logical grid; one-pixel dark outlines around every form. Materials are declared by dither pattern: brushed metal as parallel interleaved bands with a single specular dot, skin as a flat tone plus a cheek-blush dither patch, hair as a flat fill with one crescent highlight band, CRT glass as one diagonal white streak, fabric as flat color with a single hard-edged dithered shadow step. There is no anti-aliasing, no soft shadow, no blur — only pattern density.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the tradition documents them.** (1) Dithering and quantized color as the defining technique. (2) High-resolution, highly detailed anime pixel art. (3) Character close-ups and face portraits — the art "tends to focus" on them. (4) Distinct game UI: thick decorative borders (often patterned as circuit boards, filigree or metal plates), stylized bitmap fonts, custom menus of slot grids, and a bottom dialogue band carrying white Japanese text; kanji label plates with a small English gloss ("Information Sect") appear as diegetic signage. (5) 1980s–90s anime face grammar: large glossy eyes with two or more highlight dots, sharp small chins, tiny mouths, heavy eyelash lines. (6) The eroge lineage's intimate gaze — close framing, private rooms, charged silence; the sampled material keeps this at the level of intimacy and emotional proximity rather than explicit content. (7) Period hardware as ornament: printed circuit traces, floppy disks, CRT terminals, even cartridge and bullet shapes woven into border decoration — a secondary motif, supported by the platform's hardware history. (8) As cultural halo rather than visual element, the bell-like FM synthesis chiptune (Yamaha OPN/OPNA) imaginary: bright, chime-like, bittersweet.

**Subjects.** Visual-novel encounters and dialogue moments; cockpit and mecha interiors with seated crews; offices with CRT monitors, swivel chairs and blinds; schoolrooms, corridors and uniforms; city streets at dusk; fantasy fields, forests and witch hats; domestic interiors; single-character head-and-shoulders portraits; and sparse tactical or RPG screens where tiny sprites and columns of numerals float in black void.

**Composition and lighting.** The canvas is a game screen, usually 640×400 (16:10), often letterboxed by black bands. Four recurring layouts: a full-bleed event still with a bottom dialogue band taking roughly a fifth of the height; a windowed screen with a decorative outer border, an inner scene window, a portrait inset overlapping one edge and menu panels beside it; a portrait close-up filling the frame with minimal chrome; and the bare tactical screen of sprites and stats on black. Framing is frontal and stage-like, medium-close, rarely extreme in angle. Lighting is flat ambient with a single implied key: cool fluorescent teal interiors, warm sunset backlight throwing silhouette rims, night neon in magenta, pastel overcast daylight, or near-monochrome noir sets. Light intensity is expressed by dither density, never by glow.

## Mood & Values

Intimacy, emotional connection, melancholy, nostalgia, and an erotic charge that usually manifests as closeness rather than display. A PC-98 image feels like a confidential moment paused mid-sentence: someone looking at you across a desk, a cockpit crew waiting, a confession under a vermilion sky. Underneath runs "time capsule" sentiment — craft pride in hand-placed pixels, and the bittersweet brightness of FM chimes echoing in an empty room after school.

## Era & Origins

Japan, 1980s to late 1990s, on NEC PC-9800 hardware (µPD7220 display controller, 4096-color palette, scarce video RAM). The scarcity is the style: limited VRAM and dense Japanese glyph rendering forced dithering and economized palettes, producing a visual language parallel to but distinct from Western Pixel UI. Dominance faded in the early 1990s when foreign manufacturers finally addressed the Japanese market; the last machine shipped in 2000. The platform birthed the visual novel and the Touhou Project, and its preservation scene keeps it alive as a capsule of Japanese computing culture.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Commit to the pixel grid: logical resolution around 640×400, square countable pixels, one-pixel outlines; nothing off-grid.
2. Quantize first, then dither: choose a small palette (16–256 colors), band every gradient, bridge bands with checker or Bayer patterns.
3. Build a *screen*, not just a picture: decide whether this is an event still with dialogue band, a bordered windowed UI with portrait inset and menus, a portrait close-up, or a sparse tactical void.
4. Treat Japanese bitmap text as graphic material: white kanji lines in a black band, kanji-plus-English label plates.
5. Draw faces in 90s anime grammar: big multi-highlight eyes, sharp chin, heavy lash line, banded hair highlight.
6. Light with dither density and hard shadow patches; keep one dominant color family, one accent, plus black and paper white.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any of sci-fi cockpit, office, school, domestic room, city dusk, fantasy wilderness, horror interior, portrait study. Composition: any of the four layouts above, portrait insets left or right, borders ornate or austere. Lighting: fluorescent cool, sunset backlight, neon night, pastel daylight, monochrome noir. Color handling: teal machine sets, magenta romance sets, vermilion dusk sets, pastel fantasy sets, grayscale sets. Mood: melancholy, intimacy, dread, wonder, deadpan humor. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a never-before-seen scene, rendered on-grid with quantized color and game chrome, is fully PC-98.

**Avoid.** Smooth gradients, airbrush shading, soft shadows, bloom, lens flare, chromatic aberration; anti-aliasing or sub-pixel edges; photographic texture or true-color film looks; modern HD anime rendering with gradient hair and thin lines; chunky 8-bit blockiness and coarse NES-grade palettes without dither finesse; Western gray-bevel desktop chrome and modern flat UI (rounded corners, drop shadows, sans-serif web fonts); Vaporwave statues and grid suns; glossy rain-slick cyberpunk rendering; ultrawide cinematic aspect ratios; palettes exceeding a few hundred colors; hand-drawn wobble line or painterly brushwork.

## Discrimination Cues

- **8-Bit:** coarser logical resolution, blockier sprites, harder palettes with little or crude dithering, simplified chibi proportions; PC-98 is fine-grained 640×400 pixel work with dithered gradients, detailed anime faces and dense Japanese dialogue bands.
- **Cyberpunk:** a content genre renderable in any medium (photo, 3D, smooth neon renders); PC-98 is a medium-bound grammar — it can depict cyberpunk subjects but only through quantized pixels, dither and game chrome, never through lens effects or teal-orange film grading.
- **Heisei Retro:** broader 90s Japanese nostalgia spanning photography, cel anime, VHS and print, with film grain and true color; PC-98 is strictly digital screen art — pixel grid, dither, UI — and never photographic.
- **Pixel UI:** Western desktop aesthetic of gray beveled windows, austere productivity chrome and Latin bitmap sans; PC-98 UI is ornamental, patterned, anime-integrated, and set in kanji bitmap fonts inside a game world.
- **Animecore:** 90s OVA cel look with continuous resolution, clean cel paint and cinematic framing, no pixel grid and no interface; PC-98 shares the face grammar but everything snaps to a countable grid, shades by dither, and usually carries dialogue bands or menu chrome.

One honesty note: period print ephemera (hardware advertisements) exists around this culture but is peripheral context; the core grammar above is screen art, and the intimate/erotic value documented in the tradition appears in surviving public material as closeness of framing rather than explicit depiction.
