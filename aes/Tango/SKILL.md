# Tango

Tango is an aesthetic that grew out of a music and dance genre born in the 1880s in the ports and poor quarters of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, where lower-class European immigrants and formerly enslaved African peoples mixed their cultures. Though primarily music-based, it carries a strong visual arm: images of the dance itself (literal or abstracted into colour and silhouette), the instruments that sound it, and the urban world that bred it — cobblestones, docks, brothels, dance halls. Its emotional core is tragic romance: passion, sexuality, freedom, and a continual playing with and against gender roles, from the traditional leader/follower pair to the open-role and same-sex "queer tango" that exists from the genre's earliest days. Visually the style oscillates between three registers — candle-black chiaroscuro painting, grainy black-and-white documentary photography, and flat vintage poster lithography — all held together by one palette rule: red as a wound or a flag, set into a field of black and bone white.

## Visual Identity

### Colour palette

The infobox key colours are **red, black, white**, and every register of the style obeys that triad, treating red as a concentrated accent rather than a background wash.

| Role | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Red (accent, never field) | `#7A0C0C` oxblood → `#D21F2B` scarlet | dress, rose, lip, lettering, shoe; one red locus per image is typical |
| Black (ground) | `#0B0B0D` → `#241F1A` warm black | suits, voids, night interiors; warm-toned, never cool blue-black |
| White / bone (ground) | `#F2EDE3` → `#E8DFC9` | shirt fronts, poster paper, cream borders, sepia highlights |
| Support earth tones | `#4A3B2E` umber, `#6B4A32` sepia, `#C89B3C` ochre | skin light, aged paper, bar wood, architecture |
| Poster sky / architecture | `#3E6E8E` muted teal, `#7A8CA0` grey-blue | flat travel-poster backgrounds only |
| Abstract exemption | iridescent lavender/pink/pearl (`#C9A6D6`, `#E8B7C0`) | allowed only in the documented "abstract tango art" variant, where dancer shapes dissolve into holographic colour |

Rule of thumb: high contrast is mandatory, either in value (black void vs. lit skin) or in hue (scarlet vs. bone). Muddy mid-tone realism with no red locus reads as generic, not tango.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how firmly the text documents them)

1. **The dancing couple** — "the most common visuals are those related to the dance": photographs or paintings of people dancing, or abstract compositions using only the colours and general shapes of dancers. The embrace is the style's true subject.
2. **Instruments, above all the bandoneón** — the accordion-like box the text calls "an integral part of tango music"; then guitar, violin, flute, piano, the full *Orquesta Típica* or a lone player.
3. **Origin geography** — cobblestone streets; brothels and ports, especially poor-city quarters; the dockside world of La Guardia Vieja.
4. **Concert and dance halls** — the milonga interior: parquet or checkered tile floors, tall windows, bar façades with glazed signs and globe lamps.
5. **Roses, especially red roses** — held, thrown, or tucked behind an ear.
6. **Argentine patriotic symbols** — the flag, the Obelisco of Buenos Aires; poster lettering naming Argentina or Buenos Aires.

Image-corroborated secondary motifs: fedora and wide-brimmed hats; the singer's portrait with a flower in the hair (Gardel/Merello-era star iconography); wrought-iron street lamps; cigarette smoke; suspenders and shirt sleeves in open-role practice scenes; worn poster edges and print distress.

### Materials and textures

Commit to one medium register and hold it throughout: **oil paint** with loaded, visible brushwork and glazed dark grounds; **litho / gouache poster** with flat shapes, hard edges, cream paper grain, creased and sun-faded borders; **gelatin silver print** with film grain and soft documentary contrast; **loose watercolor** for early-20th-century illustration manners. Object textures that sell the style: bandoneón wood, mother-of-pearl buttons and bellows folds; wool suiting and satin sheen; fringe and slit silk on dresses; fishnet; worn leather shoes; cobblestone and chipped tile; aged paper.

### Recurring subjects

Paired dancers in close embrace — man/woman, man/man, woman/woman (open-role and same-sex pairing is internal to the style, documented from its origin, not a variant of it); a solitary musician bent over a bandoneón or guitar; a golden-age singer in star-portrait framing; street and hall scenes of social dancing; still-life condensations (rose, hat, bandoneón on a chair); and fully abstract colour-field evocations of dancer silhouettes.

### Composition and lighting

Composition: two bodies fused into **one interlocked vertical silhouette**, usually in portrait format, isolated against a void, a flat colour field, or a sparse architectural backdrop. Diagonal energy comes from the legs — hooked ganchos, flicked boleos, a lifted foot breaking the outline — while the torsos stay pressed and axial. Heads turn cheek-to-cheek or fling back; gazes go inward, closed or downcast, never greeting the viewer (singer portraits excepted). Poster variants add bold lettering bands top or bottom and a cream margin.

Lighting: three sanctioned registers — (a) **tenebrist single source**: near-black ground, one warm key raking skin, satin and shirt cuff, figures carved out of darkness; (b) **flat even poster light**, no modelling, shape against shape; (c) **overcast or window natural light** in monochrome documentary scenes. All three keep contrast high.

## Mood & Values

Romance, tragic love, passion, freedom, sexuality, and the simultaneous following and subverting of gender roles. The mood is intimacy under pressure: melancholy, defiance, longing held in a rigid embrace — a dance that was banned across Europe for being "too sexual" and wears that taboo as honour. Nostalgia for the golden age (1930–1952, Gardel, Perón-era Argentine cinema) coexists with port-quarter grit. Joy here is never breezy; even social milonga scenes carry concentration, pride and a slight ache.

## Era & Origins

Emergent 1880s in the Río de la Plata ports (La Guardia Vieja, 1880s–1910s); exported in the 1900s–1910s "European tango craze" (Paris, London, Berlin, New York) with its scandal and bans; consolidated in the Golden Age, 1930–1952, under the first Perón government alongside the Golden Age of Argentine cinema; renewed from the 1980s by tango nuevo and neotango (Piazzolla, Gotan Project), which licenses contemporary urban settings. Visual period signifiers therefore range from 1890s dockside roughness through 1920s–40s suit-and-satin glamour to timeless black, plus retro poster typography (Art Deco and early-century serif capitals) that may be applied to modern subjects.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Build the image around the embrace or its trace: a dancing pair, an instrument still warm from hands, a rose on an empty chair — the dance must be felt even when absent.
2. Keep the red–black–white spine; concentrate red in one locus (dress, rose, lip, lettering) against black or bone.
3. Fuse two bodies into a single silhouette; let legs supply the diagonals and the torsos supply the pressure.
4. Choose one medium register (oil chiaroscuro, litho poster, silver print, watercolor) and keep its texture consistent edge to edge.
5. Anchor the scene in tango geography: cobblestones, docks, brothel-quarter walls, milonga parquet or checkered tile, bar façade with globe lamps, Obelisco or flag as distant signifier.
6. Direct the gaze inward — closed eyes, downcast lids, cheek to cheek — except in deliberate star-portrait mode.
7. If using type, use period capitals with print wear; lettering is a poster convention, not a logo overlay.

**Variation axes (all of these remain inside the style)**

- *Subject*: heterosexual, same-sex or open-role couples; solo instrumentalist; singer portrait; social-dance crowd; still life; pure abstraction of dancer shapes and colours.
- *Composition*: full-length vertical pair; cropped embrace close-up; dynamic lift or hook; static apilado stillness; poster layout with lettering bands; empty-hall tableau.
- *Lighting*: spotlight tenebrism; candle or bar-lamp glow; flat poster light; overcast monochrome daylight.
- *Colour*: full red/black/white; sepia or pure monochrome; muted earth tones with a single red; iridescent pastel abstraction only.
- *Era*: 1880s port grit, 1920s–40s glamour, or contemporary neotango (subway car, plaza, street flash-mob) in monochrome or desaturated-with-red handling.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — palette spine, embrace logic, medium consistency, mood — not any reference image. A painterly abstract colour field with no legible figures and a crisp modern subway scene in black-and-white are equally valid tangos.

**Avoid**

- Tropical salsa/carnival codes: maracas, congas, palm trees, confetti multicolour, neon, sequined carnival bodies.
- Flamenco codes: ruffled polka-dot bata de cola, castanets, hand fans, Andalusian patio staging (the guitar is shared; the rest is not).
- Ballroom or lindy-hop codes: open separated frame with bouncing space, sporty kicks, knee socks, grinning exuberance, rise-and-float posture.
- Romance-kitch: pink hearts, soft-focus wedding glow, cuddling sweetness — tango intimacy is tragic, not cute.
- Cool film-noir grading (blue-grey shadows) without the red locus and the embrace; noir borrows the fedora but not the chest-to-chest press.
- Modern club lighting: lasers, LED wash, EDM haze, glossy 3D-render sheen, glassmorphism, corporate gradients.
- Generic "Latin" props unattached to Argentina: sombreros, mariachi, cacti, tacos.
- Frontal smiling couples posing at the camera in dance hold; the tango gaze is absorbed, not performative for the lens.
- Candy pastel palettes outside the documented abstract variant.

## Discrimination Cues

The source records no named neighbour styles, so these cues are drawn from the visual evidence and the style's own definitions. Against **flamenco**: tango is a partnered close embrace in urban interiors or streets, with satin/fringe dresses and bandoneón; flamenco is solo-bodied, ruffled, castanet-driven, patio-lit. Against **ballroom waltz or swing**: tango keeps bent knees, staccato leg hooks, head snaps and chest contact — no floating glide, no open bouncing frame. Against **film noir**: shared chiaroscuro and fedoras, but noir centres crime narrative and cool greys; tango centres the embrace and warms its blacks with ochre skin light and a red accent. Against **generic vintage travel posters**: tango posters always carry a dance, instrument, rose or Buenos Aires signifier and the red–black–white spine, not merely scenic prettiness. Against **salsa/cumbia aesthetics**: tango's posture is pressed and inward, its palette austere; salsa's is open, hip-driven and polychrome. Note finally that same-sex and open-role pairing is not a separate style but a documented strand of tango itself — an image of two men or two women in close embrace, in this palette and mood, is fully inside the style.
