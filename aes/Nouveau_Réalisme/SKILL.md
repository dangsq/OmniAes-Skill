# Nouveau Réalisme

Nouveau Réalisme ("New Realism") is the French movement founded in Paris in 1960 by critic Pierre Restany and painter Yves Klein, active until 1970 and closely associated with the École de Nice. It grew out of post-war France's booming consumer society and declared war on abstract painting by refusing to *represent* reality at all: instead of painting a thing, the artist presents the thing itself — a torn poster pulled from a street wall, a heap of identical manufactured objects, a crushed automobile, the remains of a dinner glued to a table, a machine built from scrap that moves and destroys itself. The movement's motto-like idea is a "poetic recycling of reality": urban, industrial and advertising detritus is appropriated directly into the artwork, carrying its own wear, print layers and history with it. The result is a style that looks less like authored imagery and more like evidence — sociological samples of mass production, displayed deadpan in the white cube or documented in harsh black-and-white during happenings.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette is not one harmony but three coexisting registers, all governed by a single rule: **saturation must arrive from printed ink, raw pigment or manufactured plastic, never from painterly mixing.**

1. **Poster stratigraphy (dominant register).** A warm, aged paper ground — cream, bone, beige (`#E9DFC8` to `#F4ECD8`) — shredded open to expose saturated printer's inks: flag/vermilion red (`#C8102E`–`#E4002B`), cobalt and ultramarine blue (`#002FA7`–`#1B4BB4`), cadmium and ochre yellow (`#E9B44C`–`#F2C230`), magenta and rose (`#D6006E`–`#F279A8`), emerald green (`#00843D`–`#3FA757`), tangerine orange (`#E8720C`). Colors appear as torn shards, faded sun-bleached patches and halftone dot screens, never as clean designed fields.
2. **Monochrome and ink register.** Deep matte ultramarine — International Klein Blue, around `#002FA7` to `#1F00A8` — as full field or as imprinted wash on cream paper; plus documentary grayscale from near-black to paper white (`#0D0D0D`–`#E6E6E6`) for machine and happening photographs.
3. **Object and documentation register.** Neutral gallery white and gray (`#F4F4F2`, `#C9C9C9`), dark pedestal browns and near-blacks (`#2B2320`), aluminum and oxidized metal grays, against which consumer objects show their own colors: pastel plastics (mint, lemon, coral, sky blue) or kitsch Pop blocks of red, green and pink.

Allowable range runs from full polychrome poster chaos to a single saturated monochrome field to pure grayscale; what must stay constant is the *source* of the color (print, pigment, plastic) and its contrast against weathered or neutral grounds.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Everyday, discarded and industrial objects presented directly** — the movement's core: household items, cosmetics, spools, utensils, coins, tickets, rags, sheet metal, automobile parts, appliance housings, plastic novelties.
2. **Décollage / lacerated posters** — torn street advertising stripped in layers: fragmented display typography (partial words, amputated letters), halftone cinema and advertisement faces of women cut mid-feature, ghost layers of earlier posters bleeding through, fibrous white tear edges.
3. **Accumulations** — serial repetition of one identical manufactured object massed into clusters, columns, grids or walls; humorous and somber at once.
4. **Compressions** — objects, classically automobiles, crushed into dense solid blocks (strongly documented in text; thin in the surviving image sample).
5. **Assemblage and kinetic scrap machines** — wire, rods, spheres, wheels and mechanical arms joined into precarious moving contraptions, usually seen in high-contrast black-and-white.
6. **Monochrome blue canvases and imprint works** — Klein's raw-pigment blue fields, body imprints (anthropometries) and weather-marked cosmogonies.
7. **Trap pictures** — meal remains, plates, cutlery and cloth fixed exactly where the dinner ended, often on checkered tablecloth.
8. **Pop-inflected figuration and plastic** — kitsch female portraits with airbrushed skin, flat saturated color blocks, attached plastic objects and neon-bright accessories (Raysse's bridge toward Pop).
9. **Emballage (wrapped objects) and shooting-painting splatter** — text-documented techniques (Christo's wrappings, Niki de Saint Phalle's fired paint bags) that belong to the style's rule set even where the image record is sparse.

### Materials and textures

Torn paper with frayed white fiber edges; glued paper stratigraphy with visible overlaps and delamination; flaking printed ink and halftone dot screens; matte raw pigment that reads as powder, not gloss; dusty and scratched plastic; oxidized, greased or rusted metal; wire and rod; cloth; dried food debris; crushed and folded sheet metal. Every surface is **indexical**: it records a physical operation — a tear, a crush, a spill, an imprint, a wrap, a gunshot, an assembly — rather than a depicted image.

### Recurring subjects

Mass-produced consumer goods; street advertising and cinema publicity (especially female stars' faces); urban wall surfaces; meal leftovers; machine scrap; the human body as imprint or silhouette; packaged and wrapped parcels; grids of identical units.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

- **Décollage register:** full-frame, edge-to-edge surface with no horizon and no focal hierarchy; composition is decided by the tear, not the designer; letters run off edges; faces are interrupted mid-eye or mid-mouth.
- **Object register:** centered, frontal, near-symmetrical presentation of an assemblage or accumulation on a pedestal or wall, surrounded by generous neutral negative space — the white cube as witness.
- **Serial register:** grids, rows or columns of repeated units filling the frame with rhythmic excess.
- **Machine/happening register:** dark ambient space, silhouetted mechanical chaos, smoke or motion, hard spotlight contrast, grayscale.
- **Lighting:** even, diffuse, shadow-soft documentation light for objects; flat frontal or slightly raking light that reveals paper relief for poster surfaces; harsh single-source contrast for kinetic and performance work. Color temperature stays neutral; there is no atmospheric or romantic light.

## Mood & Values

Deadpan, sociological, anti-aesthetic. The work behaves like evidence filed from consumer society: curious and affectionate about mass production while critically recycling it. Humor and morbidity coexist (a heap of dental prosthetics is both joke and memento mori). Values are **appropriation of the real, social commentary, anti-consumerism and collective singularity** — many hands, one shared refusal to idealize. Nothing is beautified by taste; beauty, when it appears, leaks out of the material's own history. The mood can swing from clinical inventory to kitsch playfulness to mechanical menace, but never toward lyricism, romance or heroic narrative.

## Era & Origins

Emerges in France around 1960 (Restany's declaration signed at Klein's apartment), with roots in the 1940s French social-realist opposition to abstract painting; first shown at the Paris Avant-Garde Festival in November 1960 and at Milan's Galerie Apollinaire, then New York and the 1963 San Marino Biennale. The formal period closes with Klein's death in 1962; the group disperses by 1970. It is the French counterpart to American Pop Art and a sibling of Fluxus, reacting simultaneously against abstract painting's inwardness and against the new disposable consumer landscape it documents.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. *Present, don't represent:* the artwork is the object, poster fragment or debris itself, not a painted picture of one.
2. *Indexical surface:* every mark must read as the trace of a physical operation — tear, crush, spill, imprint, wrap, shot, glue, rust.
3. *Stratigraphy over design:* composition emerges from accumulated layers and piled objects, never from arranged ornament.
4. *Seriality and density:* repeat, mass, pile, compress; one object is a readymade, five hundred are Nouveau Réalisme.
5. *Fragmented typography:* advertising letters appear as torn shapes and partial words, legible in shards, never as intact designed messaging.
6. *Consumer color, neutral stage:* printed-ink saturation and plastic pastels set against aged paper or white-cube neutrality; or the legitimate extreme of a single raw-pigment blue field.
7. *Documentary stance:* lighting and framing behave like archive or gallery documentation — frontal, even, unsentimental — or like harsh black-and-white event photography.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any mass-produced or urban realia — food remains, cosmetics, tools, tickets, coins, textiles, appliances, automobiles, posters, scrap machines, wrapped parcels, body imprints. Composition: full-frame torn surface, centered pedestal object, serial grid or column, shaped/cut support, wall installation, dark machine silhouette. Lighting: diffuse white-cube, flat frontal on paper relief, raking tear-light, hard grayscale spotlight. Color: polychrome poster shards on cream, monochrome IKB, full grayscale, pastel plastic cluster, flat Pop red/green blocks. Mood: deadpan inventory, kitsch play, somber accumulation, mechanical chaos. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a wrapped appliance, a column of bottle caps or a torn tram advertisement never seen in the references is fully valid if it obeys the rules above.

**Avoid.** Painterly gestural brushwork and lyrical abstraction (the enemy the movement formed against); clean silkscreen or Ben-Day reproduction with crisp pristine edges (that is Pop, not décollage); intact, fully legible advertising posters with no tear or layering; noble materials handled reverently (bronze, marble, gold leaf); digital gloss, CGI sheen, gradient renders, neon-futurist palettes; painted narrative scenes of workers or social conditions (Social Realism); pastoral organic humility — soil, twigs, live animals as primary matter (Arte Povera's territory; Nouveau Réalisme's poor materials are urban and industrial); cartoon line art and vinyl-toy gloss (Neo-Pop); ornamental symmetry, decorative pattern, handwritten expressive lettering; product photography that celebrates the commodity as desirable rather than filing it as evidence.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Arte Povera:** both use humble materials, but Povera's are organic and earthy — soil, rags, twigs, lettuce, animals — with an ephemeral, anti-industrial poetry and natural monochrome tones. Nouveau Réalisme's matter is the city: printed advertising, plastic, metal, machines, and it keeps the saturated noise of consumer print.
- **Figuration Narrative:** FN *paints* flat, graphic, politically legible figures and scenes. Nouveau Réalisme *presents* the real artifact — an actual torn poster, an actual object — so its imagery is fragmented, indexical and non-narrative rather than a composed painted story.
- **Pop Art:** Pop reproduces commercial imagery with cool, pristine finish — silkscreen, acrylic, Ben-Day dots, crisp edges. Nouveau Réalisme appropriates the physical commercial artifact itself: weathered, torn, glued, layered, sun-faded. Pop's surface is a clean print; Nouveau Réalisme's surface is street-worn stratigraphy. (Raysse's kitsch portraits are the shared border: flat Pop color blocks, but with attached plastic objects and French advertisement sources.)
- **Neo-Pop:** Neo-Pop (1980s onward) is glossy, cartoonish, vector-clean, hyper-saturated and toy-like. Nouveau Réalisme is analog, halftone-grained, paper-and-metal, historically anchored in 1960s print technology and gallery documentation.
- **Social Realism:** the 1940s precursor current (and earlier 19th-century realism) depicts social conditions through illusionistic painted scenes with moral narrative and earthy chiaroscuro. Nouveau Réalisme inherits the anti-idealization but abandons the painted scene entirely: reality is not depicted, it is mounted, torn, piled and trapped.
