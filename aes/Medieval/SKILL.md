# Medieval

## Overview

The Medieval style — the Middle Ages — spans roughly the 5th to the 15th century, from the fall of Rome to the arrival of Renaissance perspective, and behaves less like one school than like an umbrella over several: Insular, Carolingian, Ottonian, Romanesque and the regional Gothics. Its two declared key values are **Christianity** and **feudalism**, and nearly every formal decision in the style serves one of those two orders: the divine hierarchy and the social hierarchy. The surviving visual record concentrates in four media — manuscript illumination on vellum, wall painting under arcades, woven wool tapestry, and goldsmith work set with enamel and gems — and all four share one conviction: the picture is a decorated, luminous *surface*, not a window into space. Note honestly that the source text leaves most sub-school sections as stubs, so the fine-grained motif ranking below leans on the two key values and the named sub-schools, with palette, texture and material detail drawn from the surviving artifacts themselves.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** Work from a jewel-and-metal range, never from observed nature: burnished gold leaf (#C9A227–#E3C567) as ground or halo; lapis/ultramarine blues (#1E3F8F–#2E5AAC); madder and vermilion reds (#8E2323–#C0392B); verdigris and malachite greens (#3E7C59–#6DA07B); rose pink (#D98A9A); enamel turquoise (#2F8F83); pearl white (#F2EDE4); steel greys for armour (#7C7F82–#A8ABAD); and neutral carriers of vellum cream (#EFE6CF–#D9CBA8) or oak-gall ink brown-black (#241C15) for contour lines. Saturation may run from full jewel intensity (illumination, tapestry) to chalky fresco fade (wall painting), but hue families stay within this set.

**Ground and light.** Light is not directional; it is a property of materials. Gold leaf *is* the light source — punched or tooled into halos and backgrounds — and pigments glow evenly with no cast shadows, no chiaroscuro, no atmospheric depth. Choose one ground type per image: tooled gold, plain parchment cream, a solid saturated field (red or blue), or an all-over millefleurs scatter of small flowers on red.

**Line and figure.** Contour first, volume second: figures are drawn in firm ink outline and filled with flat or lightly modelled tempera. Faces are pale ovals with small mouths, almond eyes, reddish-gold or brown hair in stylized curls or braids; bodies are elongated, hands slender and gestural. Drapery falls in calligraphic fold-lines rather than observed cloth physics.

**Motifs, ranked by textual prominence.**
1. *Christian apparatus* (first key value): the cross — plain, processional, or gem-set; halos; angels; saints and martyrs in armour; inscribed banderoles and scrolls carrying sacred names; reliquaries and votive objects.
2. *Feudal and heraldic apparatus* (second key value): plate armour with pauldrons and gauntlets; banners, pennons and tent canopies; fleurs-de-lis; heraldic lion and unicorn supporters; canopy and throne as rank markers; hierarchical scale, where the most important figure is largest.
3. *Music and courtly leisure* (the text's Music and Media sections, strongly confirmed visually): the vielle (bowed fiddle), citole and psaltery; seated ladies in fitted gowns; climbing rose vines; birds and small dogs.
4. *Sub-school formal markers* (named in the text): Insular interlace and carpet-page ornament; Carolingian and Ottonian gold grounds with frontal imperial or donor portraits; Romanesque rounded arches, stubby colonnettes and heavy carved capitals framing figures.
5. *Precious materials* (the Media stub, fleshed out visually): cloisonné enamel plaques, cabochon gems and pearls set in gilded silver, wool-weft tapestry weave, matte fresco plaster.

**Composition.** The picture plane stays flat and stacked. Architecture appears as a *frame* (arcade, column, border band) rather than as inhabitable space. Borders are patterned strips — lozenges, diaper, foliate runs — and empty space is either left as radiant gold or filled horror-vacui with millefleurs, trees and animals. Symmetry governs devotional and heraldic subjects; gentle diagonal asymmetry (a banner pole, a bowed instrument, a scrolling vine) governs narrative ones. Inscriptions double as ornament: unrolling banderoles with lettering are compositional lines, not captions.

## Mood & Values

Devotional solemnity fused with courtly pageantry. The mood is ordered, hierarchical and luminous: gold and gems express divine light, heraldry expresses lawful rank, and dense pattern expresses reverence rather than clutter. Craftsmanship is anonymous and devotional — the maker serves the value, not the ego. Even leisure (a fiddle played to a seated lady) carries a formal, ceremonial calm.

## Era & Origins

Emerges in the 5th century from Late Antique and Early Christian roots plus Migration-period metalwork, and matures through Insular, Carolingian, Ottonian and Romanesque phases into the Gothic centuries, ending when Renaissance linear perspective replaces the flat gold plane. It absorbs Byzantine icon conventions and, as the source notes, significant Islamic influence on ornament and craft — the style is pan-European and more cosmopolitan than popular "dark ages" myth suggests.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the image as layered flat planes — ground, pattern field, framed figure — never as a perspectival window.
2. Decide the ground first (gold, parchment, solid field, millefleurs); it sets the medium's identity.
3. Draw with contour; model lightly; forbid cast shadows.
4. Let materials supply the light: burnished gold, enamel gloss, pearl highlights.
5. Use inscription and heraldry as design lines: banderoles, banner poles, diapered borders.
6. Rank figures by importance, not by distance.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any of sacred narrative, saint or evangelist portrait, knight or Joan-type armored heroine, courtly music and love, labors of the months, bestiary animals, heraldic display, or a reliquary/object study. Composition: full-page gold-ground panel, arcade-framed fresco bay, all-over tapestry field, or centered object on a dark ground. Lighting: always even and non-directional, but gold may be burnished bright or aged matte. Color: full jewel saturation through fresco chalkiness, within the stated hue families. Figure count: one frontal saint to a crowded court. Medium look: vellum illumination, wool weave, fresco matte, or gilded metal. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a harvest scene, a bestiary owl or an imagined reliquary are equally valid if they obey the flat plane, the palette and the value-order.

**Avoid.** Linear perspective and vanishing points; chiaroscuro, tenebrism, sfumato; cast shadows and directional sunlight; photographic depth of field or bokeh; muddy "dark ages" grime realism (the style is luminous, not filthy); neon, pastel-modern or desaturated cinematic palettes; anime or cartoon exaggeration beyond period stylization; Renaissance anatomical naturalism; modern objects, materials or typography; airbrushed gradients; symmetrical CGI perfection that erases the hand-made wobble of line and gilding.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Bardcore** is a modern digital nostalgia aesthetic: soft pastoral illustration, muted cozy tones, deliberate anachronisms rendered quaintly, no gold leaf, no heraldic or liturgical rigor. Medieval proper keeps tempera flatness, gold grounds and doctrinal iconography.
- **Southern French Gothic** is a regional subset with meridional brightness, brick-and-stone architecture and Italianate volumetric modeling in its frescoes; the broader Medieval idiom here is flatter, gold-grounded and pan-European.
- **Visigothic Art** (Iberian early medieval) favors horseshoe arches, chiseled votive crowns with hanging pendilia, bird-and-beast interlace and sparse, near-aniconic figuration; Medieval at large is narrative and figural.
- **Byzantine** shares gold grounds but follows the Eastern icon canon: Greek lettering, mosaic tesserae, elongated ascetic faces, reverse perspective; Western Medieval shows Latin banderoles, chivalric armour, heraldry and tapestry culture.
- **Dolly Kei** is a modern Japanese fashion style borrowing medieval motifs (crosses, layered thrifted textiles, fur) in contemporary photographs; look for camera realism and synthetic fabric sheen — absent here, where every surface is period craft.
