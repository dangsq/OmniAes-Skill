# Medieval Fantasy

Medieval Fantasy is the fantastical version of the Middle Ages: not a documentary reconstruction but a wishful one, built from 5th–15th century European — most often English — material culture and then freely embellished with magic. Knights kneel at altars, outlaw archers wait in forest shade, castles stack their spires into mist, and a sword is always more than a tool. The style absorbs everything from 19th-century academic painting to desaturated modern film stills to flat animation cels; what holds it together is subject matter (chivalric, courtly, magical medievalism) and a shared colour-and-material grammar, not any single rendering technique.

## Visual Identity

### Palette
Two legitimate poles share one rule: **earthy neutrals carry the majority of the frame; saturated colour arrives as deliberate, concentrated accents.**
- **Earth pole** (cinematic, reenactment, travel-worn): bark and umber brown (#6B4A2F, #4A3526), tan and oat (#C8A878, #D9C39A), charcoal and soot (#2B2622), moss and grey-green (#6E7A6A, #4F5A46), rust and terracotta (#8A4B2A).
- **Jewel pole** (painterly, courtly): crimson and oxblood (#8E1F2F, #A31621), emerald and deep forest green (#1F5C3D, #2E5E4E), royal blue (#24457A), royal purple (#5B3A8E), gold (#C9A227); bright gown colours — yellow, pink, red, purple — are text-sanctioned at court.

Rule of use: heraldic blocks (a crimson cross on a white surcoat), a green damask gown, a rust cloak or a purple mantle read as *accent areas* against stone grey, wood brown and parchment tan. Purple is reserved for royalty and magic. Never neon; even the saturated pole keeps its shadows earthy.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence
1. **Magic** — spells, enchantment, mystical beings; the licence that separates "fantasy" from history.
2. **Knights** — chainmail, surcoats, heraldic crosses, helmets set aside at altars.
3. **Castles** — keeps, battlements, spires on misty hills; stone interiors with columns, arches and altar steps.
4. **Crowns** — circlets, coronets, jewelled headpieces; flowers worn in hair as their soft counterpart.
5. **Swords** — drawn, sheathed, dedicated, or planted in stone or anvil; bows and staves as sibling weapons.

Then, from the fashion and activity record: cloaks, tunics, jewelled belts, long-sleeved gowns, veils and wimples; jousting and fencing spectacles; balls and circle dances of flowing gowns; the two iconic archetypes — the once-and-future king (Arthur, sword from stone) and the forest outlaw (Robin Hood, longbow); and small narrative props: illuminated manuscripts, pouches of silver coins, saddled horses.

### Materials and textures
Chainmail rings; tooled, laced and boiled leather (bracers, cuirasses, belts, pouches); frayed wool cloaks, sometimes under a netted or fringed mantle; velvet and damask gowns — including the slight sheen of costuming polyester stretch velvet, which the style openly accepts; silk veils and wimples; rough-hewn stone and mortar; oak, bark and wooden bow staves; iron and steel blades; gold, gems and coin silver; parchment and vellum with ink and gilding; fur trim at the travel-worn pole. Everything should look handled: hems fray, leather creases, stone erodes, silver tarnishes.

### Recurring subjects
Knights in heraldic surcoats; crowned monarchs and praying ladies in wimple or loose hair with circlet; hooded archers and rangers; mounted riders with the horse as co-subject; witches, wizards and dragons as presences rather than décor; dancers at balls or in village circles; and non-figure subjects entirely — a castle silhouetted in fog, an open illuminated book, coins spilled from a purse on a plank table.

### Composition and light
The wiki text is silent on lighting and framing; the visual corpus supplies consistent habits. Figure-forward framing where costume and weapon carry the narrative, often in vertical portrait crops that may sever the head to study garment and grip. Architectural framing: arches, columns, altar steps, battlement edges. Distance shots reduce castles to silhouettes in mist. Still-life arrangements of props on wood. Riders caught mid-shot at the horse's shoulder. Light is soft and diffuse — overcast sky, forest shade, tall window light in stone interiors — or warm and local: candle, torch, hearth glow. A flat even cel light governs the animation pole. Grades tend muted with desaturated shadows; when colour saturates, it saturates in the accent blocks named above, never across the whole frame.

## Mood & Values
The style's declared values are **escapism and chivalry**, and every formal choice serves them. Devotion: a sword laid on an altar, hands clasped in prayer, a knight dedicated to a lady. Romance and grace: coupled dances, circles of flowing gowns, flowers in hair. Pageantry and spectacle: tournaments watched as much as fought, balls, crowns worn openly — hierarchy itself as dreamed spectacle. Quest tension: the near-miss of the joust, the drawn bow, the road out of the castle gate. Even at its grittiest the mood keeps hope and honour legible; even at its most idealised it keeps weight — stone is heavy, mail is heavy, vows are heavy. A playful, witty register (animated sword-in-stone, fairytale parody) is a sanctioned minority tone, not a breach.

## Era & Origins
The source period is the European Middle Ages, 5th–15th century, with medieval England the dominant flavour; some borrowings slip in from Renaissance weapons and armour, and recent practice widens to non-English medieval cultures. The aesthetic itself is a modern layer cake: 19th- and early-20th-century romantic medievalism in academic painting, mid-20th-century animation and adventure film, tabletop and video games from 1974 onward, and the desaturated cinematic grade of 2010s–2020s fantasy television, with Bardcore as its sonic cousin. It reacts against modernity by offering somewhere else to be, and it explicitly prefers the fantastical over the accurate: silhouettes are medieval, fabrics and fittings need not be.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Build a wishful Middle Ages, not a museum one: medieval silhouettes, optional accuracy, mandatory atmosphere.
- Let earthy neutrals hold the frame; place jewel-tone accents as heraldic blocks, gowns, cloaks or magic glow.
- Show at least one key motif (magic, knight, castle, crown, sword) and support it with period material: mail, leather, wool, velvet, stone, parchment.
- Keep light soft or flame-lit; keep shadows earthy; keep saturation local, not global.
- Make objects look used: frayed hems, creased leather, eroded mortar, tarnished coin.
- Carry a chivalric or escapist emotion — devotion, quest, pageantry, romance — in posture and gaze.

**Variation axes** (these move independently)
- **Subject**: any figure or thing inside the medieval-fantastical world — knight, monarch, outlaw archer, lady, witch, wizard, dragon, horse, dancer, villager; or no figure at all: castle landscape, manuscript close-up, prop still life.
- **Cultural flavour**: English-default, but non-English medieval influences are sanctioned.
- **Composition**: full narrative tableau; headless costume study; architectural framing; misty silhouette distance; still-life arrangement; rider mid-shot.
- **Lighting**: overcast diffuse, forest shade, window light in stone, candle/torch warmth, fog-flat grey, even cel light.
- **Colour handling**: anywhere from near-monochrome earth to full jewel saturation, as long as the accent-block rule holds and purple stays royal/magical.
- **Medium**: academic oil finish, cinematic photographic grade, cosplay photograph, flat animation cel, book illustration, tapestry-like rendering.
- **Mood**: romantic idealism ↔ gritty realism; solemn devotion ↔ courtly joy ↔ fairytale wit.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A mossy bridge, a scullery, a siege camp or an invented ritual can all read as Medieval Fantasy if the palette, materials, light and chivalric-escapist mood hold.

**Avoid**
- Modern objects and infrastructure: cars, firearms, plastics, glass towers, electric light, signage, zippers, synthetic sportswear, modern hairstyles or makeup.
- Neon or cyber-saturated colour, global saturation, clean vector minimalism, corporate flat design.
- Horror-first content: gore, undead hordes, corruption as the sole subject — that crosses into Dark Fantasy.
- Pure pastoral domesticity with no chivalric, courtly or magical marker — that drifts to Cottagecore.
- Occult-only still lifes (crystals, candles, herbs with no medieval world around them) — that is Witchcore.
- Creature-centric images with no human medieval culture in frame — that is Dragoncore.
- Sci-fi or steampunk hybrids: chrome, spaceships, energy weapons, clockwork gears.
- Documentary reenactment dryness: seamless white backdrops, catalogue lighting, modern studio gloss.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | Core difference | Quick tell |
|---|---|---|
| Bardcore | Music-first meme aesthetic: medieval-instrument covers of modern songs, minstrel and tapestry humour | Lutes, hurdy-gurdies, woodcut-tapestry rendering, joke anachronisms; lacks Medieval Fantasy's narrative worldbuilding of knights, castles and magic |
| Witchcore | Occult practice and witchcraft paraphernalia as the whole subject | Candles, crystals, herbs, moon phases, black robes; no heraldry, armour, crowns or chivalric narrative |
| Cottagecore | Pastoral domestic comfort, deliberately without hierarchy or violence | Linen, picnics, gardens, baking, soft sunlight; no swords, mail, castles or quest tension |
| Dark Fantasy | Horror-inflected fantasy; dread is the point | Gore, decay, monstrous focus, oppressive black; Medieval Fantasy stays escapist and honour-bound even when gritty |
| Dragoncore | Dragons and reptilian creatures as the subject itself | Creature-centric framing with medieval culture as backdrop or absent; Medieval Fantasy keeps the human chivalric world central, dragons one motif among five |

Knightcore and Royalcore are armour-only and court-only slices of this style; Fantasy at large is its parent. The medieval anchor — 5th–15th century European material culture, however fantastical — is what keeps Medieval Fantasy distinct from all of them.
