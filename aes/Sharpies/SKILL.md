# Sharpies (Sharps)

Sharpies were a working-class youth subculture and gang phenomenon of 1960s–1970s Australia, strongest in Melbourne with outliers in Sydney and Perth. The look began "sharp" — custom-tailored, neat, deliberately anti-rocker and anti-hippie — and hardened by the early 1970s into a body-clinging uniform of tiny striped cardigans, high-waisted flares, mullets and platforms. Gangs named themselves after their suburb or street and wore that territory on their bodies, their knits and their hand-painted signs. The subculture was short-lived, violently territorial and poorly documented; what survives visually is mostly amateur colour snapshot photography and a thinner strand of black-and-white press and studio portraits, so the style's native medium is the faded, grainy, flash-blown family or gang snapshot rather than polished studio work.

## Visual Identity

### Colour palette

The palette works in two layers: a **garment layer** of saturated knit accents over desaturated denim, and an **environment layer** of sun-bleached suburban neutrals, all usually submerged in a faded-print colour cast.

| Layer | Range | Hex anchors |
|---|---|---|
| Denim (faded to raw) | mid indigo to washed blue | `#35506B` → `#4E6E8E` → `#7C93A8` |
| Knit reds | oxblood, brick, maroon | `#6E2B2B`, `#8C3A32`, `#7A2F3A` |
| Knit earths | mustard, camel, tan | `#B0824A` → `#C99A5B` |
| Knit darks | black, charcoal | `#1C1B1A` → `#3A3835` |
| Knit cools | cobalt, royal; plum tees | `#2F4BA8`, `#5C4A72` |
| Suburban neutrals | concrete, brick veneer, cream wall, chain-link grey | `#B9B7AE`, `#C9BBA6`, `#D8D2C4`, `#8E9194` |
| Print cast | yellowed highlights, muted shadows | `#E8DCC0`, `#6B6560` |
| Hair | bleached blonde, henna red, dark brown | `#D9B36A`, `#A34A2A`, `#3B2A1E` |

Rule of thumb: one or two **loud knit colours** (red, mustard, cobalt, purple) held against a field of **flat denim blue and pale concrete**; skin reads sun-tanned. Pastels, neons, jewel-tone richness and clean modern grading are foreign to the style. Monochrome is legitimate for the 1964–70 first wave and for press-style images.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how strongly the written record documents them)

1. **The Conny cardigan** — the defining item. An ultra-tight wool/acrylic cardigan, typically striped, worn several sizes too small to sculpt the torso; five buttons, a small belt at the back, often custom-made in unique colourways by Melbourne tailors (the name derives from Mr. Conti of Thornbury). Sleeveless knit-vest readings of the Conny appear in surviving photographs and fit the custom-version tradition.
2. **High-waisted flared pants or jeans** — for all genders; the flare balances the constricted top. Rolled or cuffed hems are common in snapshots.
3. **The mullet** — short on top and sides, long at the back; bleached or red-dyed hair as a popular variant. The shaved-close sides frame the next motif.
4. **Heavy platform shoes** — standard footwear for everyone; subtle design differences signalled the wearer's neighborhood.
5. **Tattoos** — professional and homemade, considered standard even when illegal for minors; gang members' names inked on arms, chests and backs, often deliberately displayed shirtless or from behind.
6. **The male pierced ear** — a defiant claim of masculinity against hippie longhair.
7. **Gang names and suburban territory** — hand-painted boards and signs, lettering on tees and knits, names tattooed on skin; the suburb is the brand.
8. **"Brushes" (Sharpie girls) wardrobe** — Connys plus twinsets, pleated skirts, miniskirts or pinafores. Well documented in text, thinly represented in surviving images; treat as licensed but optional.
9. **First-wave suit (1964–70)** — custom-made suits and short, neat haircuts; the "sharp" that named the crew. Text-supported, with a few black-and-white group photographs behind it.
10. **Photograph-observed extras (image evidence, not named in text)** — denim bib overalls/dungarees worn over bare chests or tees; plain striped tees; hooded tops. Recurring enough in the snapshot record to use, but subordinate to the nine motifs above.

### Materials and textures

Ribbed and intarsia-striped knitwear stretched to its limit, buttons straining; denim twill, faded, creased at the knee and hip; corduroy and denim overalls; crepe and stacked rubber platform soles; sun-tanned skin, chest hair, slightly blurred homemade tattoo linework. Environment textures: chain-link fence, brick veneer and weatherboard walls, cracked concrete sidewalk, vinyl train-carriage interiors, painted hall floors.

### Recurring subjects

Gang group portraits in rows or tight clusters; solo full-length stance portraits on sidewalk or driveway; shirtless tattoo displays including rear views showing back pieces; dance floors at town halls, school dances and early discos; train carriage interiors; suburban streets, backyards and fence lines; hand-painted territory signage as backdrop; couples and girls in brushes dress; black-and-white studio or press portraits for the early wave.

### Composition and lighting

Frontal, confrontational or deadpan group grammar: bodies shoulder-to-shoulder, arms crossed, hands in pockets or draped over mates' shoulders, weight on one hip. Solo figures framed full-length and centred, head-to-platform-shoe, against an ordinary street. Tattoo roll-calls shot from behind in a line. Candid motion only on dance floors. Lighting is either **harsh overhead sunlight** with hard short shadows on concrete, or **direct on-camera flash** indoors — hot foreheads, blown highlights, backgrounds falling to murk. Overcast flat daylight appears in the black-and-white strand. Framing is amateur: slightly off-level, centre-weighted, no art-direction.

### Medium and finish

Consumer 35mm/126 colour print aesthetics: visible grain, magenta–cyan drift, yellowed highlights, muted shadows, soft focus, occasional white print border or square crop. Black-and-white gelatin-silver tone for first-wave and press images. Never crisp, never HDR, never seamless-backdrop studio polish.

## Mood & Values

Defiant pride and territorial belonging. The uniform is peacock masculinity: a cardigan worn too small is a boast about the body under it, and the pierced ear and ink are deliberate provocations aimed at hippies, rival gangs and polite society alike. Beneath the bravado runs gang intimacy — arms locked around shoulders, bodies packed together, names carried on skin. "Sharp" is self-named dignity: working-class kids dressing with tailor-made precision on their own terms, in their own suburb, answerable to nobody else.

## Era & Origins

Early 1960s, working-class suburbs of Melbourne (with Sydney and Perth satellites), emerging alongside postwar European immigrant culture — the Conny itself comes from a Greek tailor in Thornbury. First wave c. 1964–70: neat, conservative, custom-suited, defined against declining rockers and the rising hippie movement. Iconic phase early 1970s to early 1980s: body-clinging, flare-legged, mulleted, absorbing some British skinhead aggression while remaining distinctly Australian. Soundtrack is Australian pub rock and hard rock — Rose Tattoo, AC/DC, Billy Thorpe and the Aztecs, Lobby Loyde and the Coloured Balls — plus American soul and British beat on the dance floor, with home-grown dances like "the Break"/"the Sharpie Shuffle" (line dancing) and "the Sharpie Rock" (couples). Media-fuelled moral panic and police crackdowns, plus new youth cultures, killed the subculture by the mid-1980s.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. **Silhouette rule first:** constricted knit top + high-waisted flared bottom + heavy platform footwear. If the silhouette reads tight-above-flare-below, the image is halfway home.
2. **Conny logic:** cropped, skin-tight, striped banding across the chest, five-button placket, small back belt; custom colourway; sleeveless vest variant acceptable. The knit should look bought or made too small on purpose.
3. **Head and body code:** mullet (optionally bleached or red), single pierced ear on boys, visible tattoos with homemade linework — names, lettering, simple flash — on arms, chest or back.
4. **Territory code:** put the suburb on something — a hand-painted sign, tee lettering, a tattoo — or at least imply it with a fence-line, street-corner or carriage full of one crew.
5. **Setting grammar:** Australian working-class suburbia and its transit/leisure circuit — brick veneer houses, chain-link fences, concrete sidewalks, train carriages, town-hall dances, discos, pub-rock venues.
6. **Group grammar:** rows, clusters, physical contact, deadpan or grinning defiance facing the lens; or candid line-dancing motion.
7. **Medium finish:** faded amateur colour print (grain, cast, flash or hard sun) or black-and-white for first-wave/press moods.
8. **Phase choice:** either the neat 1964–70 suited wave (short hair, custom suit, monochrome-friendly) or the iconic 1970s flare-and-Conny wave. Do not blend phases inconsistently.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range from solo stance portrait to ten-person gang cluster, dance-floor candid, train interior, backyard gathering, tattoo display or studio portrait. Composition may be frontal row, pyramid cluster, rear-view line, full-length centred solo, or motion candid. Lighting may be harsh midday sun, direct flash, overcast flat, or hall tungsten. Colour handling may run from heavily faded warm print to cooler faded cast to full monochrome. Mood may run from menacing to celebratory to tender mate-ship. Gender expression may include brushes styling (twinset, pleated skirt, miniskirt, pinafore) alongside the core uniform. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**

- Leather biker jackets, motorcycles, helmets, engineer boots, chrome-and-road iconography.
- Pompadours, quiffs, ducktails, slicked hair; hippie tie-dye, headbands, flowing centre-parted hair, peace signs (Sharpies defined themselves against both).
- Mod parkas, scooters, bowl crops; preppy chinos, boat shoes, Oxford buttons, argyle, country-club settings.
- Rockabilly revival costume: polka dots, circle skirts, retro diner signage.
- Full UK skinhead uniform as the dominant code (cropped hair, braces, Harrington jacket, Dr. Martens); the influence is real but the mullet, Conny and platforms must win.
- Low-rise, baggy, straight or skinny pants; oversized untucked tees; modern sneakers, logos, sportswear, phones.
- Digital crispness, seamless backdrops, studio fashion polish, modern teal-orange or pastel grading.
- American or British location cues: the suburb must feel Australian — brick veneer, weatherboard, chain-link, wide concrete footpaths.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Overlap | Sharpie tell |
|---|---|---|
| Biker | Tattoos, toughness, working-class grit | Knit Conny and denim instead of leather and chrome; territory is a suburb on foot or by train, not the open road |
| Greaser | 1950s-derived toughness, jeans | Mullet not ducktail; high-waisted flare not straight Levi's; striped too-small cardigan not leather jacket or white tee; 60s–70s Australia not 50s US |
| Mod | Early-wave suit neatness, tailoring influence | Iconic phase is body-clinging knit + flare + platforms + tattoos; suburban tailor, not Carnaby Street; no parka, scooter or crop |
| Preppy | Cardigan as key garment | Conny is deliberately several sizes too small with back belt and loud stripes, worn with flared denim and platforms — opposite class signal to relaxed Ivy chinos and boat shoes |
| Rockabilly | Retro 50s roots, tattoos | Contemporaneous 70s street style, not revival costume; mullet and flare, not quiff and circle skirt |

Also watch the skinhead border: both are working-class and aggressive, but the Sharpie head is a mullet (long at the back), the foot is a platform, and the torso is a Conny — cropped hair, braces and boots mean skinhead, not Sharpie. When in doubt, ask three questions: is the knit too small on purpose, is the waist high and the hem flared, and does the hair run long at the back? Three yeses is a Sharpie.
