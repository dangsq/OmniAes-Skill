# Funk

Funk is the aesthetic that grew out of the music born in African-American communities in the mid-1960s — a rhythm-first sound built on "The One," the accented first beat of the bar — and flowered into a fully visual culture in the 1970s. Where earlier soul acts presented themselves in conservative matching suits, funk presented the body as a loud, proud, theatrical instrument: flared silhouettes, platform soles, Afros, sequins and velvet, clashing color, and a stagecraft that could turn bedsheets into intergalactic robes. It is an aesthetic of groove made visible — everything in the frame looks like it could move on the downbeat.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is **saturated clash, not harmony**: three to five hues from one family sit side by side at near-equal intensity, color-blocked rather than blended. The core family is tangerine and burnt orange (#E85D1F, #FF6B1A), hot magenta and pink (#D6336C, #FF2E88), royal through electric purple (#6A2C91, #7B3FF2), gold and mustard (#F2B32C, #FFC93C), turquoise and lime (#17BEBB, #A8D400), and fire-engine red (#C1272D). These read best against a neutral carrier — cream or warm off-white seamless backdrops (#EFE3C8, #E8DCC0) in photography, or flat black (#141414) in poster work, which makes the hues vibrate. A documented second register appears later in the 1970s: **earth tones** — rust (#9C4A1F), olive (#6B6B23), chocolate (#3E2A1E), cream — used with the same boldness but lower key. The only place hues are allowed to melt into each other is psychedelic print: tie-dye swirls, sunbursts, and concentric rings where orange bleeds into magenta into violet.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Flared silhouettes**: bell-bottoms widening from the knee, jumpsuits, suits with wide lapels, vests worn over bare chests. The silhouette is the style's skeleton.
2. **The Afro** and natural textured hair, worn voluminous as an emblem of cultural pride and natural beauty.
3. **Platform footwear**: thick stacked soles and heels on boots and sandals, adopted by men and women alike; the sole height is a feature, often shown deliberately.
4. **Shine and plush fabrics**: polyester, satin, sequins, velvet, metallic lamé — the flashy, luxurious surface feel is definitional.
5. **Psychedelic and bold geometric pattern**: swirls, concentric circles, sunbursts, stripes, star prints, on garments and backdrops alike.
6. **Theatrical cosmic costume**: capes, robes and drapery treated as regalia (the P-Funk "freedom of dress," bedsheets fashioned into elaborate capes), star emblems and star-shaped instruments, glitter and face paint, oversized collars, hats and headpieces; an "intergalactic weirdness" and cosmic fantasy running through stage design.
7. **Afrocentric and Ancient Egyptian iconography** (pyramids, sun discs, hieroglyphic-flavored geometry), documented in album art and performance — an occasional accent rather than a constant.
8. **The empowered urban figure** of Blaxploitation imagery: street posture, leather trenches and caps, inner-city settings carrying social commentary.
9. **Accessories**: hoop earrings, beaded necklaces, medallions, tinted or star-shaped sunglasses, wide metallic cuffs.
10. **The ensemble**: bands and dance crews posed as coordinated-but-individual clusters — matching palette and energy, never identical uniforms.

### Materials and textures

Polyester sheen and satin highlight; sequin fields that catch flash as scattered white sparks; velvet depth; patent and tooled leather on platform boots; metallic lamé and mirror lenses; against these, soft matte counterweights — tulle clouds, feather boas, fur-trim collars. The image medium itself carries texture: warm, slightly faded 1970s print color with visible grain and press softness; airbrushed mural backdrops (stylized suns, rainbows, clouds); and in graphic work, flat poster ink with halftone or screen-print character.

### Recurring subjects

Solo performer portraits in full costume; band and crew group shots; dancers caught mid-step; party and crowd density; stage spectacle with props (star-shaped guitars and basses, capes, towering headpieces); album covers and event posters built as flat graphics; street scenes with urban grit; and, at the quiet end, costume as still life — a row of platform boots, a hung cape.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Composition honors the silhouette: full-body or three-quarter framing so flare, platform and collar all read; centered frontal stances with hand-on-hip swagger or a pointing, inviting gesture; group shots staggered into pyramids of seated, kneeling and standing figures; poster work reduced to flat fields, concentric rings and silhouette profiles. Lighting is period-direct: on-camera flash with hot highlights and quick falloff into dark rooms; even, warm studio light on seamless cream or single-color backdrops; saturated stage gels (magenta, green, orange) cutting through darkness; or open daylight for the earth-tone register. Graphic poster modes use no modeled light at all — pure ink shape.

## Mood & Values

Funk images radiate **self-expression, individuality, cultural pride, liberation, energetic movement and rebellion against convention**. The mood is joy as assertion: swagger, grin, open stance, bodies angled as if the bass line just landed. Excess is sincerity — sequins, capes and glitter are declarations of freedom of dress, not irony. Underneath the celebration runs a current of urgent social commentary and Black pride, so grit and dignity can share the frame with the glitter. Nothing in funk is apologetic or restrained.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in African-American communities in the United States in the mid-1960s, crystallized by James Brown's percussive "The One" (first definitively recorded in 1967), and visually dominant through the 1970s via Sly and the Family Stone's psychedelic blend, George Clinton's Parliament-Funkadelic cosmic mythos, Earth, Wind & Fire's Afrocentric pageantry, Soul Train dance culture, and Blaxploitation cinema (Shaft, Super Fly, Car Wash). The style is a deliberate departure from the formal, conservative presentation of earlier soul acts and from mainstream convention; earth tones enter later in the decade before the aesthetic's lineage runs into 1980s figures like Prince and Rick James.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Build the silhouette first: flare from knee or hip, platform sole height, oversized collar, lapel or cape volume. If the outline is generic, the image is not funk.
2. Clash deliberately: pick three to five hues from the core family and color-block them at equal saturation; reserve blending for tie-dye and sunburst graphics.
3. Put shine and plush in the same frame — sequins or satin against velvet, tulle or feather.
4. Crown the head: Afro volume or period wig; hair is a pride statement, never an afterthought.
5. Seed cosmic play: stars, sunbursts, concentric rings as garment print, prop, backdrop or poster structure.
6. Keep the period medium: warm faded print color with grain, airbrushed mural smoothness, or flat screen-print ink. Avoid contemporary digital crispness and cool grading.
7. Put the body in the groove: weight shifted, mid-step, gesturing, dancing — stillness only when it is posed swagger.
8. For groups, coordinate without uniforming: shared palette and energy, individual garments.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a solo portrait, a band cluster, a dancer, a crowd, a stage spectacle, a street scene, a poster graphic or a costume still life. Composition may be centered full-body, staggered group pyramid, collage poster, or flat graphic field. Lighting may be direct flash, even studio, colored stage gel, or daylight. Color handling may run neon-saturated or late-70s earth-tone. Mood may run from pure celebration to socially aware urban grit. Medium may be photograph, mural-backed studio set, or vector-psychedelic poster. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A still life of platform boots on cream, or a flat poster of concentric rings around a silhouette profile, is as valid as a costumed performer.

**Avoid**

- Minimalist restraint, neutral Scandinavian palettes, negative space as a virtue.
- Modern clothing: skinny jeans, athleisure, sneakers, contemporary streetwear, digital-era tailoring.
- Disco gloss as the dominant note: mirror balls, champagne silver, sleek silk-shirt nightclub cool.
- Punk hardware: leather jackets with studs, mohawks, safety pins, nihilist aggression.
- Hippie rusticity: denim, fringe, peace signs, loose natural fibers, sun-faded washes, long straight hair as the hair statement.
- Bohemian layering: ethnic textile stacks, floppy hats, muted wanderer palettes.
- Sleek chrome sci-fi: clean CGI spacecraft, polished metal futurism without handmade glitter.
- Cool desaturated teal-and-orange cinematic grading; clean corporate vector flatness without psychedelic content.
- Conservative matching suits and formal respectability presentation — the thing funk rebelled against.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Their tell | Funk's difference |
|---|---|---|
| Afrofuturism | Sleek technology, chrome, speculative futurism, often contemporary or forward-dated | Funk's cosmic strand is handmade 1970s stagecraft — glitter, drapery, star props, print grain — earthbound and groove-driven, not technologically sleek |
| Disco | Mirror-ball gloss, silk and gold chains, cool nightclub neon, streamlined glamour | Funk is grittier, more maximalist and costumed; clashing saturated color, platforms and Afros over feathered polish; sweat and flash over champagne cool |
| Hippie | Denim, fringe, beads, pastoral ease, sun-faded color, straight hair | Funk is urban, synthetic-shine (polyester, satin, sequins), tailored flare, high-contrast saturation, Afrocentric pride |
| Afropunk | Leather, studs, mohawks, monochrome with neon accents, confrontational DIY | Funk is celebratory, shiny-fabric rather than leather-armored, period-1970s, joy rather than nihilism |
| Bohemian | Layered ethnic textiles, muted earth layers, relaxed drift | Funk's earth-tone phase keeps the flare-and-platform silhouette, sequin shine and graphic punch; boho avoids neon and sequins entirely |

When in doubt, test the silhouette and the surface: flare, platform, Afro, and at least one shining fabric against one plush one, in clashing saturated color with 1970s print warmth — that combination is funk and nothing else.
