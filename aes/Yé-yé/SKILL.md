# Yé-yé

Yé-yé is the pop-music and youth phenomenon that emerged in France around 1959–1962 and spread through Spain, Italy, Portugal and Quebec, carried by the radio show and magazine *Salut les copains* and by its teenage idols, the "yé-yé girls" (chicas yé-yé, ragazze yé-yé). Visually it is a print-and-broadcast culture: record sleeves, teen-magazine covers, painted illustration, and black-and-white studio, television and party photography. It recontextualized American rock and roll and London Mod into a Romance-language pop consciousness — softer, girlier, more wholesome and more graphic than its British cousin, with a wink of mild rebellion under parental approval.

## Visual Identity

**Colour language — two registers, never muddy between them.**
1. *Silver-print register:* neutral black-and-white with a full grey scale, high-key seamless backgrounds, crisp blacks and clean whites. No sepia, no warm antique tint.
2. *Flat-print colour register:* bright pastel fields — powder pink, butter yellow, baby blue, cream — punctured by one or two bold primaries (tomato red, marigold orange, cobalt) and anchored by black or white type and shapes. Approximate ranges: pastels #FADADD–#F4C2C2 (pink), #F7E39A–#FFD966 (yellow), #BBDCEB–#A8D8E8 (blue), #F5EEDC (cream); accents #D93A2B–#E8722A (red-orange), #111111 (black), #FDFDFD (white). Florals appear as flat, naive folk graphics — daisies, poppies, rosettes in pink, red and grass green — never as watercolor or liberty-print softness. A black ground with cream/gold lettering is a documented sleeve variant.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.**
- *Hair architecture:* the fringe is the movement's emblem — long straight bangs over the eyes, bob cuts with fringe, beehive and bouffant volumes, ponytails, sleek straight lengths. Boys wear heavily combed, voluminous bouffant fringes.
- *Wardrobe:* miniskirts, A-line and shift dresses, go-go and knee-high boots, Mary Jane shoes, striped shirts, turtlenecks, headbands, bow ties, cat-eye sunglasses; white Peter Pan and scalloped collars recur strongly in the photographic record.
- *Makeup:* heavy cat-eye eyeliner as the single dramatic gesture; lips pale or candy-bright; otherwise minimal.
- *Graphic devices:* geometric patterns — zigzags, circles, stripes, ladder-like linear grids — and rounded, groovy display type, often red or orange, set across sleeve and magazine tops; circular photo insets on patterned grounds.
- *Props and settings:* microphones and mic stands (TV stage, contest hall), telephones, lollipops, party dance floors, seaside steps; the surreal painted sleeve of a giant fringed head worked on by tiny ladder-mounted figures marks an illustrative strand where scale play is permitted.

**Materials and textures.** Matte coated paper, visible halftone dot, gouache and airbrush illustration, silver-gelatin grain; patent leather and PVC boots, wool crepe dresses, cotton piqué collars, sequins, occasional fur or feather trim.

**Recurring subjects.** Predominantly young female singers posed as idols; romantic, sensitive male idols in neat suits and white shirts; couples and duos; dancing girls at parties; illustrated faces; cover layouts with masthead type. The male and regional (Portuguese, Quebec) visual record is thinner and survives mostly as black-and-white studio portraiture.

**Composition and lighting.** Face-first: centered bust or head portraits dominate, gaze direct or slightly aside, expression between innocent pout and open smile. Backgrounds are flat — seamless grey, solid colour field, or patterned graphic ground. Full-body stage and street shots appear but keep the figure legible and centered. Lighting is even, high-key studio flash or soft glamour key with minimal shadow; colour print work is deliberately shadowless and flat. No chiaroscuro, no atmospheric depth.

## Mood & Values

Youthful optimism, fun, innocence, first love, modernity and mild rebellion. The yé-yé image is engineered wholesomeness — polished for parents and advertisers — that nonetheless carries emancipatory charge: teenage girls as public idols, hemlines as quiet protest, especially under Francoist and Estado Novo traditionalism. A melancholic, sophisticated undercurrent (the Françoise Hardy strand) is legitimate: wistful gazes, slower poses, greyer frames. Boys project romance and sensitivity, not toughness.

## Era & Origins

Early 1960s, France first (radio 1959, magazine 1962), flourishing in Spain 1963–68 and across Italy, Portugal and Quebec; faded by the late 1960s, revived nostalgically in the late 1970s and quoted by 1990s Shibuya-Kei. It reacts against post-war traditionalism and adult-shared taste, born of teenage economic empowerment and mass media; it absorbs British Mod and American rock and roll but sweetens and graphicizes them.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. The fringe and the eyeliner carry the style: build the hair as architecture (bangs, bob, bouffant) and the face around a single strong lined eye gesture.
2. Respect the register: either crisp neutral black-and-white silver-print logic, or flat shadowless print colour with hard edges. Halfway glossy realism breaks the idiom.
3. Keep innocence with a wink: playful props, open eyes, slight pout or grin; cheeky, never cynical or overtly sexual.
4. Add one graphic layer: mod geometry, naive florals, or rounded bold type; let it sit flat against the figure.
5. Honour period materiality: halftone dot, matte paper, mild registration offset, fine silver grain. Digital sharpness and gloss are anachronistic.
6. Restraint in makeup and ornament: liner plus lip; collars, bows and headbands instead of jewellery stacks.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a girl singer, boy idol, duo, dancing party-goer, painted giant face, magazine cover, TV contest stage or street scene. Composition may range from centered bust to full-body stage shot to surreal scale-play illustration to type-led cover layout. Lighting may be high-key flash, soft window glamour, or entirely flat graphic light. Colour may be full greyscale, pastel field with primary accent, primary pop on cream, or black ground with light type. Mood may run from bubblegum cheer to chanson melancholy. Medium may be photograph, painted illustration, or print collage. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image, and need not reproduce any reference's composition.

**Avoid.** Hippie and Flower Power signs: center-parted fringe-less long hair, tie-dye, paisley, beads, peace symbols. Psychedelic liquid colour and swirling poster type. Peacock Revolution dandyism: ruffles, velvet, cravats, jewel-tone brocade, long male hair. Mod hardness as dominant code: parkas, scooters, Union Jacks, target roundels, androgynous sharp tailoring. City Pop gloss: neon night palettes, airbrushed convertibles, pools, skylines. Coquette boudoir femininity: lace, pearls, satin ribbon fetishes, soft-focus cream dreamscapes. Eighties and later artifacts: chrome, neon, digital screens, smartphones, contemporary sneakers. Sepia or foxed "old photo" damage; heavy contouring, tattoos, bleached brows; grunge distress.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Mod:** shared miniskirts and geometry, but Mod is British, streetwise and androgynous — parkas, scooters, monochrome cool — while yé-yé is Romance-language, girlish (Peter Pan collars, knee socks, bows), pastel print culture with studio and TV glamour and innocence over cool.
- **Peacock Revolution:** late-Sixties male flamboyance in velvet, ruffles and jewel tones; yé-yé men are combed, suited, romantic idols, and the palette stays pastel/primary with crisp silhouettes.
- **Shibuya-Kei:** the 1990s Japanese revival quotes yé-yé but through lounge irony, vector-clean digital design and CD-booklet minimalism; yé-yé itself is analog halftone, earnest period type and silver-print grain.
- **City Pop:** late-70s/80s glossy airbrush, neon-night urban leisure and adult sophistication; yé-yé is matte, daytime, teenage, and set on stages, covers and party floors.
- **Coquette:** 2020s hyper-feminine nostalgia of lace, pearls and soft-focus cream; yé-yé femininity is graphic, geometric, crisp-collared, flat-lit and public rather than boudoir-soft.
