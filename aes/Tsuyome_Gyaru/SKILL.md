# Tsuyome Gyaru

Tsuyome (強め, from "tsuyo," strong) is the extreme-makeup wing of the Japanese gyaru subculture. It names a broad attitude as much as a wardrobe: any gyaru look pushed to its flashy, bold, sexy, cool limit can be called tsuyome, and it overlaps heavily with Banba in silhouette and makeup weight. Its visual shorthand is leopard print on a black or brown base, gold chain hardware at the hip, and a face built from deep tan, heavy lashes and nude lips. Where mainstream gyaru flirts with cute, tsuyome commits to "ora ora" — an aggressive, showy confidence — and carries rebellion as its core value. It reads as street-documentary photography: sidewalk snaps, dressing-room mirror selfies, and scanned magazine pages rather than studio work.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** The image must read as black-dominant, warmed by animal-print caramel and metallic gold, with tanned skin and bleached hair supplying the mid-tones and white fur or white makeup as punctuation. Brown-base outfits are equally valid; cool pastels are absent.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.** (1) Leopard/cheetah print, the style's key motif, as clothing or accessory. (2) Black or brown base garments. (3) Gold: chains, studs, belt hardware. (4) The D.I.A belt — a wide studded, chain-hung hip belt, the subculture's most sought-after staple piece. (5) Short shorts and mini hemlines. (6) Leg warmers, in fur or knit. (7) Strong makeup: long false lashes and nude-colored lips. (8) Bleached, high-volume hair extensions — curled, crimped or waved — ubiquitous in the image record though the text leaves it implicit. (9) Deep, even tan, inherited from the style's kuro-gyaru lineage. (10) Occasional, image-attested variation: zebra-print fur coats, leopard gloves or arm cuffs, star and heart charms, layered gold necklaces, studded cowboy hats — permissible accents, not canon.

**Materials and textures.** Faux fur (leg warmers, coats), slouched suede thigh boots, ribbed knit warmers, metallic chain and cone studs, glossy synthetic hair, shimmered tanned skin, distressed denim micro-shorts. Texture contrast is the point: soft fur against hard gold against glossy hair.

**Subjects and settings.** Young women styled head-to-toe, solo or in gyaru-circle groups. Settings are vernacular: paved Tokyo sidewalks with blurred pedestrians, boutique and shop interiors, dressing rooms faced by a mirror, tight face-framed selfies, and magazine editorial pages carrying Japanese typography. The medium is snapshot culture — on-camera flash, phone cameras, scanned print with halftone grain and text overlays.

**Composition and lighting.** Either a centered full-body or three-quarter figure that shows the leg line (bare tan thigh between micro-hem and boot or leg-warmer stack), or a tight face crop selling the eye makeup. Poses are assertive: chin up, direct gaze, hand on hip or peace sign raised. Lighting is flat overcast daylight, harsh direct flash, or indoor fluorescent — never softbox glamour. Print-scan texture and slight grain are authentic, not flaws.

## Mood & Values

Rebellion, loudly worn. Tsuyome rejects mainstream Japanese beauty norms of pale skin and restrained makeup in favor of tan, lash and gold excess. The value set is flashy, bold, sexy, cool — "ora ora" swagger tempered by playfulness. There is pride and belonging in it: gyaru circles, cafe culture, and brand loyalty make the look a membership card as much as a personal statement.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in early-2000s Japan as a toned-down successor to kuro-gyaru extremes like ganguro and manba, popularized through gyaru magazines and brands such as D.I.A spec, Garula and Skinny Lip. It declined with the wider gyaru downturn by the early 2010s, then proved one of the most durable substyles: the gyaru circle Black Diamond (founded 2012) and its Ganguro cafe kept it visible, and the 2020s gyaru revival brought second-hand demand, new labels, and the 2025 relaunch of D.I.A spec — including the returning D.I.A belt — alongside veteran brand Black Queen, whose lookbooks draw on EGG and LOALO magazine models.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the outfit on a black or brown base and place leopard print prominently — top, dress, shorts, gloves or bag.
2. Anchor the hip with a wide studded, chain-hung belt; add gold chains, studs and charms elsewhere.
3. Keep the leg story: bare tanned thigh between a micro-hem and a stacked leg warmer or slouch boot.
4. Face: deep even tan, heavy false lashes upper and lower, defined liner, pale nude lip; a white liner or nose-bridge highlight is acceptable at the Banba-leaning edge of the range.
5. Hair bleached, long and voluminous — curls, crimps or teased waves, ash platinum to honey.
6. Attitude first: confrontational playfulness, direct eye contact, unapologetic pose.
7. Shoot in snapshot vernacular: street flash, mirror selfie, or magazine scan with Japanese text; allow grain and halftone.
8. Hold palette discipline: black, gold, leopard caramel, tan, bleached blonde, white-fur accent.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a full-body street snap, a makeup-close selfie, a mirror shot, an editorial page, a circle group portrait, or even an accessory still-life. Setting may range across sidewalk, arcade, boutique, cafe or dressing room. Tan depth may run caramel to deep bronze; hair from platinum ash to honey, curled to crimped; print from leopard to zebra or cheetah, micro-scale to bold; base color from black-dominant to brown; lighting from overcast daylight to direct flash to fluorescent to scanned halftone; makeup from the nude-lip core to the white-accented extreme. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Pastel sweetness (pink frills, himegyaru lace, Sweet Lolita bows); pale untanned skin with soft natural makeup or "clean girl" glass skin; ganguro/manba extremes (paint-dark skin, neon face paint, white face patterns, neon dreadlocks, glow accessories); Rokku dominance (band tees, leather biker jackets, silver-only studs, smoky grunge eyes without lashes); minimalist normcore or muted beige palettes; leopard styled as khaki-olive safari; studio softbox glamour and high-fashion polish; traditional Japanese dress, office wear or sportswear.

## Discrimination Cues

**Against Gyaru (the parent umbrella).** Mainstream gyaru — kogal, ageha, himegyaru — keeps a lighter tan or natural skin, softer lash-and-liner work, and a wider palette of pinks, whites, pastels and casual denim. Tsuyome is the strong pole: deeper tan, heavier lashes, leopard plus black-and-gold dominance, chunkier hardware, flashier overall volume. If the look reads sweet, gentle or merely casual with a tan, it is gyaru but not tsuyome.

**Against Rokku.** Rokku gyaru borrows from rock and punk: band T-shirts, leather biker jackets, skinny jeans, silver studs and chains, smoky liner, often darker or ash-streaked hair with less tan emphasis, and animal print incidental at best. Tsuyome keeps gyaru glamour — tan, bleached volume, false lashes, nude lips, leopard, gold rather than silver-only hardware, fur and leg warmers instead of leather layering. Both wear black and studs; the discriminators are leopard-plus-gold-plus-tan-plus-lash versus leather-plus-band-tee-plus-silver-plus-smoke.

**Boundary note.** Against its kuro-gyaru siblings ganguro, manba and Banba, tsuyome is the wearable extreme: tan deep but human, makeup heavy but without neon paint, face stickers or dread extensions. White liner accents may appear at the Banba-leaning edge, but neon never does.
