# International Typographic Style (Swiss Style)

A graphic design manner born in Switzerland and Germany between the 1930s and 1950s that became the default visual language of international communication in the 1960s. It is a print culture above all: posters, exhibition announcements, book covers, corporate logos and public information sheets whose entire ambition is legibility. Ornament is treated as clutter; the grid is treated as ethics. Type is not merely carrier of text but the primary pictorial event — a single word or letterform can be the whole image. Photography enters only as neutral evidence, never as persuasion. The style reads as冷静 civic confidence: a designer who has removed themselves so the message can stand alone.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette
The rule is restraint: one paper neutral, one ink neutral, and at most one to three accent hues, all applied as flat opaque fields with no blending. Typical ranges:
- Paper neutrals: cool white to warm cream, `#FAFAF7` to `#F0EDE4`; also grey papers `#D8D6D0`.
- Ink neutrals: near-black `#0B0B0C` to `#1C1C1E`; mid greys `#8A8A88`, `#B9B7B0` (grey often appears as a second "voice" for overlapping type).
- Signal red / vermilion: `#E2001A` to `#D3352B` — the most characteristic accent, used for small text blocks or a single dominant field.
- Ochre / mustard / orange: `#E8890C` to `#F2C200`.
- Cyan / teal / mid blue: `#0093C4`, `#17A2B8`, `#005EB8`.
- Occasional secondary fields: olive green, maroon, coral salmon (mostly in later revival work); magenta and violet appear almost only as overprint layers, never alone.
Two licensed color behaviors beyond flat fields: (1) **overprint transparency**, where two shapes overlap and the intersection reads as a third mixed hue; (2) **duotone / monochrome photography**, where a photo is reduced to black plus one ink or to pure greyscale. A correct image may be strictly black-white-red or a systematic multi-hue module pattern; both sit inside the palette rule as long as hues stay flat, few, and assigned by system rather than by taste.

### Typography (the highest-ranked motif)
Sans-serif grotesques only: Akzidenz-Grotesk, Univers, Helvetica (and heirs in the same neutral groove). Type behaves structurally: monumental lowercase display words or single letters, often cropped by the page trim; against them, minute functional text — exhibition dates, opening hours, names, credits — set in tight flush-left, ragged-right blocks pinned to a corner or edge. Extreme scale contrast between these two registers is a signature. Letterforms may overlap each other or a shape, producing lighter intersection tones. Wordmarks and logos in the same manner are italicized or slashed grotesques inside geometric containers.

### Grid, asymmetry, geometry (co-equal primary motifs)
Every element sits on an invisible modular column grid; balance is asymmetric, never mirrored. Simple geometric vocabulary: squares, circles, triangles, bars, rotated rectangles and diamonds, usually repeated as a module — a row of identical shapes, a field of dots, a stack of stepped bars — with one element broken or recolored to create the focal point. Rotation (45° squares, diagonal bars) supplies tension while remaining grid-logical. Negative space is an active material: large empty paper or empty black field is normal, not sparse.

### Photography and pictograms
When imagery appears it is objective: black-and-white or single-ink photographs, evenly lit, high contrast, subjects isolated on plain grounds (a hand, a machine, an artifact, a street); photomontage and montage sequences of such photos are licensed to tell a factual sequence. Simplified geometric pictograms and icons stand in for concepts — the "international" promise of wordless legibility. Hand-drawn illustration, decorative ornament and persuasive glamour photography are foreign to the style.

### Materials, texture, lighting
Matte offset lithography on uncoated stock: flat ink, crisp edges, faint paper tooth, occasional slight misregistration where inks overprint. No gradients, no gloss, no emboss, no shadow. "Lighting" exists only inside photographic elements: neutral, shadowless, documentary even light, or stark high-contrast studio light reducing forms to black silhouette against white (or white against black). The overall surface should read as printed matter, not screen render.

### Recurring subjects
Exhibition and museum posters, concert and theatre announcements, civic and corporate identity (airlines, banks, car makers, Olympics and trade fairs), book and paperback covers, editorial spreads, public information and signage systems. Any subject — cultural, commercial, scientific, civic — is admissible once processed through the grid, the grotesque and the limited ink set.

### Composition tendencies
Portrait poster proportions dominate. Common structural habits (any one suffices): a monumental type event crossing the sheet with metadata blocks anchored top and bottom; a white field with a small off-center geometric constellation and corner text; a full-bleed colored or black field carrying a repeated module pattern; a photographic object centered on a plain ground with a banded wordmark. Columns of tiny text run flush left; right edges rag. Cropping at the trim is welcomed for type and shapes alike.

## Mood & Values
Cleanliness, readability, objectivity. The emotional register is calm authority and democratic optimism: information delivered equally to every reader, regardless of language or culture. Anonymity of the designer is a virtue; "form follows function" is the inherited Bauhaus oath. The mood is neither warm nor cold but *fair* — ordered, confident, quietly modern, allergic to rhetoric and propaganda.

## Era & Origins
Roots in 1920s–30s Swiss and German experiments (Ernst Keller, Jan Tschichold) reacting against ornamental pre-war commercial design; consolidated in the 1950s at the Basel School of Design and Zurich's Kunstgewerbeschule by Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, Emil Ruder, Max Bill, Théo Ballmer, Karl Gerstner; typefaces codified in 1957 with Helvetica (Max Miedinger, Basel) and Univers (Adrian Frutiger). Post-WWII international trade made universal visual communication an economic necessity, and the 1960s exported the style to European and North American corporate identity, publishing and institutions. Its later revolt — New Wave / Swiss Punk typography of the 1970s–80s — breaks exactly the grid discipline that defines this style.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
1. Build an invisible modular grid first; place every element on it, then balance asymmetrically.
2. Let type be the image: one neutral grotesque family, monumental lowercase display against minute flush-left ragged-right information blocks.
3. Cap the palette: paper neutral + ink neutral + one to three flat accents; use overprint intersections for extra hues instead of new inks.
4. Use geometry as system: repeat one module (dot, square, bar, triangle, diamond) in rows or fields; break the pattern once for focus.
5. If using photography, make it evidential: monochrome or duotone, even light, high contrast, plain ground; montage sequences allowed.
6. Treat whitespace (or blackspace) as structure; allow cropping of letters and shapes at the trim.
7. Finish as print: matte litho flatness, crisp edges, no digital effects.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter: any civic, cultural, corporate or scientific topic. Field: white/cream paper, full-bleed black, or a single saturated ground. Image engine: pure type, pure geometry, pictogram, objective photo, or overprint layering. Palette handling: monochrome plus one red accent; grey-plus-black type voices; systematic multi-hue modules; duotone photo. Composition: corner-anchored metadata with central module; full-width cropped wordmark; off-center constellation on empty field; banded wordmark over photo. Era flavor: 1950s litho poster, 1960s corporate identity, or contemporary revival poster in the same grammar. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid**
- Serif, script, blackletter or decorative display faces; drop caps; ornamental borders and flourishes.
- Gradients, glows, drop shadows, bevels, 3D renders, neon, glossy screen aesthetics.
- Hand-drawn illustration, painterly texture, grunge distress, paper tears, collage chaos without grid logic (that is New Wave / punk, not ITS).
- Centered, symmetrical, invitation-card layouts; mirrored balance.
- Persuasive glamour or lifestyle color photography with shallow depth of field and dramatic colored lighting.
- More than a handful of unrelated hues; colors chosen decoratively rather than systemically; blended or airbrushed transitions.
- Ornament for ornament's sake: pattern must be modular and informational, never decorative filler.

## Discrimination Cues
- **Bauhaus**: shares geometry and function, but Bauhaus work shows art-school experimentation — primary red/yellow/blue only, visible circle-triangle-square play, axial or centered compositions, experimental geometric lettering; ITS is cooler, grid-disciplined, type-led, admits greys, teals, ochres and objective photography.
- **Constructivism**: agitprop urgency — slashing diagonals, charged red/black photomontage, rhetorical type at angles serving propaganda; ITS is deliberately anti-propaganda, calm, neutral, its asymmetry quiet and grid-bound.
- **De Stijl**: neo-plastic universal harmony — visible black ruling bars, strictly orthogonal primary colors on white; ITS keeps the grid invisible, uses type scale drama, photography and asymmetric crop, never the De Stijl line lattice as picture.
- **Flat Design**: the digital descendant — rounded vector icons, bright saturated multi-color UI palettes, screen gloss-free but screen-scaled; lacks monumental cropped grotesques, tiny metadata blocks, print matte and photographic objectivity.
- **Minimalism**: art-world reduction that may contain no type and no message; ITS always communicates — information hierarchy and functional text are mandatory, minimal surface is a means, not the subject.
- **New Wave / Swiss Punk** (border case): same grotesques but shattered grid, layered chaotic type, punk energy; if the grid visibly collapses, it has left ITS.
- **Plakatstil**: single bold object on flat ground with minimal type; ITS differs by systemic grid, information blocks and photographic neutrality rather than poster-object literalism.
