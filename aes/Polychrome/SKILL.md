# Polychrome

Polychrome is a graphic-design aesthetic of the early 2010s in which color is not applied but *released*: ink spilling into water, pigment powder detonating in front of a flash, smoke curling through a light beam, paint dragged into glossy slicks. These material events are staged against sterile, empty grounds — paper white or void black — and frequently anchored by a desaturated photographic subject (a profile portrait, a product, a logo disc) that the color erupts from, trails behind, or half-swallows. The style took its name from Koan Sound's album *Polychrome*, whose cover art embodies it, and it spread through bass-music compilation sleeves and the advertising of consumer electronics, where a burst of saturated hue became the shorthand for a screen's color fidelity ("colors that come alive"). It is commercial euphoria rendered as physics: chance-driven, natural-mean material behavior framed inside a perfectly controlled studio.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette rule is *wheel-spanning saturation at maximum contrast against the ground*, not any fixed combination. The documented core set runs electric blue (`#1E6FD9`–`#3AA0FF`), turquoise/cyan (`#12C2C2`–`#5FE3E0`), signal red (`#E22B2B`), orange (`#F58220`), yellow (`#FFD23F`), magenta/fuchsia (`#E6308A`) and violet (`#6C3DF4`). Grounds are near-white paper (`#F6F6F4`), near-black void (`#0B0B0D`), or occasionally a pale cream/lilac tint. Within a plume, hues blend through soft gradient edges rather than sitting side by side as flat shapes. Multi-hue bursts typically hold at least one cool (blue/turquoise/violet) and one warm (red/orange/yellow) pole; restricted duotone or monochrome plumes (blue-lavender ink, red-black smoke) are equally valid as long as saturation stays high and the ground stays plain. Marbled variants may vein white, grey and gold (`#C9A227`) through the swirl.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them

1. **Colors bursting and mixing together** — the defining event; color always arrives as an eruption, spill or bloom, never as a fill.
2. **Material carriers**, in the text's own order of emphasis: ink/liquid spills, powder explosions, smoke, paint smears and splatters, oil slicks, blended watercolors, plastic shimmers.
3. **Simple white or black backgrounds** — the empty stage that makes the burst read as an event.
4. **Natural means of generation** — the color must look like captured matter (high-speed photography of powder, macro of ink in water, fluid simulation), not like drawn or vector art.
5. **Commercial and musical packaging frames** — grayscale or neutral subjects, centered logo emblems, thin geometric sans-serif titles set in the leftover negative space; the style's native habitats are album covers, brand advertisements and film posters.

### Materials and textures

Volumetric and particulate above all: the billowing translucent folds of smoke and ink-in-water, with soft internal shadowing; the crisp granular speckle of exploded powder, often with tiny sparkle glints suspended in the cloud; the viscous gloss of stretched paint ribbons and smears; the veined swirl of oil-slick or suminagashi marbling; the feathered bleed edge of watercolor on paper; and, at the glossy extreme, plastic-shimmer highlights on rendered ribbon-like strokes. Every texture answers to a real material behavior — viscosity, diffusion, turbulence, particle ballistics.

### Recurring subjects

Subject matter ranges from *none at all* (pure abstract burst or full-bleed macro fluid field) to a neutral anchor: a grayscale or low-saturation portrait in profile or three-quarter view; an ensemble cast standing in rows; a product (pouch, device, keyboard, car, motorcycle); a logo disc or wordmark; or a silhouette dissolved into powder. The anchor is deliberately muted so the color event owns the image; the color relates to it physically — bursting from the head and shoulders, trailing off a limb, rising as a column beside it, or enveloping it.

### Composition and lighting

Composition is asymmetric and momentum-driven: an eruption anchored to one edge or corner and traveling diagonally; a vertical plume or column; an enveloping cloud that partially occludes the subject; a scattered cluster of splats balanced across empty space; or a centered emblem laid over a symmetric smoke bloom. Negative space is structural — it is where the title, wordmark or tagline lives. Full-bleed macro fields with no ground at all form the abstract pole. Lighting is studio lighting: flash crispness that freezes powder particles with hard edges and leaves the ground shadowless; volumetric backlight or side light that makes smoke folds glow translucently; soft diffuse transmission for ink in water; occasionally a single flare or ray raking across a dark ground. White grounds stay high-key and clean; black grounds stay deep and void-like.

### Typography (when present)

Thin or light-weight geometric sans-serif, widely letterspaced, small-to-medium scale, set in the negative space — sometimes tinted with a subtle blue-to-violet gradient on white, sometimes pure white on black. Logo lockups may sit centered inside a plain circle over the color event. Type never competes with the burst; it whispers beside it.

## Mood & Values

Polychrome feels euphoric, abundant and optimistic — sensory overload held in a clean frame. It expresses faith in technology as vivid life (color as proof of a display's truth), the communal energy of electronic music culture, and mass-market festivity: even a warehouse-club magazine cover gets a confetti detonation. Underneath the polish is a value on *chance and physicality*: the beauty of real matter behaving unpredictably, captured at 1/8000th of a second, rather than designed by hand. Restraint of layout, excess of color — that tension is the style's emotional signature.

## Era & Origins

Emerging in the early 2010s, Polychrome coalesced in electronic-music packaging (Koan Sound's *Polychrome*, bass-music compilations of the period) and was industrialized by consumer-electronics and sportswear advertising — Nintendo, Playstation, Xbox, Windows, Vaio, Lenovo, Apple's iPod, Nike, Adidas, Sony's Triluminos-branded televisions — later persisting in film marketing campaigns that set grayscale casts against colored smoke columns. It reacts against the restrained corporate minimalism and glossy skeuomorphism of the late 2000s by injecting chaotic, physical color, and it was enabled by cheap high-speed photography and accessible fluid-simulation tools. It coexists with, but differs from, flat design: Polychrome keeps photographic materiality even when layout goes minimal.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Ground first: choose plain white, void black, or a pale tint, and keep it empty and shadowless.
2. One color event per image: pick a material behavior (powder burst, smoke plume, ink-in-water cloud, paint smear/splatter, oil-slick marble, watercolor bleed, glossy ribbon shimmer).
3. Saturate: three or more high-contrast hues blending at soft edges, or a coherent duotone plume; contrast against the ground must be maximal.
4. Material truth: render color as matter — particles, folds, viscosity, bleed — never as a flat vector gradient or tint overlay.
5. Optional neutral anchor: a grayscale figure, product, or logo lockup; the color must interact with it physically (erupt from, trail, envelop, stand beside).
6. Studio light: flash freeze, volumetric backlight, or diffuse transmission; no environmental light sources, no cast shadows on the ground.
7. If type appears, use thin geometric sans-serif in the negative space, small and quiet.

**Variation axes**

| Axis | Allowable range |
|---|---|
| Subject | pure abstract; single portrait; ensemble cast; product/packaging; logo or wordmark; device or instrument; vehicle |
| Ground | high-key white; void black; pale cream/lilac; full-bleed macro material with no ground |
| Material | powder; smoke; ink in water; paint smear/splatter; oil-slick marbling; watercolor bleed; glossy plastic ribbon |
| Palette | full-spectrum burst; cool-dominant (blue/lavender/teal); warm-dominant (red/orange/yellow); duotone plume; white-gold marbled veins |
| Composition | corner/edge eruption; vertical column; enveloping cloud; scattered splat cluster; centered emblem over plume; full-bleed field |
| Lighting | flash freeze; backlit volumetric; diffuse transmission; single flare raking a dark ground |
| Mood | euphoric explosion; slow dreamy diffusion; luxurious marbled calm |

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — plain ground, physical color event, saturated high-contrast hues, studio light — not any reference image. A still life, a dancer, a sneaker, a nebula-like ink cloud or a plain logo sleeve can all be perfectly Polychrome without resembling any exemplar.

**Avoid**

- Flat vector gradients, geometric tessellation, triangles, zigzags, arrows or tribal pattern (color must be matter, not shape).
- Earthy, desaturated bohemian palettes (terracotta, mustard, sage) and craft textures.
- Neon line work, hexagonal grids, chrome, holograms, laser beams, cyberpunk interfaces.
- Brushed metal, machinery, or industrial hardware as the hero surface.
- Environmental backgrounds: rooms, streets, landscapes, horizon skies, contextual props.
- Muted monochrome with no color event, or pastel-only washes lacking saturated punch.
- Hand-drawn illustration, paper collage, film grain, vintage or letterpress texture.
- Rainbow tint applied as a flat overlay or filter on a photograph instead of a material event.
- Cluttered multi-element layouts, or color reduced to a small accent; the burst is the subject.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs. Hexatron:** Hexatron's color is *emissive and hard* — neon tubes, laser lines, chrome reflections and digital grids glowing in dark technological space. Polychrome's color is *particulate and fluid* — dust, smoke, ink — lit by studio light on a plain ground. Touch test: if the color could be wiped off as wet paint or breathed in as dust, it is Polychrome; if it can only exist as light on glass or metal, it is Hexatron.
- **Vs. Geo-Boho:** Geo-Boho organizes color into flat geometric pattern (zigzags, triangles, arrows) with bohemian earth tones and woven or craft texture. Polychrome refuses edges and repetition entirely: its color is chaotic, blended, hyper-saturated, and staged on a sterile studio ground rather than a warm artisanal one.
- **Vs. Metalheart:** Metalheart centers metallic surfaces — brushed steel, chrome, machine parts — with a cold, restrained palette and industrial sheen. Polychrome centers soft matter and multi-hue saturation; metal appears at most as a specular glint on a glossy smear, never as the subject or surface language.
- **Internal border check:** a centered logo disc or thin sans-serif title over smoke is still Polychrome (its commercial lockup tradition); but if the plume hardens into wireframe, grid or neon edge-light, the image has left the style. Conversely, an image with no subject at all — only ink folding through water — remains fully Polychrome, because the material color event, not the anchor, is the style's core.
