# Cyberdelia

Cyberdelia — "cybernetic" plus "psychedelic" — is the countercultural aesthetic of the late 1980s through mid-1990s that framed cyberspace as a trip: technology as a gateway to altered consciousness, liberation and transcendence. It grew where the rave scene met early home-computer graphics, living on rave flyers, club visuals, psytrance and acid-house record sleeves, magazine covers, fractal-paint software ads and early CD-ROM worlds. Its visual argument is simple and total: the machine dreams in acid color. Everything glows, everything saturates, and the primitive render — the low-poly blob, the dithered gradient, the fractal swirl — is not a limitation but a sacrament. Crucially, Cyberdelia is optimistic: where its cyberpunk cousin imagines the machine as prison, Cyberdelia imagines it as temple.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)

Cyberdelia operates in two compatible registers, and a given image may blend them:

- **Acid-on-void:** fluorescent, near-maximum-saturation hues burning against darkness. Magenta `#FF00CC–#FF33FF`, cyan `#00CCFF–#66FFFF`, acid green `#33FF00–#00FF99`, hot orange `#FF6600–#FF9900`, electric violet `#6600FF–#9933FF`, lemon yellow `#FFFF00–#FFCC00`, all seated on near-black grounds with a color cast: `#05000F`, `#12002E`, `#1A0033`, or black-red marbled fields for duotone work.
- **Iridescent pastel sheen:** the pearl-pink `#FFB3E6`, lavender `#C9A6FF`, ice-blue `#A6D9FF` and peach `#FFC999` highlights that ride on chrome, liquid-metal and marbled surfaces — never matte pastel fields, always glossy gradient sheen.

The governing rule: **saturation and glow are constant; hue count is free.** Full rainbow polyphony (checker borders, oil-slick marbling, spectrum bursts) is typical, but saturated reductions are equally valid — a cyan-and-orange posterized pair, a red-on-black liquid marble, a violet-and-white radial burst. What never appears is muted, desaturated or earth-toned color.

### Signature motifs, ranked by definitional centrality

1. **Early, primitive CGI.** Low-polygon meshes, wireframe structures, smooth gradient-shaded 3D blobs, chrome extrusions, glossy specular orbs floating in voids. The render must look like 1993 hardware made it: banding, dithering, soft airbrush falloff. (Note: bare wireframe is text-canonical but under-represented in surviving artifacts, which lean toward fractal and marbled renders; wireframe is licensed, not obligatory.)
2. **Fractal and kaleidoscopic structure.** Mandelbrot swirls and spirals, concentric organic tunnels, mirrored mandalas, bilateral poster symmetry, radial starbursts converging on a glowing center.
3. **Rave light-show lighting.** Neon and fluorescent hues against dark grounds, mimicking strobe and blacklight; lens flares and starburst glows as the only light sources.
4. **Sculptural 3D typography.** Text as object: beveled chrome, metallic or iridescent lettering, extruded and orbiting the composition, implying morphing animation even in stills.
5. **Rave culture symbols.** The smiley face is canonical in the defining literature though scarce in surviving printed artifacts; the persistent carrier is the flyer apparatus itself — corner text blocks, line-up schedules, logo clusters orbiting central artwork.
6. **Hacking and nascent-internet iconography.** The @ glyph, terminals, cyberspace grids, and techno-mystic sigils (pyramids, eyes, peace marks) rendered as 3D still-life objects.
7. **Glitch and digital distortion.** Data-moshing is named in the literature; in artifacts it surfaces more often as posterization, solarization of photographs, melted marbling and warped checker grids. Treat glitch as ornament, secondary to the render.

### Materials and textures

Liquid-marble and oil-slick swirls mapped onto spheres and blobs; polished chrome and liquid metal; glossy plastic orbs with hard specular hits; airbrushed soft-gradient halos; dithered pixel gradients and visible banding; fractal filigree and noise bloom; holographic foil sheen; halftone and print grain where the artifact is a photographed or scanned flyer; warped checker and plaid planes used as backdrops.

### Recurring subjects

Rave flyers and club posters; psytrance, acid-house and ambient record sleeves; magazine covers and fractal-paint software advertisements; book covers about surfing cyberspace; dreamlike game worlds; posterized or solarized human heads and bodies merged with digital space (figures tinted flat cyan or orange, mouths open in euphoric shout); cyber-organic hybrids — mushrooms, eyes, flowers, sunflowers, leaves rebuilt as digital mandalas; floating icon still-lifes of orbs, glyphs and frames in dark space.

### Composition and lighting

Composition organizes chaos radially or symmetrically: center-weighted mandalas, vortex tunnels pulling to a luminous vanishing point, bilateral mirror axes, or collage constellations of 3D objects adrift in void. Warped receding grids and tilted planes add motion. Flyer pragmatism permits dense corner text. Empty space is black void, never white margin — horror vacui filled with glow. Lighting is entirely self-emissive: objects radiate, flares bloom, chrome reflects impossible environments. No sun, no daylight, no softbox, no photographic depth of field.

## Mood & Values

Euphoric, utopian, transcendent, playfully wacky. Cyberdelia expresses the conviction that the personal computer is a consciousness-expanding instrument — the "PC is the LSD of the 1990s" ethos — and that virtual immersion promises a more enlightened, decentralized, communal society. Its mysticism (mandalas, pyramids, eyes) is sincere, not ironic; its machines are doors, not cages. Communal rave joy and techno-optimism are the emotional baseline.

## Era & Origins

Rooted philosophically in 1960s psychedelia via figures like Timothy Leary, the style emerged in the late 1980s rave scene and early computer-art tooling, peaked in the mid-1990s with touchstones like *The Lawnmower Man* (1992), *Hackers* (1995) and its nightclub "Cyberdelia," and game worlds such as *LSD: Dream Emulator* and *Worlds.com*. It faded by the late 1990s, displaced by the sleeker Metalheart and Y2K Futurism looks and later by the dot-com crash, with periodic revivals since. It reacts simultaneously against analog hippie psychedelia (by digitizing it) and against cyberpunk pessimism (by rejecting it).

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Render as if on early-90s hardware: gradient-shaded primitives, low-poly facets, dithering, banding, airbrushed glow. Primitivism is the point.
2. Everything glows in darkness; light is emissive, flared, blacklit.
3. Saturate fully; choose rainbow polyphony or a saturated duotone/tritone reduction.
4. Order the chaos radially, symmetrically, or as a vortex toward a luminous center.
5. If text appears, it is sculpture: beveled chrome, iridescent metal, extruded 3D lettering.
6. Glitch, posterize and warp as seasoning over the render, never as the whole dish.
7. Keep the temperature utopian: euphoric, mystical, playful — never grim.

**Variation axes.** Subject: abstract fractal field, flyer, record or magazine cover, software ad, interface, posterized figure, cyber-organic hybrid, icon still-life. Composition: mandala, tunnel, bilateral mirror, floating collage, warped grid, tilted printed artifact. Lighting: starburst core, uniform blacklight, flat posterized light, chrome reflection. Color: full spectrum, two-hue posterization, pastel iridescence over void. Mood: rave euphoria, meditative mysticism, wacky play. Medium look: screen render, scanned print, photographed flyer on a surface. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — acid palette, primitive-CGI texture, radial or vortex order, utopian glow — not any reference image.**

**Avoid**

- Dystopian noir: rain-slick streets, trench coats, grit, cynical mood (cyberpunk).
- Sleek minimalism: white margins, flat vector shapes, thin sans-serif type, corporate chrome-on-white (Metalheart / Y2K).
- Modern CGI realism: ray-traced reflections, PBR materials, photoreal skin and subsurface scattering.
- Pure analog psychedelia: hand-inked 1960s poster swirls and woodcut mandalas with no digital fingerprint (Hippie / Psychedelia).
- Vaporwave: desaturated pastel pink-blue, classical busts, mall irony.
- Glitch as sole subject with no psychedelic or rave framing (Glitch Art).
- Earth tones and muted color: beige, brown, olive, cream, dusty terracotta, grey.
- Natural light: daylight, golden hour, window light, photographic bokeh.
- Contemporary flat UI, emoji, clean geometric sans-serif typography.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Their tell | Cyberdelia's difference |
|---|---|---|
| 90s Cool | Restrained photographic layouts, muted or monochrome palette, clean grids, breathing room | Maximalist, self-emissive, CGI-saturated; void instead of whitespace |
| Acid Design | Distorted chrome type and xerox collage as pure graphic-design experiment; can be monochrome | Always carries the early-CGI or fractal fingerprint and the cyberspace-transcendence narrative; type sits inside a rendered digital space |
| Cyberpunk | Night streets, rain, noir shadow, leather, high-tech-low-life dread | Same chrome and neon, but glowing in fractal voids and rave spaces with euphoric optimism; hope, not dread |
| Demoscene | Realtime tech-demo vocabulary: rotating logos, scrollers, copper bars, starfields, cooler metallic primaries | Adds fractal organic growth, mandalas, techno-mystic sigils, rave-flyer apparatus and psychedelic hue storms |
| Early Cyber | Sterile wireframe grids, vector neon, terminal green/amber, Tron-like cleanliness | The psychedelic, rave-inflected subset: acid color blooms, mystical symmetry, marbled chrome over clinical wireframe |

When in doubt, apply the mood test: if the machine looks like a way out rather than a way down, and the color could only exist on a blacklight-lit dancefloor or a 1994 CRT, you are looking at Cyberdelia.
