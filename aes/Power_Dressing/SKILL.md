# Power Dressing

Power Dressing is the uniform of arrival: the style adopted by the first large generation of women entering male-dominated corporate, legal and political spheres in the late 1970s and 1980s, in the United States and United Kingdom, and worn by men of the same boardroom class as part of the Yuppie moment. Its logic is sartorial translation — the man's suit jacket re-cut onto the woman's body, shoulders padded past the natural line, waist cinched, skirt narrowed to a pencil — so that competence and ambition become visible before a word is spoken. It was codified in advice literature (*Dress for Success*, 1975), built by designers (Armani, Claude Montana, Thierry Mugler, Donna Karan, Calvin Klein, Yves Saint Laurent, Balmain), worn in public by figures like Margaret Thatcher, Joan Collins and Grace Jones, and amplified by *Working Girl*, *Dynasty*, *Dallas*, *Miami Vice* and *American Gigolo*. Everything in the look serves one sentence: I belong in this room, and I intend to run it.

## Visual Identity

### Colour palette

The rule is a conservative foundation with at most one loud note. Base tones dominate the garment area; the accent, when present, arrives as a coat, a tie, a blouse or a lip — never as an all-over print clash.

- **Base neutrals:** black `#0E0E10–#1A1A1E`; charcoal `#33363B–#4B4F55`; navy `#1C2740–#2E3E5C`; mid greys of suiting wool `#6E7278–#9BA0A6`; chalk and ivory for blouses and pinstripe lines `#EDEDE8–#F4F1EA`.
- **Patterned greys:** glen check / Prince-of-Wales overcheck and herringbone sit in the `#8A8D91` family with darker `#4A4E54` crossings; pinstripes read as fine white threads on navy or charcoal.
- **Documented bold accent:** scarlet to crimson red `#B0182A–#D2232A` — the style's signature interruption of the conservative field.
- **Period secondary accents (occasional, never dominant):** sage/mint `#A8C3B0`, taupe and mushroom brown `#8B7D6F–#4A3A2E`.
- **Hardware and trim:** polished gold `#C9A227`, pewter silver `#8E9196`, black leather, black fur.

Allowed colour behaviours: monochrome head-to-toe in black, navy or charcoal; suiting patterns in grey; a single bold red garment over a black or neutral base; head-to-toe red as the maximal statement. Disallowed: multi-colour clashes, neons, candy pastels, rainbow prints.

### Silhouette and signature motifs (ranked by how firmly the style documents them)

1. **Wide, padded shoulders** — the defining feature. The shoulder line extends angularly beyond the body, squaring off the top of the figure and mimicking the masculine jacket block.
2. **The power suit** — matching tailored blazer plus pencil skirt (knee or below) or pleated, tapered trousers; the cornerstone garment.
3. **Sharp blazer construction** — double-breasted button stances, peaked lapels, military or gold buttons, nipped waist, sometimes a peplum or a wide belt with a metallic buckle; long oversized coats carry the same shoulder.
4. **Simple silk or satin blouse** underneath, occasionally with a contrast collar, bow or necktie at the throat.
5. **Wide belts and cinched waists**, defining the hourglass against the broadened shoulder.
6. **Bold statement jewelry** — substantial gold earrings, brooches, cuff-scale pieces; polish rather than delicacy.
7. **High heels** (pumps, slingbacks) with sheer or subtly textured hosiery.
8. **Neat, voluminous coiffed hair** — the blown-out bob or swept-up mane, groomed and set, never loose or casual.
9. **Authority props:** structured briefcase or handbag, leather gloves, fur cuffs, stoles or collars as the luxury edge.
10. **Men's variant:** pinstripe or charcoal suit, crisp white shirt, the bold "power tie" (red above all), briefcase in hand.

### Materials and textures

Worsted wool, wool crepe and gabardine with a crisp pressed crease; silk and satin blouses with a controlled sheen against matte suiting; smooth leather in belts, gloves and bags; fur as trim rather than as coat-length excess; metallic buttons, buckles and jewelry catching a single highlight; fine-gauge knit turtlenecks as an under-layer variant. The surface language is *constructed*: lapels roll cleanly, skirts hold their line, trousers keep a crease. Nothing slouches, nothing wrinkles.

### Recurring subjects

Professional women as the central figure — executives, lawyers, politicians, editors, entrepreneurs — plus runway and catalogue models carrying the same garments, and men in pinstripe boardroom dress. Figures appear solo or in pairs and small groups of colleagues. The body language is part of the subject: hand on hip, arms folded, chin lifted, direct level gaze, or a mid-stride walk that occupies space rather than apologizing for it.

### Composition, lighting and medium

- **Framing:** predominantly vertical, full-body or three-quarter, the figure dominant with generous negative space around it — the grammar of catalogue pages and print advertisements. Profile views are used to show the sculptural shoulder line; slight low angles amplify authority.
- **Settings:** seamless studio backdrops in off-white or grey; architectural status interiors (marble columns, stone halls, checkerboard marble floors, office lobbies); city streets with period props such as a sedan or luggage; runway strips. The setting always implies institution, money or movement between them.
- **Lighting:** even, soft studio flash with minimal shadow for catalogue work; diffused daylight for street and exterior editorial; soft bounce off stone in interiors. Light describes tailoring edges rather than dramatizing them.
- **Medium character:** 1980s print advertising and catalogue photography — slight film grain, glossy-reproduction colour cast (a touch warm or gently desaturated), sometimes marginal typography of the ad page. This finish is characteristic, not mandatory.

## Mood & Values

Authority, ambition, professionalism, confidence, and gender equality in the workplace — the values the style was invented to declare. The mood is armor, not allure: glamour exists (fur, gold, red, the *Dynasty* sheen) but it is glamour in service of command. There is optimism in it, the "greed is good" era's belief that the individual can seize the institution, and there is seriousness: every element says the wearer was dressed for a meeting that matters. Posture carries the ethics — shoulders back, gaze unflinching, space claimed.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in the late 1970s, dominant through the 1980s, in the US and UK. Its conceptual roots reach back to Coco Chanel and Elsa Schiaparelli borrowing masculine ease for women, and to Yves Saint Laurent's 1966 "Le Smoking" tuxedo, which first turned a male garment into a symbol of female power. The trend solidified as women entered high-powered corporate and political roles in number, was championed by Armani and by Margaret Thatcher's public wardrobe, and softened into understated forms in the 1990s as the decade's excess receded, with periodic revivals since. It reacts against both the soft femininity expected of earlier professional women and the casualness of contemporaneous leisurewear: this is clothing as credential.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the silhouette before anything else: shoulder line wider than hip line, waist defined by cut or belt, skirt narrow or trouser tapered. The inverted triangle is the style's fingerprint.
2. Keep palette discipline: conservative field, at most one bold accent, red first among accents.
3. Make tailoring credible — lapels, button stance, creases and pads must read as engineered cloth, never soft drape.
4. Pose as power: the figure occupies space, meets the camera, chin level or raised.
5. Add exactly one register of luxury — fur trim, gold jewelry, leather gloves, a marble hall — never logomania.
6. Optionally finish in the period medium: film grain, print-ad colour cast, vertical catalogue framing.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a woman or a man, solo, paired or in a group; an executive, politician, lawyer, editor, model or entrepreneur of any adult age. Setting may be a seamless studio, a marble lobby, a checkerboard stone hall, a city street with a period car or luggage, a runway, or a boardroom interior. Composition may range from full-figure catalogue standing poses to three-quarter editorial crops, profile shoulder studies, walking strides or seated conversational groupings. Lighting may be flat studio flash, overcast daylight, or warm interior bounce. Colour handling may be monochrome black/navy/charcoal, grey glen check or pinstripe, neutral base with a red accent, or head-to-toe red; rare period secondary accents like sage or taupe are acceptable. Mood may run from severe and cool to glamorous and triumphant. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A boardroom portrait, a street crossing, a runway frame or an abstracted studio study all qualify equally if the silhouette, palette and bearing hold.

**Avoid — elements that break the style.**
- Soft, deconstructed, unstructured or slouchy tailoring; dropped or unpadded shoulders; wrinkled cloth (this crosses into 1990s Minimalism or contemporary ease).
- Modern skinny-fit suits, cropped trousers with sneakers, athleisure, denim, grunge flannel, hoodies.
- Neon colours, candy pastels, head-to-toe pastel linen, tropical or novelty prints (Miami Vice / Yuppie leisure territory).
- Repeated visible logos and monogram mania; luxury here is material, not branded.
- Preppy and old-money props: sweaters knotted over shoulders, polo shirts, pearl strands with country tweed, riding gear, scarves-and-tweed club wear (Preppy, New Money, Bon Chic Bon Genre).
- Contemporary office realism: lanyards, ID badges, open-plan glass cubicles, modern laptops and smartphones (Corpcore).
- Slip dresses, sheer fabrics, beige tonal draping, ornament-free reduction (Minimalism).
- Sexualized styling: bare midriffs, micro hemlines, undone hair. Hems stay at or below the knee; grooming stays set; authority outranks allure.
- Costume-parody exaggeration: shoulders may be broad but must remain plausible tailored cloth, not comic prosthetics.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | Their tell | Power Dressing's difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Corpcore** | Modern office realism: badges, lanyards, boxy unisex cuts, fluorescent light, mundane irony | 1980s aspirational armor: padded shoulder, heroic pose, glossy print-ad finish, no office-tech clutter |
| **Minimalism** | Reduction: no padding, no jewelry, drape over structure, beige-and-black quiet | Additive and constructed: pads, belts, brooches, pinstripe and check, deliberate silhouette drama |
| **New Money** | Leisure wealth: tweed, pearls, cashmere, country club and estate settings | Work context and urban institution; worsted suiting as armor, not weekend cloth |
| **Yuppie** | Same era, consumption-focused: suspenders, polos, sweaters over shoulders, pastel linen, leisure props | Keeps the suit as authority rather than lifestyle; women are central subjects; no leisurewear leakage |
| **Bon Chic, Bon Genre** | Parisian old-money restraint: soft unpadded tweed, discreet scarf jewelry, understatement | Assertive Anglo-American corporate line: exaggerated shoulder, bold red accent, statement gold |
| **Preppy** | Campus-derived khaki, madras, loafers, no shoulder construction | Boardroom-derived tailoring with pads and pencil lines; adulthood and office, not campus |

**Quick recognition checklist:** shoulders wider than hips; tailored blazer with peaked or double-breasted closure; pencil skirt or creased tapered trouser; navy/charcoal/black/grey field with optional red strike; coiffed voluminous hair; gold statement jewelry or brooch; heels and sheer hose; briefcase, gloves or fur as prop; bearing of someone who owns the room; finish of a 1980s print advertisement.

*Evidence note:* the men's "power tie" and briefcase variant is documented in the style's definition but appears far less in surviving imagery than the women's suit; street, runway and studio catalogue framings all occur within the style, and architectural status props (marble, checkerboard stone) are one available setting among several, not a requirement.
