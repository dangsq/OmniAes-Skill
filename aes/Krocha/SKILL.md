# Krocha

Krocha (also "Kracher" or "Netlog Styler") was a short-lived, extremely loud Austrian youth fad that formed in Vienna, Linz and Graz around 2005, peaked in 2008 and died within the same year. It was a "pure fun culture": partying, consumerism, brand display and trend-following, with no political content whatsoever. Its imagery is therefore the imagery of teenage self-documentation — contest stages in shopping malls, funfair platforms, disco entrances, DJ booths — shot on cheap compact cameras and early phone cameras and uploaded to Netlog, YouTube and MyVideo. A secondary, commercial imagery exists: compilation album covers ("Krocha Traxx", "Krocha Hits – Bam Oida") produced by Universal Music Austria in a Vectordelia-derived vector style. Both registers belong to the aesthetic.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette.** The rule is *daylight-bright neon accents locked against a black-and-white base*, with solarium tan as the warm anchor. Neons appear as caps, scarves, tees and graphic fields; black and white carry the clothing bodies (black hoodies and tees, white sneakers, black/white keffiyeh check, black/white argyle). Approximate ranges: hot pink/magenta `#FF0080–#FF5CB8`, fluorescent yellow-green `#B6FF00–#66FF33`, canary yellow `#FFD500–#FFEE00`, base black `#000000–#202020` and pure white `#FFFFFF`, with artificial-tan skin sitting at `#B96A32–#E29A55` and metallic gold/silver jewelry as sparkle accents. In the graphic register the field flips: pink, yellow and fluorescent green become full backgrounds with white and black silhouettes on top.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the text documents them.**
1. **Vokuhila (mullet)** — short gelled spikes on top, long at the back, often bleached blonde, sometimes with shaved patterns at the sides; the single most iconic marker. Girls' hair: flat-ironed, bleached blonde or dyed black.
2. **Keffiyeh (Palestinian scarf)** worn loosely around the neck as pure fashion, in black/white check or in neon/pink colorways; fringe visible.
3. **Neon caps** (Ed Hardy above all), trucker and snapback shapes, frequently with words, phrases or logos; caps and tees carry text.
4. **Deep artificial tan** on both sexes — solarium bronze, glossy and obvious.
5. **V-neck t-shirts**, tight jeans (boxer waistband peeking above the beltline), white sneakers or boots.
6. **Large flashy belt buckles**, gold/silver chains, keychains hanging from pants.
7. **Brand logos as decoration**: Ed Hardy first, then D&G, De Puta Madre 69, Baxmen, Angel Devil, VANS.
8. **Argyle / harlequin diamond patterns** on caps, vests and scarves (strongly evidenced in the photography even though the text does not name it).
9. **Slang typography**: Austro-Pidgin phrases ("Oida", "Fix", "Bam Oida", "So Fesch") written with over-capitalization and distorted spellings ("SüZze", "SchnuQQi").

**Materials and textures.** Gel-stiffened hair with hard shine; woven cotton scarf fringe; jersey cotton tees; thin denim; faux-leather jackets; polished metal jewelry; laminated mall signage and painted funfair booths in the background. Photographic texture matters: direct-flash specular highlights on tanned foreheads, red-hot skin saturation, compression blocks and motion smear in video stills.

**Recurring subjects.** Teenagers, almost never over 18: packs of friends squeezed into frame, boy-girl group portraits, pairs of girls with scarves, DJs at decks, solo dancers mid-move on a funfair ride platform, contest participants on a mall stage. Crowds, press backdrops and mall atriums behind them.

**Composition and lighting.** Three legitimate registers: (a) *compact-flash snapshot* — tight group framing, faces squared to the lens, peace signs and pointing fingers, arms over shoulders, harsh on-camera flash falling off into a dark or cluttered red-lit party background; (b) *low-bitrate video still* — small frame, motion blur and trailing limbs, overcast daylight or flat fairground light, scanlines and compression artifacts, sometimes a caption line burned in; (c) *vector cover art* — flat saturated field, black silhouette of a dancer posing with scarf or cap, splatters, starbursts, halftone dots, flourishes and bubble/graffiti lettering in mixed case. Note honestly: the text states graphic design was a *minor*, industry-produced arm of the trend; the photographic registers carry the style.

## Mood & Values

Boisterous, status-proud, deliberately tasteless-by-adult-standards fun. Krocha images radiate the confidence of teenagers showing off for their own peer network: brand labels flashed, tans displayed, gestures aimed at the camera like a greeting to the Netlog feed. There is no irony inside the original images (irony arrived later, via media mockery and the satirical "Krocha Hymne"), no politics, no darkness — only consumption, dancing and belonging.

## Era & Origins

Vienna, Austria, c. 2005–2008, peak and collapse both in 2008. A child of Netlog, YouTube, MyVideo and forum culture, spread by phone-camera video; musically tied to Schranz, Hardstyle, Jumpstyle and Hands Up; danced with wild jumping and kicking (Jumpstyle legs plus a touch of Charleston). Sometimes compared to Dutch Gabber in energy but explicitly apolitical and multi-ethnic. Media contempt ("the worst of the 80s") and compilation-album exploitation mark its afterlife; a small Korean dance community ("TK Krocha") keeps the movement side alive.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Assemble the uniform checklist: black/white base garment + at least one neon accent + keffiyeh at the neck + cap or mullet + visible tan.
2. Keep subjects teenage and social; solo figures should still perform *for* a camera.
3. Commit fully to one media register (flash snapshot, compressed video still, or vector cover) including its artifacts.
4. Set the scene in consumer leisure space: mall atrium, funfair, disco entrance, contest stage, DJ booth, street in front of shops.
5. Use boastful-friendly posing: peace signs, finger-pointing at lens, arms locked over shoulders, mid-kick jumps.
6. Discipline the color: neon only as accent against black/white in photography; pink/yellow/green fields reserved for the graphic register; tan skin as the constant warm note.
7. If text appears, use distorted-capitalization slang or logo-style brand lettering, never clean minimalist type.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any party, mall event, fairground, street, DJ setup, dance contest, album cover or annotated style diagram. Composition may range from cramped group portrait to full-body candid dance to flat silhouette poster. Lighting may be direct flash, mall fluorescent, overcast daylight or flat vector color. Color handling may shift which neon leads (pink vs. green vs. yellow) as long as the black/white base holds. Mood may slide from sincere fun to satirical exaggeration. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Earth tones, muted boho or matte pastel palettes; oversized modern streetwear silhouettes (Krocha fits tight); any political framing of the keffiyeh (protest signs, flags, slogans) — the text insists it is apolitical fashion; adults as core subjects; 90s rave smileys, PLUR beads, pacifiers, warehouse UV haze; Tecktonik's sleek French clubwear and arm-wave geometry; grunge flannel and band-tee distress; high-fashion softbox editorial polish or clean contemporary computational phone photography; natural matte hair without gel shine or straightened gloss; minimalist typography; Scandinavian suburban sportswear minimalism or Italian car-culture flashiness as the setting.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Hands Up** — a genre scene, not a uniform: club night imagery, raised hands, trance euphoria, laser haze. Krocha keeps its costume (scarf, cap, mullet, tan) and appears in daytime consumer spaces, with leg-driven kick dancing rather than hands-aloft swaying.
- **Partille-Johnny** — the Swedish near-twin (gel hair, cap, neck scarf) but rooted in suburban sportswear and lager-can badboy codes, without the solarium tan creed, the mullet doctrine, the neon argyle or the Ed Hardy logo-maximalism; Krocha is louder, blacker-and-neoner, and Austrian.
- **Rave** — 90s rave means baggy silhouettes, smiley/PLUR iconography, whistles and dark warehouses; Krocha means tight jeans, brand logos, tanned skin and bright malls and funfairs.
- **Tecktonik** — same year, French: sleeker clubwear, geometric arm movements, androgynous fringes and eyeliner, dark strobe clubs. Krocha answers with kicking jumpstyle legwork, mullets, tans, keffiyehs and snapshot/video-still media texture.
- **Truzzo** — the Italian flashy stereotype: tuned cars, sunglasses, chest chains, designer belts, an older Mediterranean palette; no keffiyeh-mullet-neon-cap uniform and no vector cover grammar.
- **Scene / Vectordelia (graphic neighbors)** — Krocha cover art borrows Vectordelia's silhouettes, flourishes and splatters but restricts itself to white/pink/yellow/fluorescent green, dancing poses with scarf or cap, and Austro-Pidgin bubble lettering; Scene imagery centers online self-portraits with band tees and side-swept fringes instead.
