# Neofolk

Neofolk is a post-industrial music subculture born in early-1980s London from post-punk and industrial, which turned the acoustic folk form toward apocalyptic lament, European traditionalism, paganism and the historical trauma of the twentieth century. Its imagery behaves like an archive of a Europe that believes itself destroyed: high-contrast, largely monochromatic photographs and degraded printed matter in which archaic symbols — runic alphabets and medieval heraldry above all — are laid visibly on top of the physical remains of the World Wars: memorials, statues, military graveyards, weathered stone. The style is deliberately coded. Its insignia sit ambiguously between irony and sincerity, a provocation held in suspension, and that ambiguity is itself a visual property: nothing in neofolk declares, everything alludes. An honest caveat belongs here: the scene's founding circle entangled itself with far-right philosophy, and the imagery's provocative charge is documented fact, not accidental edge. This skill describes the visual grammar of that coding without endorsing any politics behind it.

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule

Monochrome is the default state; color is an intrusion and must behave like one. The working range:

| Role | Hex range | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Ground black / charcoal | #0B0B0B–#2E2E2E | crushed, solid blacks; backgrounds, cloth, print fields |
| Bone / parchment white | #E9E4D4–#F4F0E6 | chalky, never luminous; paper panels, type, highlights |
| Field grey | #6E7168–#8A8D82 | stone, concrete, studio seamless, silver-gelatin midtones |
| Olive drab | #4B5320–#6B705C | the only permitted hue family, always greyed toward khaki |
| Oxblood accent | #7E1416–#9A1F1F | flat ink only — a wordmark, a rubric line; never light, never gradient |

Duotones drifting toward sepia parchment or olive grey are acceptable as print aging. Saturated hue families, gradients and glow are outside the style. Evidence for color beyond monochrome is thin: a single flat red wordmark is roughly the extent of it, so treat richer color as a style break.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Runes, especially Algiz and Sowilo** — the style's first signature: carved, stitched or painted onto fabric and fur, printed in rows across paper headers, worn as iron or silver jewelry.
2. **Medieval heraldry** — lions rampant, crowned shields, symmetrical crests, engraved circular seals and sigils ringed with lettering; the emblem-as-page composition descends from this.
3. **Totenkopf** — the skull as carved architectural ornament, as insignia, as memento mori rather than metal shock.
4. **Camouflage and military surplus** — woodland camo jackets, camouflage netting as a texture ground, canvas bags, combat boots.
5. **1930s formal and intellectual wear** — turtleneck sweaters, double-breasted trench coats, high-waisted trousers, shirt-and-tie rows in montage.
6. **Woodcuts** —粗 cut linework for figures, wolves and ornamental borders; carved frames enclosing photographs.
7. **War remains** — war memorials, classical and socialist-realist statuary, military graveyards, eroded stone.
8. **Acoustic guitars** — held plainly, as object rather than performance prop.

A secondary register is documented by the scene's photography and printed matter rather than by the motif list: taxidermy crows and ravens, leaf masks and held branches, draped-cloth still-life tableaux with framed pictures and cut flowers, handwritten ink lyric sheets headed by rune rows, blackletter wordmarks, torn-paper xerox collage panels, and geometric sunwheel or barred-cross emblems set inside circles. Field recordings are sonic, not visual; they surface only indirectly as the paraphernalia of handwritten sheets and tape-era print.

### Materials and textures

Xerox grain and halftone dot, scratched negative, matte silver-gelatin print, torn paper edges, blackletter print columns used as background texture, weathered concrete and stone, wool and canvas military cloth, camo netting mesh, iron and silver metalwork, taxidermy feather, dried leaf, ink on paper. Surfaces should look copied, buried or exhumed — never rendered.

### Recurring subjects

Solitary figures in uniform-disciplined dress; masked or hooded figures (grille masks, leaf masks); classical and memorial sculpture; architectural skull carvings; overgrown fields, forest edges, winter grass; domestic occult tableaux; emblem pages, book and album covers; lyric sheets; repeated identical figures in montage rows.

### Composition and lighting

Two compositional poles. The **emblem page**: centered crest or seal on a black ground, heraldic symmetry, ornate woodcut border, vertical type runs, seal ringed by text. The **documentary snapshot**: frontal bust or full-body portrait against a flat grey seamless, harsh direct flash in a dark interior, or overcast field light among grass and stone. Between them, the **layering rule**: the past sits visibly on top of the present — a photograph panel collaged over a textured ground, a symbol printed across an image, torn edges exposing an underlayer. Lighting descends from 1920s German Expressionist cinema: hard sidelight, deep shadow, heavy grain — or else flat archival scan light with no modeled shadow at all. Contrast stays high; blacks crush to solid, whites stay chalky.

## Mood & Values

Mourning, lament, ritual solemnity, archival coldness. The style expresses anti-modernism, cultural lament, traditionalism and historical trauma: a belief that the sacred was stripped from Europe by Enlightenment, industry and the wars. Obscure history and esoteric symbol function as insider status. Intimacy and vastness coexist — a whispered voice, a continent's ruin. Provocation is a posture, but it is delivered deadpan, never winked. Nothing celebratory, cozy or ironic-pop belongs here.

## Era & Origins

Early 1980s, London post-industrial scene: Death in June (1981, from the punk band Crisis), then Current 93 and Sol Invictus; overlapping labels "apocalyptic folk," "folk noir," "dark folk." A reaction against modern rationalist society and against industrial's own electronics. Visual ancestry: 19th-century Romanticism, interwar military photography, German Expressionist film, medieval woodcut and heraldry; philosophical backdrop in Julius Evola and the Conservative Revolution, whose contested politics shaped the imagery's coded ambiguity.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** 1) Begin monochrome; add color only as a flat oxblood ink accent or a greyed olive duotone. 2) Degrade the surface: grain, halftone, scratch, torn edge, xerox contrast. 3) Layer epochs: archaic symbol over twentieth-century remain, photograph over archival texture. 4) Dress figures with discipline, not costume — surplus wool, canvas, trench coats, turtlenecks. 5) Compose either heraldically (centered emblem, symmetry, carved border) or documentarily (frontal portrait, overcast field), never cinematically. 6) Keep insignia coded and archaic — runes, heraldry, skulls, barred crosses, sunwheels — poised between irony and sincerity. 7) Let typography be archival: blackletter, rune rows, handwritten ink. 8) Light flatly or expressionistically; never glossily.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across statuary, memorials, graveyards, fields, uniformed or masked portraits, still-life tableaux, emblem pages, lyric sheets, book covers. Composition may range from centered seal to torn collage to ornate framed portrait to snapshot. Lighting may range from flat scan light to harsh flash to overcast field light to expressionist chiaroscuro. Color handling may range from pure black-and-white to sepia parchment to olive duotone to black-plus-one-red. Mood may range from grief to defiance to ritual calm to archival coldness. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Saturated color, gradients, neon, digital glow, HDR gloss. Clean vector flatness without grain; crisp commercial product lighting. Goth theatricality: lace, velvet, corsets, romantic graveyard mist, eyeliner glamour, purple and wine palettes. Metal iconography: flames, chrome lettering, spikes. Dungeon synth's painterly fantasy: castles, dragons, knights, misty digital painting. Forestpunk technology: LEDs, circuits, drones, futuristic gear in woodland. Post-punk modernism: bold sans-serif grids, primary color blocks, urban concrete irony, bright-ink Dada cut-ups. Martial industrial's clean propaganda geometry and parade polish, unless degraded into mourning. Pastoral celebratory folk: sunlit meadows, dancing crowds, bright costume. Literal, unambiguous hate insignia such as swastikas or explicit party emblems — the documented vocabulary is coded and ambiguous, and literal declaration breaks the style's central tension. Modern brand logos, smartphones, contemporary streetwear.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Goth** shares monochrome and mourning but theatricalizes: velvet, lace, silver shine, romantic graveyard, glamorous makeup. Neofolk replaces theater with uniform discipline, surplus cloth, memorial stone and grain.
- **Martial industrial** shares military iconography but thinks monumentally: constructivist montage, parade formations, crisp propaganda poster geometry, brass-and-banner polish. Neofolk thinks archivally and acoustically: xerox grain, woodcut line, intimate tableau, folk-scale grief. Constructivist montage at the edge of a neofolk set should still read degraded and elegiac.
- **Post-punk** shares the era and the xerox but keeps modernist irony: sans-serif type, urban concrete, bright ink accents, detached wit. Neofolk swaps irony for lament and sans-serif for blackletter and runes.
- **Dungeon synth** is illustrative fantasy — painted castles, dragons, fictional architecture, misty digital color. Neofolk is photographic and archival, anchored in real war remains and emblem pages; no fantasy creatures.
- **Forestpunk** fuses woodland with technology: neon accents, circuits, futuristic gear. Neofolk's forest is analog, memorial and pre-electronic — grass, stone, runes, tape-era print.

**Quick test:** grain or xerox degradation + monochrome or greyed olive + an archaic symbol (rune, crest, skull) + a twentieth-century war remain or uniform-disciplined figure + mournful, deadpan solemnity = neofolk.
