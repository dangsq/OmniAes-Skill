# Magician (Magiciancore / Stage-Magician Core / Magic-Showcore / Illusionistcore)

The Magician aesthetic centers on stage magicians, magic shows, grandiose performance and elaborate illusion. Its lineage runs from the 1700s public conjuror through music-hall and vaudeville billposting, early studio photographs of levitation acts, and mid-century stage glamour, into a modern branch of leather-and-chains street illusionism. Named as an online aesthetic on Tumblr (described as "Wizardcore with a modern twist and a flair for the dramatic"), it keeps one governing idea: magic as a *performance of deception* — wonder manufactured by tailoring, props, lighting and misdirection, never by genuine sorcery. Everything in the image should read as part of an act: the costume, the prop, the curtain, the printed promise on a poster.

## Visual Identity

### Palette
The rule is a dark jewel-tone ground carrying bright metallic or electric accents. Base hues sit in vintage red/burgundy, deep blue, emerald green, dark purple and black; against them snap gold, yellow, silver, cyan or lime green — a spotlight glint on velvet, gold leaf on black cloth, a cyan flash behind a card fan. A second, documented register inverts this: mostly light colors faded to cream, dusty rose and pale teal for a vintage, lighthearted poster or paper-canopy look. A third, horror-leaning register drains saturation further into dreary sepia, ash and near-black, trading sparkle for gloom. What never appears is full-saturation candy color or neutral corporate greyscale.

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual prominence)
1. **Top hats**, and the **rabbits** pulled from them — the style's foremost emblem; doves perched on brims belong here too.
2. **Playing cards** — fanned, cascading, scattered mid-air, or held in a gloved hand.
3. **Crystal balls** on stands; **magic wands** (short tapered props, often star- or metal-tipped).
4. **Stage architecture**: velvet curtains with fringe, proscenium arches, auditorium stages, spotlights and footlights, marquee and cabinet signage.
5. **Vintage advertisements and showman typography**: lithograph poster layouts, ornate framed signs, stepped or arched display lettering spelling incantations and taglines ("abracadabra", "as strange as it seems", "it's all an act", "what you see isn't all there is").
6. **Tailored costume**: tailcoats, waistcoats, cravats, bow ties, white gloves, capes, fishnets, statement jewelry and collar brooches, dress shoes or heeled boots.
7. **Illusion tableau**: levitating assistants, sawing-in-half boxes, floating objects, impossible geometry played straight; surreal montage and repetition.
8. **Esoteric or celestial symbols — rarely**: star and moon ornaments, zodiac embroidery on a shoulder, used as decoration, never as altar.

### Materials and textures
Velvet pile and satin sheen; sequins and metallic embroidery on wool or leather tailoring; white kid gloves; brass prop hardware (box latches, wand ferrules); gilded ornate frames; checkered stage floors; lithograph paper grain and book-cloth texture; sepia photographic grain and soft vignetting; painted canvas backdrops.

### Recurring subjects
The performer (masculine-styled tailcoat or feminine assistant/showgirl coordinates borrowing Witchcore accents); the props as still life (hat, dove, cards, wand, crystal ball); the stage mid-show; backstage rehearsal and trick equipment setup; typographic incantation pieces; fashion-plate garments with celestial embroidery; surreal collage of boxes, cards and duplicated figures.

### Composition and lighting
Frame-within-frame is characteristic: curtain legs, proscenium arches or gilded frames enclosing the act. Other recurring structures: centered emblem or typographic stack on a dark field; a diagonal levitation line crossing an empty void; flat-lay grids of accessories; symmetrical frontal stage views; montage/collage of repeated trick imagery. Lighting is theatrical and single-source: a spotlight cone cutting darkness, warm footlight or marquee glow from below, sepia studio wash with heavy vignette for vintage acts; the horror variant uses flat, dreary low light and fog. Darkness is a compositional material — the void around the lit trick is the point.

## Mood & Values
Wonder, fame, trickery, mystique, illusions, flamboyance, deception, showmanship. The style values the confident lie told beautifully: secrecy ("never telling anyone your secrets"), rehearsal discipline behind the glamour, and dream-like astonishment in front of it. Its horror inflection swaps wonder for mystery and lies — uncanny dread, drearier color, surreal imagery — while keeping the same props and tailoring.

## Era & Origins
Stage magic's public tradition begins in the 1700s and matures through 19th-century theatre and early-20th-century studio photography; the poster-and-signage vocabulary is vaudeville lithography. As a named internet aesthetic it is recent (Tumblr, user thepinkseat-askthemoonbunny), defined explicitly against Wizardcore — borrowing its magic but adding modern tailoring and dramatic flair — and adjacent to Steampunk, Victorian fashion, Ouji, and a punk/goth branch inspired by contemporary street illusionists.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
1. Everything is part of an act: costume, prop, curtain, poster promise. Even a still life implies an audience about to be deceived.
2. Dark ground, bright glint: jewel-tone or black field carrying gold/silver/cyan accents; or the faded-paper light variant; or dreary horror desaturation. Pick one register and commit.
3. Tailored silhouette over robes: tailcoat, waistcoat, gloves, top hat — sartorial precision, optionally goth/punk hardware.
4. Illusion played straight: levitation, disappearance, cascading cards, impossible balance — no wink, no visible wires unless the theme is exposed secrets.
5. Showman type when text appears: arched slab serif, stepped incantations, gold leaf on black, ornate sign panels.
6. Print-and-stage texture: lithograph grain, velvet pile, sepia emulsion, gilded frame, checkered floor.

**Variation axes**
- *Subject*: performer portrait, prop still life, poster/typographic piece, full stage scene, backstage candid, surreal illusion tableau, embroidered fashion plate, horror-mystery image, accessory flat-lay.
- *Composition*: spotlight-centered portrait, proscenium frame-within-frame, typographic stack, collage/montage, diagonal levitation, symmetrical frontal stage, grid flat-lay.
- *Lighting*: single spotlight chiaroscuro, footlight/marquee warmth, sepia studio wash, faded daylight (light variant), dreary fog-lit gloom (horror variant).
- *Color*: full jewel-dark with metallic accents; near-black with one metallic; faded cream/rose vintage; desaturated horror.
- *Mood*: flamboyant wonder ↔ cool deception ↔ uncanny dread.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A card cascade over an empty checkered floor, a typographic incantation on book cloth, or a levitating figure in a void are all equally valid Magician images.

**Avoid**
- Pointed witch/wizard hats, flowing robes, staffs, grimoires, rune circles as the focal magic (wrong lineage: magician's wand is a short prop, his book a poster).
- Domestic or forest occultism: cauldrons, herb bundles, apothecary shelves, altar candles, black cats, moon worship.
- Steampunk machinery as subject: gears, goggles, boilers, clockwork engines, airships; brass here is prop hardware only.
- Ritual occultism (pentagrams, summoning); esoteric/celestial symbols stay rare and decorative.
- Saturated candy pastels, neon cyberpunk chrome, glossy modern advertising flatness, neutral corporate minimalism.
- Crowd-filled carnival midway, clown regalia, confetti maximalism — the style keeps performer-audience intimacy and secrecy.
- Untailored casual streetwear with no stage-costume logic; daylight documentary flatness outside the intentional faded-vintage register.

## Discrimination Cues
- **Vs Steampunk**: both wear Victorian tailoring, but Steampunk foregrounds machines — gears, goggles, brass instruments, oxidized copper-brown utility. Magician foregrounds instruments of *deception* (cards, wand, hat, rabbit), stage architecture (curtain, spotlight, proscenium) and poster typography; its metal glints under velvet rather than covering boilers.
- **Vs Witchcore**: Witchcore is devotional and domestic — cottage interiors, herbs, cauldrons, pointed hats, cats, moon phases as faith, warm clutter. Magician is public and theatrical — proscenium, spotlight, tuxedo, printed advertisement — and its occult admits itself fake: moons and stars appear as sequins and embroidery, and signage confesses "it's all an act."
- **Vs Wizardcore**: Wizardcore casts magic as lived power — robes, staffs, spellbooks, central celestial cosmology, wilderness or academy settings. Magician casts magic as craft: suit not robe, short wand not staff, deck not grimoire, spotlight not aura; celestial symbols are rare ornament, and the "modern twist and flair for the dramatic" means tailoring and showmanship, not sorcery.
- **Quick tell**: tailcoat + top hat + cards/rabbit/curtain/spotlight/showman type = Magician; remove the stage-and-deception framing and you have drifted into a neighboring magic aesthetic.
