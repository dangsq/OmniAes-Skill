# Early Cyber

Early Cyber is the look of the moment image-processing software first landed on ordinary designers' desks. It names the graphic culture of the mid-1980s to mid-1990s, with prototypes in the 1970s, in which harsh pixelated thermographic imagery, warped fonts and early abstract CGI — all newly possible through the first wave of desktop publishing and image manipulation tools — were fused onto record sleeves, magazine covers, poster prints and TV idents, often carrying cyberpunk themes. It is not a photographic or cinematic style but a *processing* style: the subject matter is whatever a camera or paintbox captured, and the style is what the machine then did to it. By the late 1990s its rough edges polished themselves into Y2K Futurism and Metalheart; Early Cyber is precisely the unpolished phase.

## Visual Identity

### Color: rainbow as data, never as nature
Color in Early Cyber is information, not illumination. Naturalistic skin tones and daylight balances are overwritten by false-color ramps. Four registered keyings cover the range, and a single image often mixes two of them:

| Keying | Range (hex) | Typical carrier |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal / heat-map ramp | deep indigo `#0B0B3A` → teal `#007F7F` → green `#00A86B` → yellow `#FFD400` → orange `#FF8A00` → red `#E4002B` → magenta `#FF0090` | bodies, faces, fractal swirls, globes |
| Neon-on-black | magenta `#FF00FF`, cyan `#00E5FF`, yellow `#FFFF00`, lime `#39FF14` over near-black `#0A0A0A` | posterized portraits, rave sleeves |
| Pastel airbrush | lavender `#C8B6E2`, pink `#F7C6D9`, mint `#A8E9E0`, peach `#F6D8AE` | soft-glow portraits, illustrated scenes |
| Flat DTP blocks | orange `#F26522`, pink `#EC008C`, teal `#00A79D`, yellow `#FFC20E`, black `#231F20`, paper cream `#F5F2EC` | bands, arcs, triangles, type panels |

Duotone and tritone reductions are legitimate: a green-on-cream (`#1E5636` on `#EFEAD8`) or blue-on-black posterized portrait still reads Early Cyber if the raster damage is present. What is constant is that hue is assigned by algorithm, not by light.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the style documents them
1. **Pixelization** — heavy pixelation, pixel-mosaic portraits built from colored rectangular chips, pixel-sort debris streaking off a face, bitmap type.
2. **Thermography / altered color** — false-color heat maps of human figures and objects; the body as temperature data.
3. **Fractals and abstract CGI** — Mandelbrot-like swirls, particle-cloud stipple fields, low-poly abstract 3D; deliberately non-photoreal, more abstract than the glossy 3D of Silicon Dreams.
4. **Visual distortion** — squash and stretch, fisheye warping, water-drop tool bulges, kaleidoscope mirroring of faces and shards.
5. **Dot matrix and halftone stipple** — images dissolved into ordered dot fields, diagonal streaks of particles, dithered gradients.
6. **Posterization and solarization** — flat banded tonal steps, inverted-edge glow around silhouettes.
7. **Warped and bitmap typography** — stretched or condensed serifs, italic display serifs, pixel/block lettering, letterspaced capitals, vertical type columns, tiny technical caption blocks treated as texture.
8. **Flat geometric DTP overlays** — solid arcs, bars, triangles, dashes, zigzag edges and rules laid vector-flat over raster chaos; the Emigre / April Greiman desktop-publishing layer.
9. **Head-up displays** — wireframe grids, crosshair reticles, inset screen windows, schematic readouts framing a face or helmet.
10. **Early greenscreen and compositing seams** — visible matte edges, mismatched grain between layers, collage shards that don't quite merge.
11. **Glow effects** — airbrush halos, luminous bloom around raster forms on dark grounds.

### Materials and textures
The medium is print on card and sleeve: compact-disc and vinyl packaging, magazine covers, poster stock. Texture comes from process, not surface: dither patterns, scanlines, pixel chips, halftone dots, soft airbrush gradients sitting hard against razor-flat vector color, slight misregistration and scan-era artifacting. Contrast of resolutions is the point — a crisp solid pink arc crossing a molten, dithered heat-map field.

### Recurring subjects
Human faces and bodies are the dominant specimens: musicians, authors, models, helmeted children, even classical composers run through the posterizer. Beyond the portrait: planet globes as dot-matrix emblems, machines and helmets, wireframe meshes, fractal medallions, and blocks of text themselves used as subject matter. Any of these may appear as a small inset "window" inside a larger layout.

### Composition and lighting
Layouts are panelled collages: vertical side bars, horizontal bottom bands, inset rectangles acting as screens, a centered emblematic raster field, asymmetric desktop-publishing balance with type doing architectural work. Minimal variants exist — a flat pastel ground carrying only a thin strip of tiny thermal insets and one bold sans word — so maximalism is typical but not mandatory. Lighting is machine-emitted: glow halos and luminous raster on dark grounds, unmodulated flat color on the vector layer, occasional harsh flash-lit photographic insets. There is no consistent natural light source; light is a property of the processing.

## Mood & Values

Early Cyber expresses the machine-sublime: earnest fascination with what the new tools do, displayed by leaving the process visible. It holds optimism and dread at once — technophilic wonder at rainbows extracted from a face, alongside cyberpunk paranoia about the same machinery. Underground rave singles and corporate boardroom annual reports share its pipeline without irony; the style values novelty, demonstration, and the conviction that seeing humanity processed is seeing humanity truer.

## Era & Origins

Prototypes appear in the 1970s; the style is predominant from the mid-1980s to mid-1990s. It emerges from the first wave of desktop publishing and paintbox software (Quantel Paintbox, Amiga graphics, Macintosh-based design), early CGI demo reels such as *The Mind's Eye* (1990), and the typographic experiments of Emigre magazine and April Greiman. The term itself is a retrospective coinage by Evan Collins. It overlaps 90s Cool, Cyberdelia, Silicon Dreams, Factory Pomo and Corporate Gen-X Cyber, and dissolves into Y2K Futurism and Metalheart by the late 1990s.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. Begin with an ordinary source subject — portrait, body, machine, globe, abstract field — then run it through a simulated early raster pipeline: posterize or solarize, remap to a false-color thermal or neon ramp, pixelate into chips or dot matrix, streak pixel-sort debris.
2. Overlay a flat desktop-publishing layer: solid bands, arcs, triangles, dashes, zigzags, rules; warped serif or bitmap display type; small caption blocks; type treated as shape.
3. Let color behave as data: assign hue by ramp and band, never by light direction.
4. Keep resolution conflict visible: crisp vector flats against dithered, molten raster.
5. Emit light from the process: glow halos, luminous fields on dark grounds, airbrush gradients.
6. Compose in panels and insets rather than a single unified scene; allow asymmetry and collage seams.
7. Respect print medium: sleeve or poster framing, compact-disc-era print grain, slight registration drift.

Variation axes: **subject** may be any person, creature, machine, planet or pure fractal field; **composition** may range from maximalist multi-panel collage to a near-empty flat ground with one strip of insets; **lighting** from glow-on-black to even, shadowless daylight flats; **color handling** from full rainbow heat-map through neon-on-black to duotone reduction; **mood** from playful technophilia to cold dystopia. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

Avoid: smooth modern PBR 3D renders and glassmorphism; clean minimal flat design with no raster damage; synthwave chrome type over sunset horizon laser grids; rain-slick neon-street noir scenes; hand-drawn organic illustration; unprocessed naturalistic photography; analog film grain as the only texture; steampunk brass; polished late-90s chrome blobs (that is Y2K Futurism, the successor); seamless compositing that hides its seams.

## Discrimination Cues

- **90s Cool**: shares era and flat geometry, but 90s Cool is cleaner, drier, more minimal and attitude-led; Early Cyber always carries visible raster distortion, false color and pixel damage.
- **Cassette Futurism**: analog hardware realism — beige plastic, phosphor CRT green/amber, chunky buttons, wireframe vectors; Early Cyber is image-processing spectacle in rainbow false color, not diegetic machine realism.
- **Cyberdelia**: psychedelic rave neon with fluid airbrushed gradients and smooth tripiness; Early Cyber is harsher, pixelated, typographic and collaged, with dot-matrix grit Cyberdelia lacks.
- **Cyberpunk**: a narrative world of neon noir streets, chrome and leather; Early Cyber is a graphic-design language that may cite cyberpunk themes but expresses them through posterized raster and DTP layout, not cinematic street scenes.
- **Laser Grid**: defined by perspective horizon grids, chrome lettering and striped sunsets; Early Cyber has no obligatory grid horizon — its space is collage panels, raster fields and flat vector overlays.
