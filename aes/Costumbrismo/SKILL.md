# Costumbrismo

Costumbrismo is the nineteenth-century Spanish movement of "customs": the *cuadro de costumbres* in paint, the *artículo de costumbres* in the newspaper, the type portrait in the collective album (*Los españoles pintados por sí mismos*, 1843–44). Its subject is never the hero and never the soul; it is the **practice** — how a region drinks, courts, trades, rides, mourns, dances and prays — recorded with an observer's eye at the moment industrial modernity threatens to erase it. It sits deliberately between Realism and Romanticism: it takes Realism's commitment to observable reality and borrows Romanticism's picturesque warmth, but refuses both the realist's systemic critique and the romantic's sublime escape. The result is a picture that reads like a well-reported paragraph: minimal action, limited dialogue, dense description.

Two legitimate handwritings coexist. The **School of Madrid** brushes broadly, fixes on specific persons, and allows a critical, even vulgar, edge when portraying the lower classes. The **School of Seville** finishes delicately, turns individuals into archetypes of social types, and leans toward romance and folklore because its patrons — often foreign travellers — wanted Andalusia as the exotic essence of Spain. A third, parallel mode is the **press illustration**: wood-engraved and etched line work in ink on paper, black top hats and shop interiors rendered in cross-hatch. All three are costumbrismo.

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule: earth first, accent small

The infobox key colour is simply "natural colours," and the corpus obeys: a warm earth ground dominates every canvas, with saturated colour confined to costume details and props. Treat the palette as a budget, not a list.

- **Ground, wall and architecture (the majority of the surface):** whitewash cream and paper warm-white (`#E9DFC8`, `#F2EBDA`), sunlit ochre and sienna (`#C8A165`, `#B98A4E`), shadow umber and tobacco brown (`#7A5230`, `#3A2E22`).
- **Vegetation and shade:** olive, vine-leaf and dusty field greens (`#5F6B3C`, `#7C8A4E`), never emerald.
- **Sky and distance:** pale dusty blue to silver-grey haze (`#9FB4C4`, `#C4CDD2`), horizon warmed to bone (`#E3D9C6`); bullring and dust scenes may run almost entirely grey-blue and tan.
- **Social black:** cloak, mantilla, clerical robe, confraternity hood, bourgeois top hat (`#201A15` to `#2B2622`) — a structural dark, often in silhouetted masses.
- **Accent notes (small area, high meaning):** majo sash and banner red (`#9E3B2B`, `#C2452F`), saffron and cadmium yellow of skirts, faroles and straw (`#D9A441`, `#E4C05C`), occasional shawl blue or emerald thread in embroidery. Accents should occupy a minor fraction of the surface; if red dominates, the budget is broken.
- **Monochrome branch:** ink black and sepia on laid-paper white (`#2B2622` on `#EAE3D2`) for engravings and book illustration.

No cool saturated primaries, no violet shadows, no neon, no metallic gloss. Colour saturation is a signal of *festive or folkloric* moment, not of light.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Everyday life scenes and social archetypes (*tipos*):** the water-seller, the muleteer, the cigarette-worker, the bookseller, the pulquería drinker, the returned *indiano*, the mountain *montañés*. Figures are legible as members of a class or trade before they are individuals.
2. **Regional clothing as document:** fringed silk shawls, lace mantillas, cropped bolero jackets, wide-brimmed hats, sashes, gaiters, kerchiefs; dress is drawn with ethnographic care and is often the true subject of the picture.
3. **Traditional architecture and street furniture:** whitewashed tavern fronts, vine pergolas over doorways, wrought-iron window grilles, brick and stone vaults of factory or bodega halls, plaza arcades, tile roofs, the river tower on the skyline.
4. **Social gatherings:** tavern and pulquería tertulias around a coarse table with jug, plate and glass; market bargains; village dances and fairs; the promenade with cloaked flirtation.
5. **Religious imagery and processions:** Holy Week in Seville — black-robed confraternities, tall banners and candles threading a narrow street; parish interiors; devotional objects in domestic rooms.
6. **Marginal and romantic figures of the road:** majos, horsemen, bandits, smugglers, street children, beggars, Roma camps; mounted arrivals and departures at inn doors.
7. **Festive spectacles:** plaza and bullring events — picadors, capes, fallen horses, crowds massed along barriers or watched from arcades and balconies (strongly present in the painted record; the text files these under festive celebrations).
8. **Domestic still life and local animals,** chiefly in the Hispanic American branch: fruit, flowers, pottery, a cat among birds on a tabletop — the bodegón absorbed into customs painting.
9. **Documentary extensions:** wood-engraved press illustration of the same subjects, and, later, photography used to record everyday life (the textual record asserts the photographic extension; surviving visual evidence here is painted and engraved).

### Materials, textures, handling

Oil on panel or canvas at cabinet-to-medium scale is the default body of the work: paint applied either in **broad, patchy, loaded strokes** that still keep contour (Madrid) or in **smooth, enamel-like blending** with porcelain finish on faces and textiles (Seville). Surfaces that must read tactilely: coarse wool of cloaks, silk and fringe of shawls, lace of mantillas, dust on plaza ground, rough whitewash, glazed pottery, worn wood of tables and doors, paper and hatching in the engraved branch. Brushwork may loosen in skies and distance — dusty grey scumbles over a bullring — but never dissolves the anecdote in the foreground.

### Composition and staging

- **The tableau anecdote:** a small group arranged like a stage scene at middle distance, full bodies visible, one legible exchange — a toast, a bargain, a courtship glance, a mounted greeting, a sung verse. Minimal action is doctrinal.
- **Architecture as frame and witness:** doorways, tavern gates, pergolas, arcades and vaulted halls enclose the group; the building states the region before any face does.
- **Crowd recursion for festivals:** processions and fairs recede in ranks — banners, hoods, spectators lining walls — with the eye led down a street or around a plaza; elevated or through-arch viewpoints are acceptable for plaza and ring spectacles.
- **Horizontal cabinet formats** suit the tableau; vertical formats suit processions, type portraits and interior vignettes.
- **Depth by haze:** a clear middle ground carrying the anecdote, a softened town or hill band, a high pale sky. Foreground props — a jug, a basket, a fallen cape, a cat — anchor the eye.

### Light

Daylight reported, not dramatized: high southern sun with soft-edged shadows on ochre walls; silver-grey overcast and dust-light for ring and field scenes; interiors lit dim and warm with one brighter source at a window or door picking out faces, glassware and linen. There is no tenebrist theatre, no storm-light, no flickering plein-air sparkle. Light exists to make costume and custom readable.

## Mood & Values

The movement's stated values — tradition, community, realism, social observation, regionalism, longing — are exactly its mood. A costumbrista picture is **affectionate inventory**: it preserves rather than judges, or judges only with a satirical smile (Madrid's sharper note aside). Beneath the genre's cheer runs **nostalgia and melancholic longing** — Galician *saudade* and *morriña*, Cantabria's disappearing rural world, the anxiety that the railway will end everything worth painting. Community outranks the individual; the pleasure of the picture is recognition, the viewer nodding at a type they know. Satire, when present, is social comedy, not indictment.

## Era & Origins

Nineteenth-century Spain, with peak development from the 1830s into the mid-twentieth century. It arose in Madrid's press culture — Larra, Mesonero Romanos — as a reaction against Romantic idealism's excesses and against the erasures of the Industrial Revolution, fed by English and French sketch-journalism models and by older Spanish roots: the picaresque (*Lazarillo de Tormes*), Cervantes, and the genre eye of the Golden Age (Velázquez, Murillo). It rapidly regionalized — Andalusian folklore, Cantabrian mountains, Asturian village and *indiano* stories, Galician linguistic revival, Catalan bourgeois manners, Basque idealism — and crossed the Atlantic, where nineteenth-century Mexican painting of pulquerías, tabletop still lifes and local types echoed the older *casta* picture's cataloguing of social hierarchy. Goya's tapestry cartoons of majos and promenades stand behind it as an eighteenth-century ancestor.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Begin from a **custom**, not a character: choose one observable social practice — a trade, a courtship, a tavern game, a market haggle, a mounted departure, a procession, a factory shift, a tabletop meal — and let the picture describe it.
2. Make **costume the document**: region-specific dress, headwear and footwear rendered with care; props of the trade or rite in hand.
3. Spend the palette by the budget above: earth neutrals carry the surface; red, saffron and banner colour appear only as costume and prop accents.
4. Stage the anecdote with **minimal action**: one exchange, legible gestures, full bodies at middle distance; faces typified.
5. Let **architecture and ground state the region**: whitewash, pergola, vault, arcade, plaza dust, river tower.
6. Choose a **handwriting consciously**: broad satirical brush (Madrid), smooth romanticized finish (Seville), or ink line on paper (press engraving). Any of the three is correct; mixing them into a uniformless smear is not.
7. Light as **weather report**: clear southern day, dusty overcast, or dim warm interior with one source. Never sublime, never flickering.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across the ranked motif list, from a two-figure tavern exchange to a factory vault full of workers to a street procession to a tabletop with cat and fruit. Geography may be Madrid, Andalusia, the northern coast, Galicia, Catalonia, the Basque Country, or Hispanic America (Mexican pulquería and still life included). Crowd scale may run from two figures to festival masses. Viewpoint may be eye-level tableau, elevated plaza view, or framed through arches. Handling may be broad, smooth, or engraved. Mood may be celebratory, satirical, critical, or elegiac. Weather may be sun, dust-grey overcast, or interior gloom. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a cigarette factory, a bookseller's counter, a Galician harvest or a Mexican tavern are equally valid if they obey the palette budget, the tableau anecdote, the costume-as-document and the observational mood.

**Avoid.**
- Anachronism: automobiles, electric light, modern dress, contemporary signage; the world pictured is pre-industrial or traditionally dressed even in later decades.
- Heroic history painting, battle glorification, mythological machinery and allegorical personifications (a tavern's parody of myth aside, gods do not descend here).
- Romantic sublime weather: storms, shipwrecks, lightning-lit ruins, transcendent sunsets.
- Impressionist broken colour, comma strokes, violet shadows, dissolved contour; the anecdote must stay legible.
- Realist monumentalization of labour with psychological close-ups and systemic indictment; costumbrismo observes, it does not prosecute.
- Photographic hyperreal gloss, studio lighting, digital smoothness alien to oil or engraving.
- Saturated non-natural palette, neon, cool primaries dominating the surface.
- Empty pure landscape or pure portrait: without a human custom being performed or a social type being catalogued, the picture has left the genre (rural landscape with small labouring figures remains admissible).
- Modernist flatness, abstraction, or formal experiment that overtakes the description.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | How costumbrismo differs |
|---|---|
| **Majismo** | Majismo is the eighteenth-century culture of the majo/maja — dandyish lower-class swagger, flirtation, bright tapestry-cartoon attitude, often a single costumed figure or a teasing pair. Costumbrismo absorbs majos as *one entry* in a wider social inventory; if the picture is only costume attitude and coquetry without a documented practice, setting or anecdote, it is majismo. |
| **Realism** | Same observable world, different intent: Realism monumentalizes labour, grants individuals psychological depth and presses social critique; costumbrismo stays anecdotal, picturesque and typified, describing "without deep analysis," at cabinet scale with a lighter or gently satirical tone. Madrid-school satire is the overlap zone; scale and typification separate them. |
| **Romanticism** | Romanticism wants the sublime, the exotic hero, destiny and storm. Costumbrismo borrows only the picturesque: keep the weather ordinary, the action minimal, the detail descriptive. No protagonist, no transcendence. |
| **Impressionism** | Impressionism makes light the subject and dissolves form into broken colour and modern leisure. Costumbrismo keeps contour, costume accuracy, earth palette and narrative legibility; loose brushwork serves description, never sensation. |
| **Renovador Movement** | Evidence is thin — the relation is recorded only as a neighbouring aesthetic. Heuristic: renovador impulses renew *form* (brighter palette, modernist synthesis, compositional experiment) looking forward, while costumbrismo conserves descriptive finish and anecdote looking backward. When formal invention overtakes the document, the picture has crossed out of costumbrismo. |
| **Spanish Golden Age genre** | Ancestor, not twin: Velázquez's and Murillo's street types and bodegones share the DNA but carry Baroque tenebrism and moralizing vanity symbolism; costumbrismo is nineteenth-century, press-fed, regionalist, and lit by reported daylight. |

Where the record is thin, say so: the photographic branch is asserted by the text but not exemplified in the surviving visual set, and the Renovador contrast rests on general period logic rather than documented comparison. Everything else above — palette budget, tableau anecdote, costume-as-document, the two school handwritings and the engraved press mode — is directly evidenced by the movement's own writings and pictures.
