# New Beat

New Beat was a short-lived but enormously influential Belgian electronic genre and subculture that flourished between 1987 and 1989, centered on clubs like Boccaccio in Ghent and the Ancienne Belgique in Antwerp. Its sound was an accident — DJs playing 45 RPM EBM and New Wave records at 33 RPM — and its look was a parallel accident of appropriations: acid house's smiley turned into a weapon of mockery, gravestone porcelain portraits turned into jewelry, church rosaries and BDSM leather turned into a unisex uniform. The fashion side was partly codified by Idriz Jossa's "New Beat Fashion" collection at the Antwerp Fashion Academy, which gives the style a designed, editorial spine beneath its club chaos. Visually the style lives in three registers: flat, bold graphic design (record sleeves, flyers, badge sheets), high-contrast black-and-white fashion photography and video stills of the uniform, and analog video frames with washed color casts. What holds all three together is a duality — menacing, macabre, industrial darkness delivered with deliberate camp, irony and hedonistic joy.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The palette is a national statement as much as a taste: black, yellow and red, the colors of the Belgian flag, used to ground a homegrown movement against American and British electronic scenes. Black is always the ground and the majority; yellow and red arrive as accents — a smiley, a flag block, a lip, a banner — never as fields of equal weight.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how strongly the source documents them)

1. **The yellow smiley face** — the most prevalent symbol. Shared with Acid House, but in Belgium used subversively: pasted over the faces of politicians, military men and public figures to erase authority, or worn as an ironically exaggerated badge on tees, sweats and pins.
2. **Overwhelming black clothing**, frequently in lingerie fabrics, for both men and women — the base of the uniform.
3. **The Bomma badge** — an oval ceramic pin resembling a gravestone portrait of a grandmother; macabre nostalgia worn singly on the chest or repeated in rows down sleeves and across jackets.
4. **Cycling shorts and knee socks** with heavy black shoes, built for the frenetic "bocca-dans-mania" dance.
5. **BDSM-inspired leather gear** — jackets, harnesses, straps, gloss leather.
6. **Religious iconography** — layered rosaries, crosses, chains; crucifix pendants photographed as still-life objects.
7. **Bowler hats**, often with veils or netting over the face.
8. **Pale-faced makeup with dark-rimmed eyes and bright red lipstick**, on any gender.
9. **The Belgian tricolor** as graphic blocks, flag overlays on portraits, and the black/yellow/red scheme itself.
10. **Car logos** (Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz) worn as emblems; the scene's own logo even mimics the VW roundel with a stylized W.
11. **BOY London branding** on garments (documented in text; rarely legible in surviving imagery).
12. **Pop-Art collage treatment of authority** — halftone portraits of politicians, soldiers and statesmen, defaced with smileys, flag shapes or sticker text, Warhol-style serialization of serious faces as consumable product.

### Materials and textures

Ceramic and porcelain (the bomma badges), cheap printed paper and card (flyers, sticker sheets, sleeve collages with visible halftone dot screens), leather and rubber, lingerie satin and lace, cotton jersey tees, rosary beads and metal chain, vinyl records and merch stalls of hanging white longsleeves. Video-era materiality matters: composite-video softness, scanlines, color bleed, flash-burned whites in dim rooms, screen-print misregistration.

### Recurring subjects

Androgynous clubbers and band members in the full uniform (Nikkie Van Lierop of 101 is the archetype: bowler hat, veil, pale face, sleeves studded with oval portraits); avant-garde runway-style fashion looks (cone hats, badge-covered coats, skirted silhouettes on men); defaced political and military portraits; badge and sticker sheets; merch walls and flat-lay trays of accessories (badges, car logos, shoes, rosaries); club and stage performances; look-alike political caricature (the bandaged, pipe-smoking Prime Minister double of "Qui...?"); classical statues and museum props used as deadpan video sets.

### Composition and lighting

Graphic work is flat and frontal: collage beds of cut halftone photos, bold grotesque sans-serif type, diagonal banners, clustered circular badges, flag-colored rectangles, sticker-sheet grids. Photography is high-contrast black-and-white, studio or hard direct flash, with centered, static, theatrical poses — deadpan rather than dancing. Video stills carry analog casts (greenish, magenta, violet) and dim, gel-lit stages or dimly lit exhibition rooms lit by camera flash. Repetition is a compositional device: badges in rows, smileys in clusters, portraits in series.

## Mood & Values

Hedonism, experimentation, underground club belonging, androgyny and dark humor. The defining emotional register is a duality: menacing, industrial, almost funereal visuals (gravestone portraits, rosaries, leather, veils) delivered with camp exaggeration and a wink. Authority is mocked, not fought — a smiley over a general's face reduces power to absurdity. There is nostalgia turned morbid (the "bomma" grandmother portraits), consumer irony (car logos as jewelry), and a sense of a generation dancing in the dark at a deliberately slowed, hypnotic tempo. Nothing is sincere except the pleasure; everything solemn is quoted, inflated and deflated.

## Era & Origins

Belgium, 1987–1989. Rooted in the country's dark EBM scene (Front 242, A Split-Second) and the slower-tempo Popcorn soul subculture; crystallized when DJs such as Marc Grouls played 45 RPM EBM singles at 33 RPM, producing the dark, heavy, mid-tempo signature. Exploded through Boccaccio and a frantic Monday-recorded, weekend-pressed production cycle; declined by 1990 under oversaturation of formulaic "Nougat Beat" imitations, then mutated into the faster styles that became hardcore techno and gabber. Legacy: European precursor to rave; documented in *The Sound of Belgium* (2012); Praga Khan's lineage reaching the *Mortal Kombat* "Techno Syndrome" theme. The visual style is therefore strictly late-80s analog: print collages, VHS, flash photography, no digital gloss.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

1. **Black first.** Any composition begins from a black or halftone-greyscale mass; yellow and red puncture it.
2. **The smiley is a weapon or an ironic badge.** It defaces a serious face, or sits on black clothing as exaggerated camp. It is never wholesome décor.
3. **Macabre domesticity.** Oval ceramic ancestor portraits, rosaries, crosses, veils and bowler hats bring funeral-and-parlor imagery into clubwear.
4. **Androgynous silhouette.** Cycling shorts, knee socks, heavy shoes, lingerie fabrics and leather on any gender; pale face, dark-rimmed eyes, red mouth.
5. **Analog materiality.** Halftone dots, paper grain, screen-print flatness, VHS cast and scanlines, flash burn. Choose one register: flat print collage, high-contrast B&W photo, or degraded color video frame.
6. **Deadpan performance.** Poses are static, theatrical, hypnotic or bored — menace plus wink, never euphoric abandon.
7. **Deface authority with Pop-Art methods.** Halftone portraits of politicians, soldiers or statesmen, overlaid with smileys, flag blocks or sticker type; serialization of the same face.
8. **Cluster and repeat.** Badges in rows down a sleeve, stickers in sheets, tees in walls; repetition is ornament.

Variation axes (what may change): subject matter (fashion portrait, sleeve collage, badge sheet, merch flat-lay, defaced portrait, club or stage still, museum-prop video tableau); composition (flat graphic bed, centered studio portrait, candid flash frame, symmetrical emblem placement); lighting (hard flash, studio key, dim gel-lit stage, flat scanner light on paper); color handling (pure tricolor graphic, pure B&W halftone, magenta/violet duotone, greenish VHS cast); mood balance (more funereal vs. more camp). A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules — black ground, tricolor accent, ironic smiley, macabre accessory, analog surface, deadpan tone — not any reference image.

Avoid:

- Sincere, celebratory smiley usage with no ironic or defacing context; rainbow or recolored smileys.
- Neon psychedelia: tie-dye, fractals, liquid chrome type, spiral 303 graphics, gradient mesh, glow trails.
- Euphoric daylight rave imagery: fields, confetti, hands-in-the-air crowds, glossy festival LED walls.
- Sportswear hardcore codes: tracksuits, shaved heads, trainer fetishism, aggressive hardcore gestures.
- Earnest militarism or industrial severity without camp (serious EBM staging).
- Modern digital sharpness, clean vector minimalism, contemporary flat-UI design, smartphones or post-1990s products.
- Pastel or earth-tone palettes; black must dominate.
- Wholesome vintage nostalgia: the macabre vintage quote must be twisted, not cozy.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared ground | New Beat tell |
|---|---|---|
| Acid House (UK) | The yellow smiley; club context | New Beat's smiley defaces authority or sits on funereal black; adds bomma badges, rosaries, bowler hats, tricolor national coding, halftone political collage; slower, darker, camp-macabre rather than euphoric |
| Electronic Body Music | Black leather, industrial darkness, Belgian origin | EBM is sincere, muscular, militaristic; New Beat slows it down and adds irony, androgyny, macabre jewelry and the smiley — menace with a wink versus menace alone |
| Gabber / Hardcore | Descendant lineage, underground club | Gabber is post-1991, Dutch, faster and aggressive: shaved heads, sportswear, trainer culture, hard flash of hardcore parties; no androgyny, no ceramic portraits, no bowler hats, no tricolor graphics |
| Acid Design (rave graphics) | Smileys, flyers, print ephemera | Acid design runs liquid chrome type, fractals, tie-dye neons on black; New Beat graphics are flat Pop-Art collage, halftone photo cutouts, grotesque type, badge clusters and flag blocks |
