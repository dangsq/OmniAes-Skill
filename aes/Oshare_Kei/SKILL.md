# Oshare Kei (オサレ系, "Osare Kei")

## Overview

Oshare Kei ("fashionable style") is the subgenre of Visual Kei that refused to mourn. Where most Visual Kei lines descended into gothic gloom, classical romance or erotic-grotesque theater, Oshare Kei — named and launched by the band BAROQUE in 2001 — dressed its rock musicians in bright, pop-saturated costumes borrowed from Harajuku street fashion and Decora maximalism. The wiki text is explicit about the formula: bright cheerful colors and pastels, massive amounts of jewelry, cartoon-character and animal bags slung over the shoulder, hairclips in dizzying numbers, and lighter makeup than the parent scene. The music matches the clothes: hard rock and punk played upbeat, touched by jazz, techno and dance, with lyrics lighter and happier. Bands such as An Cafe, SuG, LM.C, Megamasso, HERO, THE KIDDIE and v(NEU) carried the style through its mid-2000s peak.

Practically, this means Oshare Kei images are almost always *music-scene images*: band promotional portraits, album-art graphics, stage and street shots of costumed musicians. The aesthetic is a costume logic — a punk skeleton wearing pop flesh — rather than a casual wardrobe.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule, not a swatch: **one neutral anchor (pure black or paper white) carrying at least three simultaneous candy-bright hues**, plus optional pastel washes. Saturation is pushed high; values stay crisp; muddy earth tones are structurally absent. The infobox keys the palette to pink, blue, green, yellow and black, and the image corpus confirms violet/purple and tangerine as frequent honorary members (hair dye and satin jackets), with mint and baby pink appearing in pastel-mode shots.

Acceptable handling modes: candy-on-black (flash-lit satin against dark), candy-on-white (high-key studio), pastel-dominant with one black anchor item, and near-monochrome black-and-white broken by a single loud pop (a pink mohawk, a red bandana). What never varies: high chroma and the presence of the anchor.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Bright cheerful color and pastels** — the defining act; every outfit commits to at least one saturated candy hue.
2. **Accessory maximalism** — "massive amounts of jewelry," cartoon-character and animal bags slung on, "tons of hairclips that make you go dizzy." Countable excess is canonical, not decorative.
3. **Punk hardware** — biker chains, cuffs, studded bracelets, buckles and straps, handcuffs, goggles; the rock skeleton underneath the pop.
4. **Prints** — animal print and polka dots per the text; the corpus extends the print-clash vocabulary to plaid, bold stripes, star fields, halftone dots and skeleton-bone prints. Clashing prints intentionally is in-genre.
5. **Leg- and armwear layering** — arm warmers, rags tied at the wrist, ripped jeans with bright leggings or socks underneath, knee-high socks either striped or finished with bows.
6. **The face** — dramatic black eyeliner and fake eyelashes, brightly colored contact lenses, but overall *lighter* makeup than other Visual Kei: clean skin, no corpsepaint, smiles and smirks permitted.
7. **Hair as color block** — teased, spiked, layered and heavily styled hair in unnatural shades (pink, orange, violet, teal, platinum streaks over black); uneven two-tone dye is common.

### Materials and textures

Gloss dominates: satin and sateen jackets with a hard sheen, sequined and glittered shirts, patent leather boots and belts, polished metal chains and studs, plastic clips and charms. Against these, matte cotton plaid and striped knits, denim distress, occasional feather, fur-trim or chiffon layering in the more theatrical (circus-costume) end of the corpus. Surfaces read new and costumed, never worn-out or grungy.

### Recurring subjects

Band group portraits of three to six members are the corpus norm: promotional line-ups, seated cluster shots, album-cover graphics. Also in range: solo musician portraits, live-stage shots under colored light, and street or studio snaps of fans wearing the fashion. Subjects are young, androgynous-presenting performers posing deliberately for camera.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

(Note: the wiki text documents fashion and music, not photography; these cues are distilled from the promotional image corpus.) Group shots spread horizontally across the frame with staggered heights — some seated, some standing, one crouching — so every costume reads. Poses are direct-to-camera and playful: pointing fingers, peace signs, tilted heads, hands in pockets. Backdrops fall into three families: seamless white high-key voids; black voids punctuated by a single prop (a white baroque sofa, hanging bead or chain curtains); and saturated graphic pop environments — halftone dots, zigzags, radiating rays, doodled stars and hearts, geometric diamonds, band logotypes. Lighting is flat, even studio flash or slightly overexposed outdoor daylight; contrast is crisp, saturation pushed, finish glossy. Occasional watercolor-fade or collage treatments appear as album-art variants.

## Mood & Values

Cheerfulness as defiance. Oshare Kei values being *oshare* — stylish, put-together, visibly effortful — and treats joy, kawaii culture and pop accessibility as compatible with rock credibility. The mood is upbeat, mischievous, self-celebratory: costumes that say the show is fun before a note is played. There is pride in excess (more clips, more chains, more color) and in androgynous prettiness: eyeliner and lashes worn openly by male performers. Underneath the candy is punk posture — the chains and studs guarantee the smile reads as rebellion, not innocence.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in Japan in 2001, credited to BAROQUE, flourishing through the mid-to-late 2000s alongside An Cafe, SuG, LM.C and peers. It reacts against two things at once: the dark, gothic and ero-guro tendencies of mainstream Visual Kei, and the idea that rock seriousness requires somber dress. It borrows openly from contemporaneous Harajuku street styles (Decora, Fairy Kei) while remaining a stage-costume tradition tied to bands and live houses.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Anchor plus candy: build every frame on black or white, then load at least three saturated candy hues; never let earth tones lead.
2. Punk skeleton, pop flesh: each outfit keeps a rock base (skinny or ripped jeans, boots, chains, studs) topped with kawaii-pop layers (prints, bows, character bags, pastel pieces).
3. Accessory abundance: pile bracelets, clips, chains and straps until counting fails; restraint breaks the style.
4. Print clash: combine polka dots, plaid, stripes, animal or skeleton prints deliberately within one costume.
5. Face rule: heavy black eyeliner and colored contacts over clean, lightly made-up skin; expressions may smile or smirk.
6. Hair as pigment: teased, spiked, unevenly dyed hair in unnatural colors functions as part of the palette.
7. Pop environment: prefer high-key white, prop-punctured black, or printed graphic backdrops (dots, rays, zigzags, doodles) with even flash lighting and glossy grading.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may range across band group promos, solo portraits, live stages, fan street snaps, and graphic album-art collages. Composition may be a straight line-up, a staggered cluster, a seated tableau, or a flat graphic layout with logotype space. Lighting may be high-key studio flash, overexposed daylight, or flash-on-black; colored stage light is acceptable for live scenes. Color handling may run candy-on-black, candy-on-white, pastel-dominant, or monochrome-plus-one-pop. Mood may slide from bubbly and clownish to cool pop-sultry, so long as it never turns funereal. Theoretical subjects outside music (a costumed café staff, a fashion editorial) are acceptable if the costume rules hold. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.**
- Muddy or earth-tone palettes (beige, olive, rust, browns) as the dominant scheme.
- Gothic/angura gloom: corpsepaint, blood, ero-guro props, funereal expressions, candlelit chiaroscuro.
- Full grayscale with no candy pop anywhere in frame.
- Elegant classical Lolita or aristocratic Victorian silhouettes as the sole costume language.
- Minimalist, normcore or single-garment outfits lacking prints, hardware and layered accessories.
- Decora-level accessory overload that erases the rock base (no chains, studs, jeans or eyeliner left).
- Casual Western Scene snapshot realism: plain band tees and jeans without costumed layering.
- Desaturated matte film looks, grunge decay, dirty textures, worn-out fabrics.
- Formal symmetrical portraiture, Rembrandt lighting, naturalistic golden-hour warmth.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Decora:** Decora is a street fashion of plastic-accessory maximalism with no music-scene context and no punk hardware; faces often disappear under clips. Oshare Kei keeps the rock skeleton — chains, studs, skinny jeans, dramatic eyeliner — and appears as band costume in promo framing.
- **Vs Fairy Kei:** Fairy Kei is soft 1980s-retro pastel streetwear with no black anchor, no studs or chains and no musicians. Oshare Kei always pairs its pastels with black, hardware and eyeliner.
- **Vs Harajuku Fashion (general):** Harajuku styles are civilian street fashion photographed candidly or in lookbooks. Oshare Kei is performer costume: group band line-ups, stage-ready styling, promotional graphics.
- **Vs Scene:** Western Scene shares skinny jeans and neon hair streaks but is casual, snapshot-driven, Myspace-era youth wear with minimal accessory layering and no kawaii print-clash or character bags. Oshare Kei is deliberately costumed, accessory-dense and group-promo oriented.
- **Vs Visual Kei (default/dark subgenres):** Mainstream Visual Kei leans gothic, classical, metallic or erotic-grotesque with heavier makeup and somber palettes. The quick test: if the image reads *fun* — candy colors, smiles, pop graphics, light makeup over one black anchor — it is Oshare Kei; if it reads *funereal or aristocratic*, it is another Visual Kei branch.
