# Vectorheart

Vectorheart is the look of the future as a printed graphic system: sleek vector geometry, industrial-diagonal structure and experimental type, assembled with the discipline of Swiss modernism and the smirk of a rave flyer that knows advertising is a joke. It emerged from mid-late-1990s design studios — The Designers Republic and Bionic Systems above all — and saturated the visual culture of electronica sleeves, anti-gravity racing games and rhythm-game interfaces through the 2000s. Because the Wipeout series carried it so loudly, the style is sometimes called Wipeout Design; because it shares Y2K Futurism's obsession with a clean tomorrow, it is often filed as a Y2K subgenre. Its defining tension is "maximalist-minimalism": vast empty flat fields coexisting with obsessive clusters of microtype, barcodes, icons and trademark glyphs.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)
Work in flat, unmodulated color at high contrast. The usable range runs from neutral grounds — paper white and off-white, gunmetal and slate gray, ink black — to saturated signal accents: signal red, traffic orange, warning yellow, hot magenta, racing/kelly green, and slate teal or petrol blue. A typical scheme pairs one saturated signal with one neutral (red on white, yellow on black, orange on white, magenta on white, green on white), or sets two signals against each other (yellow field with black type, slate field with an orange wedge). Dark navy or near-black "space" grounds with neon bar accents exist as a minority variant tied to wireframe game visuals. What never appears: pastel desaturation, muddy mid-tones, airbrushed blends, or more than roughly four hues plus neutrals in one piece. Approximate anchors: paper `#F2F1EC`–`#FFFFFF`, gunmetal `#6E7B85`–`#B9BEC4`, ink `#0A0A0C`, red `#D90429`–`#E60012`, orange `#F28000`–`#FFA300`, yellow `#F2D500`–`#FFE500`, magenta `#E5007E`–`#FF2E93`, green `#2E9E4F`–`#58B368`, teal-slate `#3E6E7E`–`#5A8CA0`.

### Geometry and composition
The structural signature is the diagonal: bands, splits, arrows and rotated type set at 45 or 60 degrees, slicing an otherwise calm field. Composition is asymmetric and grid-descended — a diagonal band crossing a flat ground; a centered emblem, pictogram or isometric machine block floating in empty white; a full-bleed saturated field carrying white line-geometry and corner-anchored data; or giant fragmented letterforms used as the image itself. Negative space is structural, not accidental: the empty field is what makes the dense cluster of microtext, icons and barcodes read as "maximalist." Silhouettes — aircraft, racing craft, robots, human pictogram figures — sit flat against the field with no cast shadow.

### Typography and text behavior
Type is a protagonist, not a caption. Display faces are futuristic and experimental: extended or compressed sans-serifs, stencil-fragmented letterforms, pixel/bitmap type, outline type rotated 90 degrees or run along the diagonal axis. Against them, monospace or condensed microtype supplies the texture: catalog numbers, spec lines, tracklists, coordinate strings, justified columns of tiny text, and the style's ironic punctuation — ™, ©, ®, barcodes, logo stacks and parody advertising claims. Japanese strings (kanji, katakana) appear as a documented recurring reference layer. Text may be vertical, diagonal, cropped by a band, or overprinted on shape; legibility of the microtext is optional, legibility of the display type is not.

### Motifs and objects, ranked by how centrally the style documents them
1. Striking flat vector shapes and hard-edged geometric planes.
2. 45°/60° diagonal lines, bands and splits.
3. Futuristic/experimental typography and monospace microtext blocks.
4. Flat, high-contrast color fields.
5. Maximalist-minimalist balance of void and data-cluster.
6. Silhouettes of machines, vehicles and pictogram humans.
7. Icons: concentric-circle targets and reticles, arrows, signage pictograms, plug/connector glyphs, isometric machine assemblies.
8. Japanese culture references: kanji/katakana strings, anime-influenced flat-cel figures and mascots.
9. Ironic consumerism: logo over-abundance, in-your-face ad phrasing, trademark and copyright glyphs, price/spec tables, barcodes.
Secondary, image-attested but optional: radial sunburst rays behind a figure, checkerboard strips, flip-digit scoreboard numerals, racing equipment (tires, pit boards), wireframe light-ray convergence, and — as a minority collage variant — a small photographic cutout pasted into the vector field with tiny leader-line labels.

### Materials, texture, lighting
The surface is print, not scene: matte flat ink on paper, crisp vector edges, no atmospheric depth. Texture comes from information (microtype, barcode stripes, line grids), not from grain or distress. Shading, when present at all, is flat cel stepping on figures or three-tone plane shading on isometric objects; a subtle radial sunburst is the most gradient-like effect the style admits. There is no naturalistic light source, no cast shadow, no glow bloom; the rare soft bokeh belongs only to the photo-collage variant. Rendered 3D appears only as flat-shaded, diagrammatic machinery, never as glossy PBR material.

### Typical subjects
Album and single sleeves for IDM, techno and hardstyle; game boxart, key art and menu UI for racing and rhythm games; exhibition and club posters; icon sets and pictogram signage; mascot and anime-figure promotional art; diagrammatic arrows and machine schematics; parody consumer packaging and advertising.

## Mood & Values

Vectorheart feels sleek, clinical and fleet — machine-age optimism delivered with club-culture cool and a raised eyebrow. Its values are utilitarian precision (the industrial-design lineage), intellectual play (type as puzzle, data as ornament), and ironic critique: it attacks consumerism by over-identifying with it, flooding the frame with trademarks and ad-speak until the advertising exposes itself. Beneath the detachment is rave-era euphoria — the diagonals and sunbursts are energy lines, the flat color is volume at maximum gain.

## Era & Origins

Born mid-to-late 1990s in UK and Japanese design studios, loosely descended from Swiss modernism, utilitarian industrial design, brutalism, and Peter Saville's Factory Records sleeves; coined as a term much later by Evan Collins. It peaked through the early-to-mid 2000s across Wipeout, Ridge Racer Type 4, Ace Combat 3, Beatmania IIDX, Rez and electronica sleeve design, stayed viable into the early 2010s, then lost mainstream ground to Flat Design's simpler corporate language, with a small revival from the late 2010s onward. It is a sibling of Y2K Futurism, Gen X Soft Club and Metalheart, and a cousin of Vectordelia and Vectorflourish.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. Build the image as a flat vector poster surface: hard edges, unmodulated color planes, zero depth-of-field.
2. Organize everything along a 45° or 60° axis — band, split, arrow, rotated baseline.
3. Let type carry the composition: one experimental display treatment plus at least one cluster of monospace microtext, catalog numbers and ™/©/® glyphs.
4. Choose one neutral ground and one to three signal colors from the palette rule; keep contrast maximal.
5. Balance void and density: a large calm field against one overloaded data cluster, icon stack or barcode strip.
6. Populate with silhouettes, pictograms, reticles, arrows or isometric machine blocks rather than modeled scenes.
7. Add the optional Japanese layer (katakana/kanji strings, flat-cel anime figure) and/or the irony layer (logo stacks, parody claims, spec tables) as seasoning, not requirement.
8. Keep lighting flat: no cast shadows, no glow; cel steps or isometric plane tones only.

Variation axes: subject may be any of the typical subjects above, from a restroom-style pictogram panel to a racing mascot to an abstract machine diagram; composition may range from near-empty minimal (one smear shape and a line of tiny type) to full-bleed maximal (shard clusters and overlapping microtext); color handling may invert between white-ground-with-accents and saturated-ground-with-white-geometry, with the dark space-ground neon-bar variant at the edge of the range; mood may slide from clinical museum poster to euphoric club flyer to satirical ad. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — no composition, figure or framing here is canonical.

Avoid: realistic photography as the base layer (small flat cutout collage excepted); gradients, gloss, chrome, glass or bevels; naturalistic lighting, cast shadows, atmospheric haze; painterly brushwork, watercolor, hand-drawn wobble; grunge distress, torn edges, acid-bright type-on-black rave chaos; hexagonal lattice HUD grids and scanline glow; night-city narrative scenes with neon signage; pastel airbrush softness and rounded lounge blobs; ornamental floral vector swirls; and corporate Flat Design simplicity devoid of microtext, diagonals and irony — the very language that replaced Vectorheart is not Vectorheart.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Abstract Tech** renders abstract 3D primitives with chrome, glass, soft glow and gradients; Vectorheart stays flat, printed and typography-led, with Swiss-grid discipline.
- **Acid Design** is later, deliberately ugly and chaotic: distorted type, grunge texture, neon on black; Vectorheart's density is ordered, its edges clean, its palette flat print ink.
- **Cyberpunk** depicts narrative environments — rain, night cities, cinematic neon light; Vectorheart depicts graphic surfaces with no environment and no light source at all.
- **Gen X Soft Club** shares the Y2K family but softens everything: airbrushed pastel gradients, rounded organic blobs, lounge warmth; Vectorheart is hard-edged, saturated and high-contrast.
- **Hexatron** builds its tech feel from hexagonal lattices, dark HUD panels and holographic glow; Vectorheart builds from 45/60-degree diagonals, flat poster fields and print microtext, and its futurism reads as ink on paper, not as a screen.
