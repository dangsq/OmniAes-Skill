## Overview

Expressionism is less a unified look than an attitude: the world rendered from inside a nervous system. Emerging in Germany in the 1900s–1920s through Die Brücke (Dresden, 1905) and Der Blaue Reiter (Munich, 1911), it rejects the recording of objective reality in favor of subjective emotional truth — anxiety, alienation, ecstasy, spiritual hunger — achieved through radical distortion of form and intense, non-naturalistic color. It grew as a reaction against industrialization, positivism, Naturalism and Impressionism, and it absorbed lessons from Van Gogh's charged brushwork, Gauguin's symbolic flat color and Edvard Munch's psychological swirls. Two temperaments coexist inside it: the social, urban, raw-emotion strand (Kirchner, Heckel, Schiele, Kokoschka) and the spiritual, symbolically colored, semi-abstract strand (Kandinsky, Marc, Jawlensky). A painted still life or a near-abstract color vortex can be equally Expressionist if the same rules of feeling-first distortion govern it.

## Visual Identity

**Color rule.** Color is assigned by emotion and symbol, never by local appearance: a blue horse, a green cheek, an orange sky are all correct. The palette runs intense, arbitrary and deliberately dissonant, favoring jarring contrasts over harmonious blending. Two broad chord families cover the historical range:

|---|---|---|
| Saturated dissonance | cobalt/ultramarine #1D3FA8–#2E4BA0, vermilion/crimson #C1272D–#E4342F, acid yellow #F2C230, viridian #1E7A46, magenta-violet #C2417E–#7A3B8F | Blaue Reiter animals and color-vortices, mask-like heads, street scenes |
| Somber earth | umber #5A4632, brick #8C3B2E, ochre #C89B3C, cream #E8DCC4, pale flesh #E8C9B0 over rust #A65A3A | Flemish-leaning still lifes and interiors, gaunt portraits, embrace scenes |

Either chord is legitimate; what is constant is that hues clash or glow with emotional intent and shadows are colored, never neutral gray.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them.** (1) Distorted or elongated figures — stretched limbs, hunched backs, hands clutched to chest or pressed to temple. (2) Raw psychological states made visible in faces and gesture: widened eyes, gaunt cheeks, clasped hands, embracing couples fused into one anxious mass. (3) Urban alienation — crowded streets, cafés, interiors where figures isolate amid each other. (4) Spirituality and symbolic color — animals (horses, deer) as vessels of inner life, landscapes of red hills and unnatural skies, compositions drifting toward abstraction as color-orchestration. (5) Masks — faces simplified to flat, mask-like planes with hard dark contours. (6) Woodcut texture — the revived print medium's blunt black lines, gouged planes and crude directness leaking into painting. (7) Agitated, swirling brushstrokes — curvilinear vortices and nervous directional marks.

**Materials and textures.** Oil paint laid on visibly: loaded, directional, agitated strokes whose grain follows feeling rather than form; impasto ridges in flower petals and sky alike. Alternate surface logic: flat tempera-like planes bounded by blunt black outlines (the mask-head and woodcut manner), and rough crayon or pastel dryness in drawn figures. Brushwork is never anonymized — no smooth blending, no polished glaze.

**Recurring subjects.** Self-portraits and portraits of strained sitters; embracing or entwined couples; solitary seated figures; street crowds and city nightlife; animals, especially horses and deer; landscapes with tilted hills and burning or sulfurous skies; flower still lifes in jugs subjected to the same distortion logic; and semi-abstract arrangements of curving color-forms.

**Composition and lighting.** Space is compressed and shallow, figures pushed against the picture plane; axes tilt, floors rake, perspective skews into wedges and vortices; horizons sit high or vanish. Balance is deliberately uneasy — asymmetry, cropped bodies, crowding. The spiritual strand organizes the canvas as swirling concentric or ribbon-like color rhythms instead of depicted space. Lighting is never a consistent natural source: light is emotional temperature — acid glare, sulfurous yellow daylight, night gloom with pallid faces, or flat even illumination on mask-like heads.

## Mood & Values

Angst is the cliché but not the limit: the style voices alienation, turmoil, erotic tension, tenderness under pressure, and a yearning for spiritual renewal. Its values rank subjective emotional experience over objective reality, sincerity over beauty, and ugliness admitted as truth. It carries a critique of modern industrial society alongside a belief that art can supply the spiritual charge modernity lacks. An Expressionist image should feel confessed, not composed.

## Era & Origins

Germany and Austria, 1900s–1920s: Die Brücke (Kirchner, Heckel, Schmidt-Rottluff) from 1905 with jarring color, agitated brushwork and the revived woodcut; Der Blaue Reiter (Kandinsky, Marc, Macke, Münter) from 1911 with symbolic color and steps toward abstraction. Precursors: Van Gogh, Gauguin, Munch; older echoes in Grünewald and El Greco. The style flourished in Weimar-era Berlin and spread across architecture, theatre, film and music (Metropolis, 1927), with parallel currents in Austria (Schiele, Kokoschka) and Flanders (Permeke, De Smet, Van den Berghe), where earthier palettes appear. It waned by the 1920s before New Objectivity, and seeded Abstract Expressionism and Neo-Expressionism.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** Decide the inner state first, then distort everything in service of it. Let color symbolize rather than describe. Bend anatomy, perspective and scale until they express feeling. Keep marks visible and gestural, or planes flat and blunt — never finishless polish. Prefer directness and crudeness over refinement; allow dissonance, awkwardness and ugliness. Give shadows and highlights emotional hues.

**Variation axes.** Subject: portrait, self-portrait, embrace, street crowd, café interior, animal, landscape, still life, or semi-abstraction — all valid. Composition: frontal mask-head, tilted street wedge, compressed interior, swirling color vortex, cropped couple. Lighting: acid daylight, nocturnal gloom, flat icon-like evenness, or no identifiable source at all. Color handling: saturated dissonant chords, somber earth chords, or near-abstract color orchestration. Mood: dread, ecstasy, solitude, tenderness, solemnity. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a flower jug, a blue animal, or an invented cityscape all read Expressionist when distortion, symbolic color and agitated or blunt handling govern them.

**Avoid.** Naturalistic local color and correct academic anatomy; smooth blended finishes, airbrushed gradients, photographic depth of field; Impressionist optical dapple describing light effects; decorative pastel prettiness or harmonious ornament; rational single-point perspective and symmetrical classical balance; neutral gray shadows; ironic satire or performative wink (sincerity is the contract); monochrome analytical faceting done for intellect rather than feeling; literal replication of famous compositions as templates.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Fauvism:** also arbitrary color, but Fauvist color delights — harmonious, decorative, rounded, at leisure with interiors and coasts. Expressionist color wounds: dissonant chords, jagged or blunt contours, anxious or spiritual subjects, ugliness embraced rather than avoided.
- **Cubism:** also distorts, but Cubism analyzes — multiple viewpoints, faceted planes, muted ochre-gray-sage palettes, intellectual construction. Expressionism distorts to feel — saturated symbolic color, legible emotional gesture, tilting or swirling space. Angular Brücke streets and late Marc semi-abstraction sit near the border; decide by color charge and felt gesture versus geometric analysis.
- **Post-Impressionism:** the precursor's charged brushwork and flat color still anchor in the observed motif and structured composition. Expressionism cuts the anchor further: skewed perspective, mask-like faces, urban and psychological themes, printmaking crudeness entering paint. Post-Impressionism intensifies nature; Expressionism replaces it.
- **Weimar Cabaret:** same cities and years, shared grotesquerie, but cabaret is performative — stage light, smoke, sequins, satirical grin, illustrative decadence. Expressionism is confessional: angst without irony, painterly gesture, spiritual weight. Cabaret mocks decay; Expressionism suffers it or seeks redemption through it.
- **Czech Cubism:** builds the picture from crystalline, sharp-edged facets and architectonic geometry, color subordinated to construction even in its Cubo-Expressionist fusion. Expressionist distortion is organic and gestural — limbs elongate, contours wobble, strokes agitate, and color leads structure. Cut-crystal planes and prismatic faces point to Prague; melting anatomy and screaming color point to Expressionism.

**Evidence note.** The surviving painted record emphasizes the oil-paint strand — impasto, visible stroke, flat mask planes, and an earthy Flemish-leaning register in some still lifes. The woodcut texture documented as central by the movement's own writings surfaces in painting mainly as blunt contour and planar crudeness; when generating print-like images, invoke gouged black line and bare plank-white areas directly rather than expecting it from painterly references.
