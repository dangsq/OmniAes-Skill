# Deathrock

Deathrock is the "missing link" between punk and goth: a late-1970s/early-1980s fusion that keeps punk's raw, confrontational DIY energy and bolts on gothic atmosphere and classic horror-film theatrics. It developed in parallel in Los Angeles (Christian Death, 45 Grave, Castration Squad, Kommunity FK) and the UK (London's Batcave, Specimen, Alien Sex Fiend, plus a doomier Yorkshire scene), and its look is accordingly a hybrid — street-level punk scruff dressed up as a walking B-movie. Everything in the aesthetic is self-made: hair teased into architecture, jackets painted and pinned at kitchen tables, fishnets deliberately destroyed. The result reads simultaneously spooky, campy and defiant.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is a black-dominated frame punctured by pale skin and at most one or two small accents. Black carries the clothing, hair and background (#050505–#1c1c1e); bone white and corpse pallor carry faces and blown highlights (#d8d2c4–#f2efe6). Accents are rationed: blood red (#6b0f0f–#a11212) in lipstick, print or a single garment; deep purple and violet (#2f1b3d–#5a3a7a) as hair sheen or stage gel; cold blue (#232a3a–#4a5a7a) as dye tint or shadow tone. Where color film appears at all, it is degraded — cyan-green or sepia casts (#7d8f83, #a8906a) from cheap 35mm stock and aged prints. A correct image may be pure monochrome, faded color, or black-plus-one-accent; what must hold is the dominance of black and the pallor of the face against it.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how firmly the text documents them)

1. **The deathhawk** — the single most iconic feature: a teased, backcombed variation of the punk mohawk, raised into a tall fan or crest, often with shaved or short sides and longer side pieces framing the face. Usually black, sometimes with purple or blue sheen.
2. **Ripped fishnets** — a staple worn as tights, as shirts, or cut into "spiderweb" sleeves on jackets and tops; runs, holes and ladderings are intentional.
3. **Corpse makeup on all genders** — pale white foundation, heavy black eyeliner socked around the eye, dark lipstick (black, oxblood, purple). Androgynous and exaggerated, never subtle.
4. **DIY-customized clothing** — layered leather, lace and band t-shirts; hand-painted band lettering and occult symbols across jacket backs; patches, studs, spikes, safety pins, tape repairs.
5. **B-movie horror and occult graphics** — spider webs, bats, zombies, cemeteries, skeletons, crosses (straight and inverted), surreal juxtapositions (a wedding dress on a corpse-painted figure is exactly in spirit).
6. **Leather-and-lace mixture** — cropped or cropped-down leather jackets over lace tops and mesh; plain black knit sweaters as the mundane counterweight.
7. **Hardware** — studded collars and bracelets, chokers, chains, bondage straps, platform boots.
8. **Secondary, image-observed accents** (low rank, not text-canonical): black-and-white or black-and-red striped sleeves and leg warmers, huddled backstage groupings, graffiti-covered walls and lockers as backdrop.

### Materials and textures

Matte teased hair with visible backcombed strands; open-mesh fishnet with broken threads; cracked and scuffed leather; coarse lace; chunky knit; cold metal studs, spikes and chains; fabric paint sitting stiff and slightly uneven on jacket backs; paper-thin band flyers and photocopied print matter. Surfaces should look handled, worn and self-made, never factory-fresh.

### Recurring subjects

Musicians mid-performance gripping a mic stand; backstage and dressing-room candids; street and club-corner portraits; couples and small groups huddled shoulder-to-shoulder; full-body silhouette shots showing the vertical logic of tall hair, narrow black column, fishnet legs and platforms. All genders wear the same makeup and hair logic. Prop tableaux (cemetery, webs, bats) are licensed by the text even though the photographic record favors portraits and gig shots.

### Composition and lighting

Portrait-orientation frames dominate. Single figures centered from chest or waist up, or clusters of two to five bodies packed tight; gazes are direct and confrontational or lost in performance. Backgrounds are cluttered with place-evidence: graffiti, lockers, club walls, stone steps. Lighting is harsh and unglamorous — direct on-camera flash blowing the pale face against black cloth, a single magenta/violet/blue stage gel washing the scene, flat overcast daylight, or a single bare bulb. Monochrome treatments push toward photocopy contrast: crushed blacks, blown highlights, coarse grain.

### Medium and surface

Grainy 35mm snapshot, fanzine photocopy, gig flyer print. Analog degradation — grain, contrast crush, color cast, print breakup — is part of the aesthetic language, not a defect.

## Mood & Values

Macabre theatre played with a wink: campy horror rather than genuine menace, doom rather than despair. The core value is the DIY ethos — self-creation, individuality, androgyny, everything assembled by hand. There is gloom (the UK scene grew in industrial cities "blackened with exhaust fumes" under economic collapse and serial-killer fear) but also play: morbid humor, surrealism, deliberate bad taste worn proudly. The face says corpse; the posture says defiance.

## Era & Origins

Emerges circa 1979–1984 from the punk scenes of Los Angeles (Anti-Club, *New Wave Theatre*, Christian Death's 1982 *Only Theatre of Pain*) and concurrently in London's Batcave (opened 1982) and industrial Yorkshire. Reacts against straight punk's street realism by adding horror theatrics, and against goth's later romanticism by keeping punk's crude speed and confrontation. Fades into European gothic rock by the late 80s; revived late 90s through the 2000s; feeds 2010s "dark punk" and G-beat. Visual shorthand should default to the early-80s moment unless a revival flavor is explicitly wanted.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Build the silhouette first: deathhawk volume on top, narrow black column below, fishnet breaks in the mesh, pale mask of a face between them.
- Keep black dominant; spend at most one or two accent colors, in small areas (lips, hair sheen, one gel light).
- Make the hand-made visible: uneven painted lettering, safety-pin repairs, deliberate runs in fishnets, crooked patches.
- Apply corpse makeup regardless of gender: matte pallor, smudged black eye sockets, dark lips.
- Degrade the medium: grain, flash blowout, crushed monochrome contrast, or a faded color cast.
- Keep horror B-movie and camp: printed skeletons, paper webs, rubber bats, surrealist props — never glossy gore.
- Pose confrontational or absorbed (mic gripped, arms crossed, huddled), never polished or seductive-glam.

**Variation axes.** Subject: stage performance, backstage candid, street portrait, couple or group huddle, club corner, cemetery or prop tableau, surreal flyer/album artwork. Composition: vertical portrait, cramped group, full-body silhouette, low stage angle, tight interior. Lighting: direct flash, single colored gel, overcast daylight, bare-bulb chiaroscuro, xerox monochrome. Color handling: pure black-and-white, faded cyan/sepia film, or black plus one accent. Mood: campy play, doomy gloom, cold confrontation, romantic-macabre. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**

- Clean digital gloss: smooth skin, softbox studio light, saturated commercial color, tidy symmetry.
- Goth romanticism: flowing velvet, Victorian corsetry, candelabra elegance, ethereal mist.
- Horror-punk rockabilly: quiffs, pompadours, devilocks, bowling shirts, 50s diner camp.
- Psychobilly: tall rockabilly quiffs, double basses, tattoo-flash brightness.
- Plain punk: short spiked mohawk strips without teased volume, plaid as the main event, no corpse makeup.
- Cybergoth / nu-goth / pastel goth: neon falls, goggles, PVC shine, pastel hair, cute motifs.
- Metal signifiers: long natural hair, denim battle vests as the focal garment.
- Factory-new clothing, perfect seams, absence of pins, paint or patches.
- Warm golden-hour light, beach daylight, any sunny wholesome context.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Fastest tells |
|---|---|
| **Goth** | Goth leans romantic/ethereal: velvet, lace finery, flowing or sleek backcombed hair, melancholic elegance. Deathrock keeps punk scruff: deathhawk, ripped fishnets, studs, campy B-movie horror, sweat and motion. |
| **Horror Punk** | Horror punk roots in 50s rockabilly/doo-wop: devilocks, comic skeleton prints, cleaner graphic-tee look. Deathrock roots in glam rock: androgynous corpse makeup, deathhawk volume, spiderweb fishnets, Batcave theatrics. |
| **Post-Punk** | Post-punk is art-school austerity — ordinary or sharp-tailored clothes, monochrome without makeup theatre, neutral affect. Add corpse paint, hair architecture and occult motifs and it becomes deathrock. |
| **Psychobilly** | Quiff versus deathhawk is the instant tell; psychobilly adds rockabilly cuts, double bass, brighter reds and blues. |
| **Punk** | Punk mohawks are short spiked strips; punk favors plaid, street realism, minimal makeup. Deathrock teases volume with side pieces, paints every face pale, and dresses the whole body in horror iconography. |

When an image legitimately sits on a boundary (the style openly overlaps horror punk and goth), decide by the trio: deathhawk silhouette, ripped fishnet, and visible DIY horror hand-work. All three present means deathrock.
