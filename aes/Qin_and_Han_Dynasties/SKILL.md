# Qin and Han Dynasties

The first imperial aesthetic of China (221 BCE–220 CE), born when the Qin unified the warring states and the Han consolidated the empire. It is an art of two masters: the state and the tomb. Qin Legalism produced taste that is simple, grand and monumental — art as proof of empire — while Han Confucianism pushed ritual hierarchy into every layout, garment and procession row. At the same time, a nationwide cult of elaborate burial sealed most surviving works inside tombs, so the style we know is overwhelmingly funerary: realistic terracotta armies, painted silk banners, murals of myths, lacquer cases and boards packed into dark chambers. Overlaying all of it is a color doctrine: under wuxing (five-elements) theory the Qin claimed the Water Virtue and honored black, the Eastern Han claimed the Fire Virtue and honored red — which is why the style's irreducible signature is cinnabar red and lacquer black, each serving as ground or figure for the other.

## Visual Identity

### Palette: two sovereigns over an earth chorus

The rule is a two-color sovereignty — red and black — supported by untreated earth neutrals. Neither sovereign ever appears alone: red decorates black lacquer, black lines decorate red lacquer, and either may dominate depending on whether the image reads Qin (black-noble) or Han (red-noble). Around them sit the unpainted materials: clay, stone, bronze patina, raw silk. Gilt and jade-white appear only as accents; there is no pastel, no pure white field, no saturated modern hue.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual authority

1. **The Four Divine Sishen creatures** — Azure Dragon, White Tiger, Vermilion Bird, Black Tortoise — the style's emblematic beasts, documented on eaves tiles, mirrors, murals and textiles. They are never static: each coils, wings or tails streaming into arcs that fill its frame.
2. **Yunqi cloud-qi scrolls** — abstract, spiraling lines of fluid motion that fill lacquer fields, silk embroidery and coffin paintings with continuous S-curves and comma-shaped whorls.
3. **Liubo board-game geometry (TLV pattern)** — a square inscribed in a circle, edged with T-, L- and V-shaped marks; the signature of bronze mirrors and lacquer game boards.
4. **Other mythic beings** — Fuxi and Nüwa, the loong, the fenghuang, the jinwu sun-crow — prevalent in tomb paintings.
5. **Geometric, textual and symbolic animal-plant motifs**, later joined by Silk Road imports: grapevine, camels, merchant caravans, and the first Buddhist elements (late Han only).
6. **Secular narrative scenes** on pictorial bricks: harvests, feasts, storytelling, markets, and horse-and-chariot travel organized strictly by social rank.

### Materials and textures

Glossy painted lacquer with rounded contours and flared rims inherited from bronze and pottery; cast bronze with dark patina and occasional gilt inlay; matte granular terracotta and fired clay; dense jacquard brocade and chain-stitch embroidery whose floats catch light; stone relief known both carved and as flat ink rubbing; painted silk banners with tassels; rarely, Mediterranean-formula soda-lime glass. Surface character splits cleanly: ornament is fluid and abstract, sculpture is sober and realistic.

### Recurring subjects

Burial goods above all: terracotta soldier armies, lacquer liubo boards and lian cases, painted silk funerary banners, tomb murals. Architectural wadang eaves tiles; bronze mirrors; mountain-form incense burners and other ritual metalwork; galloping bronze horses. Human figures in cross-collared pao and shenyi robes — quju with its curved, wrapping hem in the Western Han, zhiju with its straight hem in the Eastern Han — noble women with the tilted duoma-ji bun, men with rank-defining guan hats. Horses, chariots, farmers, storytellers, officials in ranked rows.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

- **Circular medallion**: a creature coiled around a central hemispherical boss inside a round frame (eaves tiles, mirror knobs), limbs and tails stretched into arcs so the circle reads full.
- **Concentric bands and square-in-circle**: rings of pattern around a liubo square; order within swirl.
- **Horizontal narrative registers**: flat, profile figures stacked in bands, sized and placed by social rank; conical mountains and gabled towers scattered as stage props without perspective.
- **Central-axis symmetry** in architecture, courtyards and processions — Confucian ritual made spatial.
- **All-over swirling fill** in lacquer and textile fields: horror vacui of yunqi whorls, never empty minimal ground.
- **Lighting modes**: flat, unmodeled light in pictorial art; even diffuse museum light on isolated objects; warm torch-and-lantern glow against vermilion-and-black interiors in reconstructed scenes; soft daylight for costume and field scenes. All are legitimate.

## Mood & Values

Solemn grandeur and cosmic order. The style expresses imperial power (Qin's simple, colossal statements), ritual hierarchy (Han's ranked rows and symmetrical axes), and a deep afterlife pragmatism — armies, banners, vessels and murals built to serve the dead. Against that gravity runs a counter-current of liquid vitality: clouds spiral, dragons coil, horses gallop on one hoof. Lavishness is earned, not frivolous: wealth displays through dense craft (brocade, lacquer, gilt) rather than loose opulence.

## Era & Origins

Emerges 221 BCE with Qin unification; state workshops and a constrained economy concentrate early art around the First Emperor's commissions. The Han inherits Chu-region art — Liu Bang's love of Chu music carried pre-Qin southern swirl and romance into Han lacquer design — then loosens: after the Rule of Wen and Jing, private wealth funds lavish art and elaborate burial becomes a national trend. From 130 BCE the Silk Road injects Central Asian techniques and motifs. The style reacts against the pre-Qin Bronze Age: still religious, but less theocratic, increasingly interested in regular secular life.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** Build every image from the two-sovereign palette plus earth neutrals; let red-on-black or black-on-red carry the focal surface. Choose motifs by the ranking above: Sishen, yunqi swirl and liubo geometry first, mythic figures and secular narrative next, Silk Road imports only as late-Han accents. Commit to a material logic (lacquer gloss, clay matte, bronze patina, silk float, rubbing ink) and keep texture honest to it. Use the composition grammar: medallion coil, concentric bands, ranked registers, central axis, or all-over swirl fill. Dress figures in cross-collared shenyi (quju or zhiju), guan hats, duoma-ji buns. Pick one lighting mode and stay in it.

**Variation axes.** Subject: vessel still-life, garment study, court or procession scene, harvest narrative, beast sculpture, tile or mirror design, textile close-up, ink rubbing, tomb mural, axial architecture. Composition: medallion, register, symmetry, swirl fill, single-object framing. Lighting: diffuse museum, warm lantern, flat pictorial, soft daylight. Color handling: red-dominant, black-dominant, monochrome clay/rubbing grey, patina-with-gilt, silk rust-ochre-cream. Mood: funerary solemn, courtly lavish, martial grand, cosmic mythic. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a lacquer cup on plain cloth, an invented cloud-scroll banner, or a fictional garrison scene all qualify if they obey palette, motif and composition law.

**Avoid.** Later-dynasty dress and objects: round-collared robes, buttoned jackets, standing collars, blue-and-white porcelain, sancai glaze, ink landscape painting. One-point perspective, cast shadows and chiaroscuro modeling in pictorial modes. Pastel, neon or desaturated modern palettes; pure-white minimal fields. Empty negative space where the style expects swirling fill (ranked processions and axial architecture excepted). Dominant Buddhist iconography before the late Han. Gold-dominant opulence — gilt is inlay, not surface. Static, heraldic, front-facing beasts: Sishen must coil. Modern studio gloss or synthetic sheen on lacquer and silk.

## Discrimination Cues

No neighboring styles are recorded in the source, so these cues are derived from the recorded history itself. Against the **pre-Qin Bronze Age (Shang–Zhou)**: Qin–Han work is less theocratic, full of secular daily life, and speaks red-black lacquer rather than ritual-bronze dominance. Against **Chu art**: Chu supplies the swirling lacquer language but is regional and pre-imperial; Qin–Han adds imperial symmetry, rank order and funerary scale. Against **later dynasties (Tang, Song, Ming)**: those bring round-collared robes, sancai and porcelain, gold-heavy court splendor and perspective-aware painting — all absent here. Within the era, **Qin** reads black-noble, austere, colossal, with hyper-realistic martial sculpture; **Han** reads red-noble, lavish, swirl-filled, with ranked narrative bricks. Against the **modern hanfu revival**: 2010s reconstructions of the quju shenyi (some historically incorrect) appear as clean studio photography — recognizable as revival, not relic, by their photographic styling rather than by garment logic.
