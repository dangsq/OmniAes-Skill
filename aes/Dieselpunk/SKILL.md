# Dieselpunk

Dieselpunk is a retrofuturistic science-fiction aesthetic that takes the technology, design and politics of roughly the 1910s to the 1950s — the interwar period, the world wars, the Jazz Age — and extrapolates them into speculative, often alternate-history worlds. The term was coined in 2001 by game designer Lewis Pollak for his tabletop RPG *Children of the Sun*, explicitly to name something darker and grittier than Steampunk; the "-punk" suffix signals a countercultural, frequently pessimistic lens on progress. Visually the style fuses diesel-age machinery (heavy tanks, armored trains, zeppelins, biplanes, riveted automobiles, and in its modern revival, legged walker mechs) with the design languages of Art Deco, Streamline Moderne, Constructivism, Bauhaus and Futurism, plus the graphic voice of wartime propaganda posters and pulp magazines. The tradition splits into two recognized flavors: **Ottensian** (aka Decopunk) — utopian, chrome-polished, Roaring-Twenties optimism uninterrupted — and **Piecraftian** — grim, militaristic, war-haunted, further divided into a crude, grimy WWI-flavored vein and a more refined, welded WWII-flavored vein, with **Diesel Noir** (crime-ridden but not dystopian cities) and **Diesel Dystopia** (totalitarian or post-apocalyptic collapse) as its narrative poles. Most unqualified "dieselpunk" imagery lives on the Piecraftian side of that spectrum, with Deco brightness used as accent rather than baseline.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (the rule, not a swatch)

The base of a dieselpunk image is desaturated industrial neutral: soot black, gunmetal and fog grey, olive drab, oxide brown, bare-steel silver. Against that base, accent color is rationed and purposeful — brass and bronze on fittings, bone-cream poster stock, vermilion or ochre on signage and insignia, Deco teal or deep navy in Jazz-Age or poster contexts. Fully monochrome (ink grey, photographic black-and-white) is a legitimate handling, inherited from period press photography and illustration. Bright, saturated Deco color is allowed only where the text of the style allows optimism: posters, nightlife, Ottensian chrome-and-lacquer interiors.

- Soot black / charcoal: `#14161a` – `#2b2d31`
- Gunmetal / steel grey: `#3f464e` – `#7d858d`
- Fog / overcast sky blue-grey: `#9aa7b0` – `#c3ccd2`
- Olive drab / khaki: `#4a5232` – `#6b7048`
- Rust / oxide brown: `#5d4030` – `#8a5a38`
- Brass / bronze accent: `#8a6a3a` – `#b08d57`
- Bone / cream poster stock: `#e6dcc3` – `#f2ead6`
- Deco accents (rationed): teal `#5f8f88`, vermilion `#b23a2e`, ochre `#c9973b`, navy `#23304a`

Rule of thumb: neutrals occupy most of the canvas; saturated color appears in posters, trim, insignia, lamps and sky light, never as an overall wash (except in deliberate poster-mode work).

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how firmly the text documents them)

1. **Diesel-powered machinery as protagonist.** Heavy tanks, armored trains, early aircraft (zeppelins and biplanes above all), robust riveted automobiles; mechanical elements — exposed gears, intricate pipework, oversized functional components — exaggerated or stylized. Walker mechs and legged war machines are a strong modern extension of this motif (concept-art and tabletop lineage), always bulky, slow-looking and engine-driven rather than sleek robotics.
2. **Riveted surfaces and engine turning / jewelling.** Rows of rivets are the style's fingerprint; machine-etched swirl patterns on aluminum panels signal crafted industrial elegance. Weld seams replace rivets in the WWII-flavored vein — a useful period tell.
3. **Art Deco and Streamline Moderne geometry.** Sleek horizontal lines, teardrop fairings, ziggurat setbacks, sunburst motifs, chrome trim, geometric patterning on buildings, vehicles and objects; Constructivist/Bauhaus/Futurist strength of form and dynamic diagonal composition.
4. **Vacuum tubes and nascent electronics.** Glass tubes, analog dials, bakelite switches, cable bundles — mechanical technology meeting early electrical technology. (Text-documented firmly; visually subtle, so treat as detail dressing rather than subject.)
5. **Propaganda poster and pulp magazine graphics.** Bold display typography, imperative slogans, radial sunburst rays, flat heroic figures, limited print palettes — the style's graphic-design voice.
6. **Period fashion ensembles.** Trench coats, bomber jackets, military uniforms, mechanic overalls, waistcoats, pinstriped suits, flapper dresses, wide-leg trousers; fedoras, aviator caps and goggles, leather gloves, wristwatches, Oxfords; riveted metal accessories and Deco-patterned details. Uniforms and workwear dominate the grim pole; tailored Jazz-Age civilian dress marks the optimistic pole. Stylized steel cuirasses and helms on soldiers appear as a militaristic variation, but only read as dieselpunk when fused with period firearms and machinery.
7. **Industrial settings.** Hangars, train sheds and terminals, factories, refineries, smoggy city canyons, trenches, airfields, docks — architecture of steel truss, glass shed roofs and concrete mass.

### Materials and textures

Steel plate with rivet rows or weld beads; engine-turned and cast aluminum; cast iron and blackened steel; brass fittings; bakelite and glass (tubes, dials, lenses); oiled canvas, leather straps and flight jackets, wool uniforms and tweed; rubber hoses; cordwood and coal grime. Surfaces carry use: soot streaks behind exhausts, oil staining under engines, rust blooms at seams, mud on lower hulls and boots, steam and condensation. Polish and chrome belong to hero surfaces, Deco interiors and the Ottensian pole — never to the whole world.

### Recurring subjects

Machines at work or at rest dwarfing human figures; crowds in period dress massed beneath locomotives or in terminals; soldiers, mechanics, aviators, detectives and spies; legged war machines striding over fields and snow; flying ships and airships above cloud decks; ruined or totalitarian cityscapes; poster heroes in mid-flight or mid-stride. Human scale is the measuring stick: the machine is the monument.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Wide horizontal matte-painting views of industrial canyons, terminals and battlefields; low-angle hero shots that let a hull or locomotive loom; diagonals thrusting across frame (Futurist dynamism); tiny silhouetted figures for scale; crowds as a dark textured band along the bottom edge. Poster mode flips this: vertical format, flat centered figure, radial rays, heavy type. Lighting ranges from film-noir chiaroscuro — cool ambient dark pierced by warm tungsten lamps, searchlight shafts and volumetric smoke — to overcast diffuse daylight on fields and trenches, to pale high-altitude sun above cloud seas, to the even flat light of printed poster art.

### Medium and finish

Painterly digital concept art and matte painting with visible brush economy; gouache/liquitex poster illustration with print-like flatness; ink or grayscale illustration echoing period press art; clean neutral-background character and vehicle study sheets. Photorealism is rare; the style tolerates and enjoys the hand-made look.

## Mood & Values

Dieselpunk holds two feelings in tension: awe at machine-age power and dread of what that power does to people. Its stated values — technological advancement, societal change, war, rebellion, dystopia and utopia, human-machine interaction, noir elements — produce images that are simultaneously admiring and wary: gleaming hulls under dirty skies, elegant Deco lettering on weapons, crowds cheering machines that will consume them. The "-punk" ethos means countercultural scrutiny: totalitarianism, espionage, resistance and stagnation are always available as subtext. Even optimistic Ottensian work carries a whiff of decadence — champagne brightness on the eve of a crash.

## Era & Origins

The visual language is borrowed from 1910s–1950s Europe and America: interwar industrialization, the world wars, the Jazz Age, early cinema (*Metropolis*, 1927; *Things to Come*, 1936), pulp magazines, film noir, and dystopian literature such as Orwell's *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. The label itself is recent — coined 2001 — and the aesthetic self-consciously positions against Steampunk (darker, grittier, steel instead of brass) and within the wider retrofuturism family. Its revival runs through concept art, tabletop wargames and video games that imagine alternate 1930s–40s wars fought with impossible machines.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Pick a flavor first (Ottensian / Piecraftian-WWI / Piecraftian-WWII / Diesel Noir / Diesel Dystopia); it decides finish, palette grit and mood before any subject is chosen.
2. Build machines from interwar logic: riveted or welded steel plate, radial engines and propellers, fabric-and-frame wings, zeppelin hulls, rail bogies, caterpillar tracks, articulated walker legs, exhaust stacks, access hatches, analog dials. Exaggerate scale and function, keep mechanical plausibility.
3. Dress surfaces with history: rivet rows or weld seams per flavor, engine-turned panels on hero metal, soot, oil, rust, mud; reserve mirror chrome for Deco trim and Ottensian work.
4. Keep palette discipline: dominant desaturated neutrals, rationed accents in brass, cream, vermilion, teal, ochre; monochrome handling is acceptable.
5. Clothe people in period ensembles — uniforms, overalls, trench coats, fedoras, aviator caps and goggles — and use them as scale markers against machinery.
6. Fill the air: smoke, steam, smog, rain, snow, cloud decks; light shafts, searchlights, tungsten practicals against cool shadow.
7. Compose for monumentality (tiny figures, massed crowds, looming diagonals) or switch fully into poster mode (flat figure, sunburst rays, bold Deco type, imperative slogan) — never half-way.
8. Let narrative tension show: insignia, queues, checkpoints, wreckage, rallies, or noir rain-slick streets imply the politics without spelling them out.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any vehicle, machine, building, interior, battlefield, cityscape, portrait, fashion plate, poster or vehicle-study sheet within the era logic. Composition may range from epic wide matte to intimate cockpit or street-corner noir to flat poster geometry. Lighting may be noir night, overcast day, hangar spotlights, high-altitude sun or printed flatness. Color handling may be monochrome, muted painterly, or Deco-vivid at the optimistic pole. Mood may run from utopian gleam to dystopian ash. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a dieselpunk greenhouse, radio studio, ocean liner or boxing gym is as valid as a train or mech, provided the materials, palette, silhouette and mood obey the rules above.

**Avoid.**
- Steampunk ornament: brass filigree, decorative clockwork, copper boilers, corsets and frock coats, Victorian clutter.
- Atompunk signifiers: radiation trefoils, mushroom clouds, googie diners, tailfins, flying saucers, bubble domes, mid-century pastels, space-race iconography.
- Raygun-gothic space opera: alien planets, starfields, finned moon rockets, ray guns with glass rings as the focal gadget.
- Modern anachronism: LEDs, flat screens, plastics, carbon fiber, seamless CNC-smooth curves, digital signage, contemporary firearms and vehicles.
- Cyberpunk neon, holograms, chrome-and-rain night cities lit by signage.
- Fantasy kitsch: swords, heraldry, dragons, magic; note that stylized soldier plate is only acceptable fused with industrial militarism.
- Incoherent flavor mixing: pristine chrome utopia with no wear in a gritty brief, or gratuitous grime over an Ottensian Deco scene; oversaturated fantasy color or clean vector flatness without period texture.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shares with dieselpunk | Tells that mean dieselpunk instead |
|---|---|---|
| Adventure Pulp | Same era wardrobe: fedoras, satchels, aviators, exotic locales | Dieselpunk adds impossible or exaggerated machinery, alt-history politics and industrial scale; pulp stays within plausible period adventure |
| Art Deco | Deco geometry, sunbursts, chrome trim, sleek lines | Deco alone is ornament and luxury without narrative; dieselpunk subordinates Deco to machines, soot, uniforms and story tension |
| Atompunk | Retrofuturism, mid-century optimism | Atompunk is post-1945 atomic/space age: googie, tailfins, saucers, pastels, radiation motifs; dieselpunk stops at propellers, rivets, zeppelins and war grit |
| Decopunk | Identical Deco/Streamline vocabulary | Decopunk is the clean utopian pole: polished chrome, no soot, no war, no noir; dieselpunk proper admits grime, olive drab, battle damage and shadow |
| Raygun Gothic | Streamlined rocketry, poster heroics | Raygun Gothic is gadget-and-planet space adventure in comic primaries; dieselpunk's speculative tech stays terrestrial, industrial and politically weighted |

Quick tests: look at the metal — rivets, welds and soot say dieselpunk; brass filigree says Steampunk; seamless chrome and tailfins say Atompunk/Decopunk. Look at the air — smoke, smog and searchlight shafts say dieselpunk; clear pastel skies say Atompunk. Look at the mood — war, noir or wary optimism says dieselpunk; pure decorative glamour says Art Deco; pure swashbuckling says Adventure Pulp. Film Noir shares dieselpunk's lighting but lacks its speculative machinery; Steampunk shares its retrofuturism but wears the previous century's clothes.
