# Decoplex (90s Deco Revival)

Decoplex is the populist end of the Art Deco revival: the design language of 1990s and 2000s movie multiplexes, revived diners, metro stations, studio title cards and event logos. It is the third wave of deco motifs after Disco Deco and Deco-Luxe, and it deliberately drops their luxury pretensions — instead of marble and champagne glamour it offers neon-traced carpet, chrome edging and concession-stand spectacle. Its imagination is a retro-futuristic dream of the mid-20th century — 1930s–40s Hollywood, WPA murals, early air travel — rendered through a 1990s lens of cel-flat illustration, airbrush gradients and early CGI smoothness. The result is theatrical, playful and cinematic: deco as mass entertainment, not as aristocracy.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is **high-chroma neon carried on darkness or depth, with chrome only as trim**. Three neon primaries dominate: neon red/coral (`#FF2D3F`–`#FF5A4E`), neon yellow-gold (`#FFC72C`–`#FF9E00`), and electric blue/teal (`#00A8C6`–`#23D5E8`), often joined by hot magenta (`#E6007E`–`#FF3FA4`) as a fourth accent. These sit on near-black grounds (`#0A0A0C`–`#16161C`) for signage and night exteriors, or on deep jewel tones — sapphire `#14315C`, emerald-teal `#0E5A5A`, plum `#6E1E4A` — for interiors. Chromium and brushed steel (`#B9BEC4`–`#E8ECEF`) appear as edging, fins and type faces, never as a field color. A second legitimate register is the **print/poster ground**: warm cream (`#F2E8D5`), pastel mint (`#A8D8CE`) and pastel rose (`#F2B8C6`) with flat black shapes, inherited from WPA murals and aero posters. Contrasting cool/warm splits (teal wall against magenta neon, orange rays against blue sky) are core; muted, monochrome or desaturated palettes are outside the style.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Vertical projections** — towering blade signs, fin stacks, stepped entryway pylons, vertical neon tracking and bulb-studded marquee towers that pull the eye upward.
2. **Parallel horizontal lines** — speed lines and layered banding on logos, cornices and ribbed ceilings; the streamline "motion" device.
3. **Neon accents** — tube light used as *drawing line*: outlining arches, swoops, zigzags and letterforms, not as ambient wash.
4. **Art deco fonts** — thick geometric caps, center-extruded with a ridge bevel, finished in polished metallic, hard drop shadow or neon edging; plus the thin, tall, pointed-apex deco face used on studio title cards.
5. **Swoops, sunbursts, arches, bold streamlined curves, zigzags** — the ornamental grammar behind logos, prosceniums and poster rays.
6. **Floor planes** — massive neon-lit geometric patterned carpet, black-and-white checkerboard tile, stylized terrazzo.
7. **Material surfaces** — chromium edging, glass brick windows, brushed steel, vitrolite glass panels.
8. **Branch subjects** — zigzag modernized metro architecture, curved silver diner bodies, chromium-edged booths.

### Materials and textures

Glossy and hard: vitrolite enamel glass, glass brick, brushed and polished chrome, terrazzo, glazed tile, patterned nylon carpet. Light sources are physical tubes and bulbs with visible housings. Illustration branches substitute flat cel shading or smooth airbrush/early-CGI gradients for these materials, but keep the same gloss logic — specular highlights on chrome, glow around tubes.

### Recurring subjects

Movie multiplex entryways, lobbies and concession stands; auditorium stages framed by arched prosceniums; diner interiors and exteriors; metro station halls; logo lockups for studios, brands and sporting events; film title cards and poster art (rocket-pack aviators, flat-shaded night cities); 1990s software box art and promotional merchandise; gig and event posters in the flat WPA-derived manner.

### Composition and lighting

Frontal symmetry for entryways and emblem logos; one-point perspective down lobbies and corridors with checkerboard or carpeted floors receding; sunburst-centered compositions that honor a logo or figure; typographic-field compositions where the type *is* the image; stepped, setback silhouettes borrowed from skyline and ziggurat forms. Lighting is self-emissive and artificial — neon tubes, marquee bulbs, cove and soffit light, glowing glass brick — against night skies or windowless interiors. The poster branch uses flat, even, shadowless light with hard flat shapes; the CGI branch uses smooth gradient glows. Daylight realism is rare and, when present, reads as flat pastel poster light.

## Mood & Values

Playfulness and nostalgia for the golden age of Hollywood. Decoplex sells awe to everyone: movie magic, family night out, the thrill of the lobby as much as the film. It is optimistic, theatrical and slightly dreamlike — a mid-century future imagined by the 1990s — and it values spectacle, craft in signage, and the comfort of familiar commercial ritual over exclusivity or irony.

## Era & Origins

Emerged in the United States in the 1990s and flourished into the 2000s, in AMC-style megaplex builds, classic 1990s Regal theaters, early-1990s Los Angeles Metro Rail stations and the revived streamlined diner. As the third deco wave it reacts against Disco Deco's glitter and Deco-Luxe's luxury by being more restrained and more populist, reaching further back to 1930s–40s movie palaces, studio title cards, WPA mural graphics and pre-war aviation posters.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Make the deco element structural: the fin, blade sign, arch or extruded wordmark *is* the subject, not a decal on a generic room.
- Let neon draw lines — outline edges, trace curves and zigzags — and keep glow contained around tubes.
- Extrude type: center-ridge bevel, metallic or neon-edged face, hard drop shadow; or use the thin pointed-apex deco face for title-card moods.
- Ground chroma in black or jewel depth; reserve chrome for trim, edging and type.
- Combine vertical thrust (fins, towers, blade signs) with horizontal speed lines in the same frame.
- Use sunbursts and ray fans as honorific backdrops for logos, heroes and stages.
- Give floors a voice: checkerboard, terrazzo or neon geometric carpet.
- Light the scene from its own fixtures; if working in the poster branch, use flat even light and hard flat shapes.
- Finish with the 90s lens: crisp vector or cel edges, airbrush gradient skies, slight CGI smoothness — no film grain, no sepia.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across theaters, diners, transit halls, posters, logos, title cards, box art, aviation heroes, stages and pavilions. Composition may range from frontal symmetry to one-point corridors to pure typographic fields. Lighting may range from neon night to cove-lit interior to flat poster light. Color handling may range from neon-on-black to jewel-tone interior to cream-and-pastel print. Medium may range from photography to cel-flat illustration to airbrush to early CGI. Mood may range from playful to awestruck to warmly nostalgic. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** Note that transit and diner branches are firmly documented in text though thinly sampled visually, and the WPA-mural influence lives mainly in the flat poster branch — treat all branches as equally canonical.

**Avoid**

- Aged patina, sepia, film grain, classical scrollwork or figurative stone sculpture (period-accurate Art Deco).
- Marble, gold leaf, lacquer, mirrored walls, champagne glamour (Deco-Luxe territory).
- Mirror balls, glitter, laser grids, dance-floor haze (Disco Deco).
- Brass goggles, airships, gears, diesel grime, dystopian narrative props (Decopunk).
- Deliberately plain, unstyled vernacular signage and weathered strip-mall realism (Genericana).
- Muted earth tones, matte minimalism, grunge textures, organic Art Nouveau curves.
- Modern RGB gaming glow, LED video walls, holographic UI, teal-orange photographic grading.
- Heavy bloom or lens flare that dissolves geometric edges; a sleek sign-painter script is allowed only as a small secondary accent, never as the main voice.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Decoplex |
|---|---|
| Art Deco (1920s–30s) | Original deco shows period materials and patina — stone, bronze, symmetrical monumentality. Decoplex always looks like a 1990s reproduction: brighter neons, crisper edges, commercial subjects. |
| Deco-Luxe | Deco-Luxe is luxury interiors: marble, brass, mirrors, adult glamour. Decoplex is populist carpet, concessions and neon, playful rather than opulent. |
| Disco Deco | Disco Deco glitter, mirror balls and club lighting celebrate the dance floor. Decoplex neon is architectural line-work on theaters, diners and transit, not party sparkle. |
| Decopunk | Decopunk is narrative retro-futurism with gadgets, airships and dystopian grit. Decoplex is clean, real-world commercial design, optimistic and unironic. |
| Genericana | Genericana is unstyled, faded everyday commerce. Decoplex is aggressively styled — every surface carries deliberate deco vocabulary. |

When in doubt, ask: does this look like a 1990s movie theater's dream of 1938? If yes — neon lines, chrome trim, extruded type, sunburst honor — it is Decoplex.
