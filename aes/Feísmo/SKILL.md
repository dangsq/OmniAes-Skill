# Feísmo

Feísmo ("ugly-ism"), also called *Canibalismo Urbanístico* or *Maltrato da Paisaxe*, names the built environment produced by decades of unregulated, unplanned construction in Galicia, northwestern Spain. It is not a designed movement and never had practitioners: it is the accumulated visual result of self-built houses started on emigrant remittances and frozen mid-construction, of planning law that existed only on paper, and of a *ti vai facendo* ("just get on with it") pragmatism. It is documented as an aesthetic because its results are stubbornly consistent and instantly recognisable — a vernacular of interruption, clash and improvised completion. Officially defined as "buildings, infrastructures and other human works with a high degree of mediocrity that somehow degrade their environment," it stops at Galicia's administrative borders with Asturias, León and Portugal despite similar conditions next door.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The colour language is mineral and damp, with exactly one layer of human loudness. Base tones come from unfinished construction materials; the saturated note is always background vegetation or a small discordant finish, never a designed accent scheme.

Rule: grey plus brick-orange plus rust dominate the built mass; green occupies ground and background; any bright colour appears only as a small misplaced finish (tile band, door, shutter) or as vegetation. A sunlit image is permissible but the sky stays pale and the light hard-neutral, never golden.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the record documents them

1. **Unfinished concrete frame** — the archetype. Walls raised, upper floors open to the sky, iron rebar stubs protruding from column tops awaiting storeys that never came. Skeletal floors of concrete or rust-red steel posts and beams.
2. **Bare facade (Paries Nudum)** — exterior of exposed brick or unrendered cement block left for decades while the interior is fully inhabited; render applied over only part of a surface and stopped mid-wall.
3. **Height discontinuity and the medianera** — a two-storey stone house pressed against an eight-storey concrete block; the taller building's blank, unrendered party wall standing as a concrete cliff over a neighbour's roof. In rural settings the equivalent is misplacement: houses dropped on hillsides, riverbed edges or mid-field with no relation to terrain or paths.
4. **Mismatched materials and additive collision** — a brick-and-concrete upper floor cantilevered over a traditional granite ground floor; aluminium window frames set into granite walls; glazed interior tile on exterior walls; corrugated sheet, pallet wood and metal cladding patched onto stone.
5. **Heritage engulfed by infrastructure** — stone cruceiros (wayside crosses), chapels and hórreos crossed and obscured by utility poles, cables and makeshift canopies; rapid electrification layered over sacred or historic forms without integration.
6. **Ornamental excess (Torreón)** — mock-medieval towers, crenellations and castle-like stone detailing applied to otherwise unremarkable concrete homes, the ostentation of returning emigrants.
7. **Reciclaxe** — found objects pressed into service: bed frames and wooden pallets as field fencing, salvaged sheets as walls; functional improvisation with zero aesthetic integration.

### Materials and textures

Raw poured concrete with formwork stains and water streaks; unrendered cement block; exposed fired brick with mortar smears; weathered granite ashlar and rubble, moss and lichen in the joints; rusting corrugated rebar; galvanised chain-link and rusted tubular posts; aluminium joinery glowing too new against old stone; glazed ceramic tile; corrugated fibre-cement or sheet-metal roofing; green construction netting; damp asphalt and wet stone with a rain sheen. Texture contrast is the point: centuries-old eroded granite against raw, stained, brand-new-looking concrete.

### Recurring subjects

Self-built village houses frozen mid-construction; tower-house ostentation; urban apartment blocks rising incongruously over low-rise corners (the Blasphemia subtype); small chapels and cruceiros tangled in wires; hórreos and granite farmhouses sharing a frame with concrete shells; sheds, garages and shacks of patched cladding; narrow stone alleys squeezed by new walls; blank medianera cliffs; salvage fences; hillsides and field edges where a building simply arrives without context. Incidental street furniture — parked cars, wheelie bins, laundry lines, a dog kennel, occasional graffiti on a boundary wall — belongs in frame as lived context, not as subject.

### Composition and lighting

The register is vernacular documentary photography: eye-level snapshot framing, slightly awkward crops, deadpan centring, deep depth of field, no stylised lens behaviour. Utility poles and crossing cables routinely cut the frame. Clashing heights are shown by vertical stacking or street-level perspective down a row of mismatched neighbours. Lighting is overwhelmingly flat, diffuse Atlantic overcast with wet surfaces; a minority of records show hard daylight under cumulus sky. Dusk, night and golden hour are essentially absent from the documented record — treat them as outside the style's habitual range.

## Mood & Values

Feísmo feels deadpan, unintentionally comic and quietly melancholic at once. Its pathos is interruption, not ruin: these structures are inhabited, funded by savings, proud even — the Torreón's fake crenellations are ostentation, not decay. The style expresses individual initiative over collective planning, functional improvisation over aesthetic integration, and the *ti vai facendo* willingness to build without plans and finish without deadline. Read generously, it is an authentic archive of emigration, remittance economies and rural resilience; read critically, it is maltreatment of the landscape. Both readings share the same visual facts, and a good feísmo image holds them in suspension: affectionate, ironic, unsentimental, anti-postcard.

## Era & Origins

Rooted in the 1960s Spanish Miracle: rapid industrialisation, mass emigration to South America and Western Europe, and construction begun with remittance income then abandoned when owners settled abroad or funds ran out. Compounding causes were extremely dispersed rural settlement, chronically vague and unenforced planning legislation, the decline of traditional Galician building crafts, and returning emigrants importing foreign methods that were fused haphazardly with local ones. Despite its shabby appearance feísmo is not a product of poverty — many structures were expensive. Debate began early (noted by Galician intellectuals in 1918), formalised with architect-led rehabilitation efforts in 1994, became the largest complaint category at the Galician ombudsman by 2003, spawned anti-feísmo forums in 2004 and 2007, an ironic reclamation project in 2011, and a 2015 field classification of 34 subtypes. The finish colours that appear on tiled facades skew 1970s–1980s Spanish domestic; the photographic register is contemporary documentary.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. **Collide, never compose.** Every image should contain at least two incompatible building logics in contact: old granite vs raw concrete, sacred vs utility, finished vs frozen, ornamental vs makeshift.
2. **Incompleteness is a permanent state, not a moment.** Rebar stubs, open upper floors, render stopped mid-wall, scaffolding-less gaps. Nothing looks "under active construction"; it looks paused for decades.
3. **Inhabited, never abandoned.** Curtains, laundry, a lit window, a parked car, a door in use. The tension is between unfinished shell and ongoing domestic life.
4. **Mismatch over harmony.** Materials, heights, rooflines and window types must disagree. No unified facade, no consistent cornice line, no coordinated streetscape.
5. **Deadpan documentary register.** Flat overcast light, snapshot framing, deep focus, incidental clutter. The camera reports; it does not glorify or mourn.
6. **Palette discipline.** Concrete grey, brick orange, rust brown, mossy granite, off-white patch render, deep green ground. One small discordant glaze or paint accent maximum.
7. **Green backdrop.** The wet Atlantic landscape surrounds and indicts the structure; vegetation is lush, mossy, rain-fed.

**Variation axes**

- *Subject*: any of village house, urban block, tower-house, chapel or cruceiro, hórreo, shed/garage, alley, medianera wall, salvage fence, hillside or field-edge misplacement — plus subjects never photographed but obeying the rules (a bus stop roofed with salvaged sheet, a garage crowned with rebar stubs).
- *Composition*: street-level snapshot, corner view emphasising cantilever, alley one-point perspective, mid-distance elevation, elevated view over rooftops; awkward crops and pole-cut frames welcome.
- *Lighting*: predominantly flat overcast with wet sheen; optionally hard neutral daylight with cumulus; rain-misted air acceptable.
- *Colour handling*: muted mineral base with green ground; accent either as misplaced tile/paint or omitted entirely.
- *Mood*: deadpan, ironic, melancholic, affectionate — any blend, but never apocalyptic and never picturesque.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A feísmo photograph of a structure type that appears nowhere in the record is still feísmo if it obeys collision, incompleteness, inhabitation and palette.

**Avoid**

- Designed coherence: unified facades, matched materials, harmonious rooflines, minimalist purity, intentional composition.
- Abandonment and ruin porn: collapsed roofs, hollow windowless shells, vegetation reclaiming interiors, derelict machinery. Feísmo is unfinished or ad-hoc finished, and lived in.
- Monolithic board-formed concrete monumentality and heroic geometry (that is Brutalism).
- Snow, frost, birch trees, prefab panel estates (that is Slavic Winter).
- Dusk, night, neon, desaturated apocalyptic gloom, empty mournful streets (that is Doomer).
- Golden-hour warmth, saturated sunsets, cinematic grading, shallow-focus glamour.
- Postcard Galicia: misty hórreos or granite villages presented pristinely, without collision.
- Graffiti or street art as the subject; it may exist incidentally on a boundary wall only.
- Non-Atlantic settings: desert, Mediterranean dryness, tropical vegetation, alpine scenery.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Brutalism:** Brutalism is intentional, monolithic and complete — board-formed concrete, designed massing, often civic programme. Feísmo is accidental, additive and domestic; its concrete is thin-frame infill, mixed with brick, tile and granite, and its forms stop mid-gesture because funding stopped, not because a drawing said so.
- **Vs Doomer:** Doomer is a mood aesthetic — desaturated dusk, emptiness, despair of decline. Feísmo is daylight, populated, mundane and strangely confident; its emotional charge is incoherence and interrupted ambition, not hopelessness.
- **Vs Slavic Winter:** Slavic Winter keys on snow, frost and prefab housing estates in cold blue-white light. Feísmo is rain, not snow: moss, wet asphalt, deep green pasture, and self-built masonry and concrete rather than state prefab.
- **Vs Industrial Decay:** Industrial Decay photographs post-industrial machinery and factories rusting after use. Feísmo photographs domestic and agricultural construction never finished or improvised into use; its rust is rebar stubs, gates and chain-link, and its buildings are occupied, not post-mortem.

Where the record is thin, say so: Torreón ornamentation and Reciclaxe salvage fencing are firmly documented in text but rarer visually than unfinished frames; night-time and winter-snow imagery is essentially undocumented and should not be invented as typical.
