# Southern French Gothic (Gothique Méridional)

Also called Meridional, Occitan, Languedoc or Toulousian Gothic, this is the Gothic of the Midi: a regional variety that absorbed the pointed arch but refused the northern French obsession with skeletal walls, glass and sculpture. It emerged in Languedoc between the 12th and 14th centuries as the Catholic Church reasserted control after the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars, and it turns austerity itself into architecture — massive brick and stone halls, fortified crowns, and interiors emptied of everything except space, light and the preacher's voice. Scholars of the tradition call it a "militant art": a fortress built to win back believers by out-austering the heretics who criticized Church opulence.

## Visual Identity

**Palette.** An earth-tone triad dominates: brick terracotta and oxide red (roughly #9C4A2F to #C4703F), warm ochre and buff stone (#B98D5F to #D9C39A), and pale cream to whitewash (#E6DCC6 to #F5F1E6). Against this sits the contextual contrast of a saturated Mediterranean sky blue (#4A90D9 range) in exterior views. Interiors add umber shadow (#6B4A32 range) and pools of amber-gold light (#D9A05B range) falling on brick and plaster. A contained accent of lapis blue and gold leaf may appear on a painted apse vault, but never as an overall gilded scheme. The rule: warm mineral walls, white light or white plaster, red brick — color comes from the building material itself, not applied decoration.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them.**
1. *Austerity and unadorned wall planes*: few openings, no sculptural program, blank surfaces read as deliberate statement.
2. *Single-nave interiors under wide barrel vaults*: one immense hall, no aisles, vault spans among the widest in medieval Europe, built for preaching and large gatherings; ribbed vaults whose ribs radiate palm-like from a slender central column occur as a celebrated regional solution.
3. *Brickwork as ornament*: hand-molded brick around Toulouse, Montauban and Albi, laid in geometric bonds, diaper patterns and contrasting colored courses — decoration achieved by masonry, not carving.
4. *Fortified silhouettes*: crenellated battlements, machicolations and watchtowers crowning church walls, mostly symbolic assertions of Church power.
5. *Solid pier buttresses*: deep, flat, wall-hugging buttress piers, often stepped or crenellated at the top, replacing flying buttresses entirely.
6. *Two bell-tower types*: the octagonal "Toulouse" tower of progressively shrinking octagonal stories pierced by arched openings, and the wall belfry — a fortified gable wall pierced by bell arches on smaller churches.
7. *Romanesque memory*: rounded arches surviving beside pointed ones, massive wall thickness, simple vaulting.
8. *Secular carriers*: the same principles on mansions, colleges and palaces in Toulouse and Albi.

**Materials and textures.** Hand-made brick with unit-to-unit color drift and thick mortar joints; rough rubble and ochre ashlar stone where brick is absent; smooth plaster or whitewash on interior walls; weathered streaking and erosion at bases; low-pitched terracotta-tiled roofs mostly hidden behind parapets. Surfaces read matte, mineral, sun-baked — never polished, never painted over except at a contained apse.

**Recurring subjects.** Fortified church and cathedral exteriors seen from street or square, showing the long buttressed flank; vast bare naves with rows of chairs or benches running to a lit chancel; upward views into barrel or palm-rib vaults; bell towers against open sky; brick flanks rhythmically divided by buttress piers with tall narrow lancet windows between; courtyards and secular brick hôtels in the same language.

**Composition and lighting.** Exteriors favor three-quarter or side elevations that stress horizontal mass and the drum-like rhythm of buttresses; the tower is the only vertical accent in an otherwise grounded silhouette. Interiors favor axial one-point perspectives down the single hall, or radial upward views into the vault. Light is rationed: exteriors in hard high Mediterranean sun with crisp shadow edges and deep blue sky; interiors dim, with defined shafts and pools of warm light from a limited number of tall narrow windows, chandeliers or candles as point sources, and a brighter or gold-lit apse as the far focal point.

## Mood & Values

Simplicity, defense, militarism, Catholicism. The buildings feel like fortresses that decided to preach: grave, silent, monumental without triumphalism. Austerity is the argument — the Church answering Cathar criticism of its wealth by stripping itself bare while keeping overwhelming scale. The mood is fortified solemnity, clarity of purpose, and a Mediterranean calm of warm walls under bright sky.

## Era & Origins

Languedoc, 12th–14th centuries, in the Cathar territories and beyond (Haute-Garonne, Tarn, Tarn-et-Garonne, Ariège, Gers, Aude, Pyrénées-Orientales, Hérault). It forms after 1209–1229, as the Albigensian Crusade ends and the Church, promoted by figures such as the anti-Cathar bishop Folquet de Marselha, rebuilds its presence through an austere "militant art." It reacts simultaneously against Cathar accusations of opulence and against northern French Gothic extravagance, while inheriting persistent local Romanesque habits. It fades gradually as Rayonnant and Renaissance influences arrive and the Black Death disrupts production in the mid-14th century.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Mass over lace: compose the building as a defensible volume; buttresses are solid piers fused to the wall, never flying arches.
2. One room, one voice: interiors are a single wide hall under a barrel or palm-rib vault, oriented toward the preacher's platform.
3. Ornament by bond, not chisel: any pattern comes from brick coursing, diapering and color contrast, not sculpture.
4. Fortified crown: battlements, machicolations or a tower-like belfry cap the silhouette.
5. Light as a rationed resource: few openings; interiors lit by shafts and pools, exteriors by hard sun.
6. Earth palette under blue: brick red, ochre, cream; sky and shadow supply the only cool notes.
7. Vertical accent only through towers: octagonal tapering stories or a pierced wall belfry, never spires.
8. Austerity as meaning: emptiness of wall surface is intentional, not unfinished.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any carrier of these rules: church exterior or interior, bell tower, vault detail, cloister or courtyard, secular brick mansion, college or palace, street view with plane trees and modern life, or an empty sunlit flank. Composition may be axial, three-quarter, radial-upward, or a distant skyline massing. Lighting may range from noon glare to dusk amber to candlelit interior gloom. Color handling may lean brick-red dominant, pale-stone dominant, or whitewashed interior with amber light; a contained lapis-and-gold apse is permissible. Mood may run from fortress gravitas to preaching-hall clarity to monastic quiet. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Flying buttresses; forests of pinnacles, gargoyles, crockets; rose windows or fully glazed clerestories dominating a façade; sculpted portals with tympanum and statue-columns; aisled basilica sections with ambulatories as the primary read; northern white-limestone lace verticality; slender stone spires; Baroque gilding and curving altarpieces as the dominant register; half-timber, thatch or vernacular cottages; horseshoe arches and spolia columns; modern curtain walls, steel trusses or neon; and anything from the unrelated American "Southern Gothic" — decaying antebellum mansions, Spanish moss, humid decay.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell |
|---|---|
| Gothic (northern/classic) | North: flying buttresses, skeletal glazed walls, rose windows, statue portals, vertical lace. Midi: solid piers, blank brick walls, horizontal fortress mass, single nave. |
| Gothic Architecture (generic) | Generic expects pointed arch, rib vault and tracery as defaults; Midi subordinates them to barrel vaults, rounded-arch memory, wall mass and brick bonding. |
| Medieval (broad) | Medieval is an umbrella of timber frames, keeps, round-arch Romanesque and manuscript culture; Midi is specifically the brick-and-stone fortified preaching hall of Languedoc. |
| Romanesque | Shares mass and round arch, but Romanesque keeps small windows, dark aisled interiors and carved capitals/portals; Midi is taller, single-nave, wider-vaulted, brick-built, crenellated, with octagonal brick towers. |
| Visigothic Art | Visigothic is 6th–8th-century Iberian: tiny rural churches, horseshoe arches, reused columns. Midi is monumental urban brick and stone of the 12th–14th centuries at cathedral scale. |

When in doubt, apply the fortress test: if the church could be mistaken for a castle that happens to contain one enormous, bare, beautifully lit hall of brick, it is Southern French Gothic.
