# Hollywood Regency

Hollywood Regency — also called Regency Moderne — is the design language of Hollywood's Golden Era, born when the lavish interiors projected on silver screens became a template for real homes. It is more ornamental, eclectic and maximalist than Art Deco, built on bold color and contrast plus metallic and glass accents that signal opulence *and* comfort at once. The governing idea, in the style's own words, is that every detail conveys luxury and that people should look good inside the design — ideally in a satin bathrobe with a cocktail in hand. Rooms behave like soundstages: symmetrical, glossy, slightly theatrical, always camera-ready.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** Jewel tones carry the room, grounded by a graphic black-and-white floor and lifted by metallics. The canonical range: topaz/turquoise blue (deep teal lacquer `#0E7C86` through bright turquoise `#3FC0C4`), emerald and lacquer green (`#0B5D3B`–`#1E8A5A`, with glossed celadon/sage `#A9C4A0`–`#C2D8B4` as a softer member of the green family), amethyst pink (`#E7A6C0`–`#F6D7E3`) with a hotter fuchsia/magenta accent option (`#C2185B`–`#D81B60`), and citrine gold (`#C9A227`–`#E6C86E`). Silver, chrome and mirrored glass (`#C7C9CE`–`#EDEDF0`) are the constant neutral metal; black `#0B0B0B` and warm white `#F4F2EC` form checkerboard grounds; taupe/greige upholstery (`#8A7F72`–`#B4A99A`) frequently acts as a calm foil so the jewel tones and prints read as accents. A correct room may be shell-dark (charcoal or green lacquer walls) or shell-pale (silvered blue, white), but it never abandons the jewel-plus-metal-plus-graphic-ground triangle.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.**
1. **Black-and-white checkerboard flooring** — the style's most repeated ground plane, from entry halls to loggias.
2. **Sunburst wall mirror or clock in silver or gold** — named as one of the most consistent recurring fixtures. (Note: surviving image samples under-show sunbursts and Greek keys; the text ranks both as key motifs, so treat them as canonical even when a given view omits them.)
3. **Animal prints and animal figures** — zebra and leopard as pillows, rugs, upholstery accents, gilt-framed beast portraits.
4. **Palms** — as living plants, gilded palm sculptures, palm-frond artwork, and landscape plantings.
5. **Greek keys** — as trim, fretwork and bordering pattern.
6. **Silk and satin** — robes, upholstery sheen, drapery liquid gloss.
7. **Ornamental lighting** — crystal chandeliers and exaggerated, oversized floor lamps with metallic or gemstone detailing.
8. **Architectural signatures (exteriors/entries)** — mansard rooflines, Doric columns, leaded oval glass windows, and "Pullman door" entry treatments.

**Materials and textures.** Reflective and glossy above all: lacquered walls and furniture, mirrored panels, chrome and gold metal, polished stone, lucite. Against that sheen, sumptuous matte-plush counterpoints: velvet, fur, silk, deep button-tufted satin upholstery. Animal-hide patterns supply graphic texture; crystal and cut glass supply sparkle. Natural unfinished wood grain is absent by definition.

**Recurring subjects.** Interiors first: living rooms, dining rooms, bedrooms, libraries with lacquered walls, and glazed loggias/verandas with painted ceilings. Star-house exteriors and entries. Glamorous figures at leisure — reclining on tufted silk sofas in satin robes, posed as if for a studio portrait. Still-life accents: ginger jars, orchids, tray with cocktail books and flowers, gilt console objects.

**Composition and lighting.** Symmetry is structural: matched pairs of chairs flanking a sofa, paired lamps, axial centerlines, centered vignettes; one-point perspectives down checkerboard loggias are typical. Furniture silhouettes are sleek and slim-profiled but finished ornately. Lighting favors gloss-catching brightness — saturated daylight through French doors, warm chandelier and lamp glow at night, editorial flash that makes lacquer and chrome flare. Period images may carry a hand-tinted photogravure grain; contemporary work is crisp digital. Both are valid; the sheen is the constant.

## Mood & Values

Glamour, opulence, comfort, extravagance, drama — in that order of emphasis. The style values looking good over being austere: it is camp-confident, cocktail-hour easy, and unapologetically staged. Comfort matters as much as glitz (plush sectionals and fur throws sit inside the gloss), so the mood is luxurious *livability*, not museum formality.

## Era & Origins

Southern California, 1920s–1950s. The term is credited to Dorothy Draper, who with William "Billy" Haines dressed movie stars' homes in larger-than-life fashion; Draper's black-and-white checkerboard floors and Rococo/Baroque-derived scrollwork and plasterwork, adapted maximalist and less repetitive, set the interior grammar. Architect John Elgin Woolf, with Robert Koch, fixed the architectural vocabulary by fusing nineteenth-century French, Greek Revival and Modernist influences for clients like Cary Grant, Judy Garland and Katharine Hepburn. The style reacts against restraint itself: it is deliberately more ornamental and eclectic than Art Deco, and glossier than the natural-wood Mid-Century Modern that followed it. Contemporary designers (Kelly Wearstler, Miles Redd, Carleton Varney) keep a modern version alive.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Give every major surface a sheen story: lacquer, mirror, chrome, gilt, satin, polished stone or lucite. Matte-only rooms fail the style.
- Build contrast engines: jewel tone against black-white checker; metal against fur/velvet; slim silhouette against heavy ornament.
- Stage with symmetry: flanking pairs, axial centers, a sunburst or chandelier as focal anchor.
- Dose animal print and palms as accents and focal art, not as all-over wallpaper chaos.
- Treat the room as a set and any figure as its star: poses relaxed, fabrics liquid, light flattering.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any interior room type, an exterior/entry with mansard roof, Doric columns, leaded oval windows or Pullman door, a palm-filled terrace or garden, a figure vignette in satin, or a still life of gilt-and-crystal objects. Composition may range from strict axial symmetry to a reclining diagonal figure to a tight material close-up (tufting, checkerboard, chrome). Lighting may be bright saturated daylight, warm evening lamplight, or editorial flash; vintage print grain is optional. Color handling may run full jewel saturation, pale silvered shell with jewel accents, or dark lacquer shell with hot pink and gilt pops; checkerboard is characteristic but not mandatory in every frame. Mood may slide from serene silver-celadon glamour to playful feather-lamp camp to dramatic charcoal-lacquer theater. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.**
- Natural matte wood, exposed grain, oak/walnut mid-century warmth; rattan or cane used in an earthy boho way with macramé and terracotta.
- Minimalism, sparse negative space, Scandinavian or Japandi restraint.
- Art Deco's strict repeating geometry, stepped ziggurat forms, ebony-and-shagreen seriousness without eclectic ornament or camp color.
- Faithful historical Regency reproduction: mahogany, brass inlay, stiff neoclassical correctness without gloss, animal print or saturation.
- Industrial loft elements: exposed brick, ducts, Edison bulbs; farmhouse shiplap and distressed paint.
- Muted earth-tone palettes (terracotta, oatmeal, clay) as the dominant key; matte plaster calm.
- Cluttered boho maximalism: gallery-wall chaos, clashing folk patterns, no metallic discipline, no symmetry.
- Tiki or tropical kitsch without glamour (thatch, tikis, novelty polynesiana).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Maximalism:** Hollywood Regency is disciplined maximalism — symmetry, gloss, a metallic thread and jewel palette hold the excess together. Generic Maximalism tolerates any palette, clashing patterns, matte surfaces and asymmetrical gallery clutter.
- **Vs Old Hollywood:** Old Hollywood is the broader glamour myth — black-and-white photography, streamline Moderne nightclubs, furs and marquees — and can be monochrome. Hollywood Regency is specifically the saturated interior/architectural language: checkerboard, sunburst, palms, Greek key, lacquer.
- **Vs Regency (Regencycore):** historical Regency is early-19th-century English neoclassicism — mahogany, striped upholstery, subdued correctness. Hollywood Regency borrows the name and some silhouette but adds chrome, lacquer, animal print and camp color.
- **Vs Art Deco:** Deco is geometric, repetitive, streamlined, veneer-driven; its sunbursts are radiating geometry inside strict order. Hollywood Regency is more eclectic and ornamental — Rococo/Baroque scrollwork, checkerboard floors, palms, florals, pink — and explicitly less repetitive.
- **Vs Hellenic:** Hellenic revives Greek antiquity in matte marble, columns and pediments with austere blue-and-white restraint. Hollywood Regency uses Greek keys and Doric columns only as glossy accents inside jewel-toned, mirror-lit rooms full of silk and animal print.
