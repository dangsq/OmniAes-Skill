# Appalachian Gothic

Appalachian Gothic is the aesthetic of the American Appalachian mountain chain and its surrounding region, from New York to Alabama: the culture, landscape and media of a place settled in the 1700s by Scottish, Irish, German and Scandinavian pioneers, industrialized by coal and lumber, impoverished by the Depression, and haunted — literally in its folklore, visually in its leftovers — ever since. Recently popularized on TikTok and in horror media (podcasts, games like *Kentucky Route Zero*), it descends literarily from Washington Irving's "Rip Van Winkle" and photographically from mid-to-late-20th-century documentary color work: faded film chroma, straight-on framings of rail towns and hillside houses, mountains standing behind everything like a debt. Unlike most gothics, it does not need night; its dread sits comfortably in an overcast noon, in fog pooling in a hollow, in a billboard announcing a layoff above a brown field.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette (rule, not recipe).** The style lives on a triad of canopy green, mineral gray and iron-oxide rust, with blue admitted only as distance or cloth:

| Role | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Canopy & kudzu green | `#2E4630` → `#5C7A48`, hazed to `#6E8B74` | Deep summer leaf mass; ridges desaturate to blue-green with distance |
| Mineral gray | sky `#C6CAC6`, fog `#DDE0DC`, gravel/silvered wood `#8E8E86`, coal shadow `#26282A` | Overcast sky and mist are structural colors, not background |
| Rust & brick brown | `#6E4A2F` → `#9C5A2E`, barn red `#7A3B2E` | Oxidized steel lattice, peeling paint, old brick; the only sanctioned warm family |
| Reserved blue | ridge haze `#5A7A96`, summer sky `#7FA6C4`, denim `#4F6A85` | Sky, far ridgelines, work cloth — never dominant |
| Rare point warmth | lantern amber `#D89A3E`, headlamp white-gold | A single flame or lamp bead in darkness |

Warm hues enter as oxidation or flame, never as sunlight glamour. Saturation stays film-faded; chroma peaks in foliage and rust, never in signage or clothing.

**Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence.** (1) Forested mountainous terrain, ridges layered and sometimes half-covered by mist or fog. (2) Supernatural occurrences — grotesque monsters, ghosts, sightings; the text's headline motif, which in the photographic record survives as implication: fog shapes, darkened treelines, an unlit window that reads as watched. (3) Small mountain towns and small dilapidated houses scattered on mountaintops, reached by curving roads, often with old rusting cars parked nearby. (4) Coal mines and their residue: abandoned mine works, tipples, hard hats with lamps, pollution scars, conservation replanting. (5) Log cabins — chinked-corner, Pennsylvania Dutch and Scandinavian lineage — plus run-down barns and abandoned homesteads carrying the stories of boom and bust. (6) Split-rail fences. (7) Kudzu swallowing edges and structures. (8) Old-fashioned lanterns. (9) Untouched thick forest, "wild" second growth reclaiming cut land. Documentary extensions attested in the image record: railroad trestles and tracks cutting diagonals through valleys, utility poles with crossing wires, gravel yards and creek beds of pale stone, roadside billboards of economic decline, and archival black-and-white crowds of working men in hats before fogged mountains.

**Materials and textures.** Oxidized steel lattice and corrugated tin; hand-hewn logs with mud chinking; checked, silvered, peeling wood; wet creek stone and slate; coal dust on everything; kudzu mats and unchecked brush; flannel wool, denim, knitted wool socks; faded color-film grain and slight print cast; damp air you can almost see on the lens.

**Recurring subjects.** Landscape as protagonist with the human figure small, turned away, or absent — presence asserted through traces: a parked car, a porch light, a fence line, a sign. When people appear, they wear the style's workwear-and-outsider fashion: flannels, overalls, cargo pants, distressed jeans, work or hiking boots, wool socks, long coats, knitted sweaters, hard hats with lamps, with a punk/metal edge rather than heritage-costume neatness. Still-life subjects: lanterns, banjos and fiddles (the African American-derived backbone of the music), ballad sheets, coal lumps, mine lamps.

**Composition and lighting.** Vantages from the roadbed, trackside, or a roadside pull-off: eye-level, straight-on, documentary. Infrastructure supplies the geometry — trestles climbing a slope, rails converging, wires crossing the frame — while ridges stack into haze behind. Horizons sit high or are cropped by hillside; verticals emphasize slope and trestle height. Default light is flat overcast diffusion; alternatives are fog pooled in hollows at dawn, blue dusk, or true night lit by a single lantern or porch bulb. Hard noon haze in high summer is acceptable; glamour is not.

## Mood & Values

Hard work, independence, conservatism — the pioneer spirit of isolated communities that had to suffice themselves. Dignity coexists with poverty: the dilapidated house is a home, not a prop. The mood is elegiac and watchful, haunted by economic booms and busts (lumber, coal, the Depression) and by folklore that treats the supernatural as neighborly fact rather than intrusion. Ballad melancholy — bluegrass banjo, fiddle, moody alt-folk — underwrites it: sorrow sung plainly, dread stated plainly, beauty refused picturesque treatment.

## Era & Origins

Roots in the 1700s–1800s settlement cultures (Scots-Irish, German Pennsylvania Dutch, Scandinavian log craft; African American musical lineage), and in literary regional gothic from Irving onward. The visual vocabulary was fixed by the industrial era's leftovers — mines, trestles, company towns — and by Depression-era poverty, then recorded in 20th-century documentary photography, including 1970s color film of coal towns. The named aesthetic coalesced recently on TikTok and in contemporary horror podcasts and games, reacting simultaneously against postcard mountain tourism and against the "hillbilly" stereotype, reclaiming decay as witness.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Land first: rounded, old, fully forested ridges and hollows; human works sit *in* the fold of the land, never atop a conquered summit.
2. Decay is documentary: rust, sag, peeling paint and abandoned works read as economic history, not horror set-dressing.
3. Keep the triad: green mass, gray air, rust iron; blue only at distance or in denim; one flame-warm accent at most.
4. Light flat or fogged; if night, one lantern, porch bulb or headlamp against true dark.
5. People small or absent; tell presence through traces (rusted car, fence, laundry line, billboard, mine lamp).
6. Dread ambient: the supernatural implied by fog, silhouette and darkness; overt grotesques allowed but folkloric and rare, never sleek.
7. Carry film memory: muted chroma, slight grain, straight-on framing.

**Variation axes.** Subject: ridge landscape, town street, mountaintop shack, mine mouth, creek crossing, cabin interior, workwear portrait, lantern/banjo still life, roadside sign, archival crowd. Composition: wide documentary vista, vertical infrastructure climb, intimate object close-up, square archival crop. Lighting: overcast noon, fogged dawn, blue dusk, lantern night, flat archival flash. Color handling: faded 1970s film chroma, muted modern digital, saturated summer greens under gray sky, or black-and-white archival. Mood: elegiac, ominous, tender, defiant. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Neon or candy saturation; golden-hour glamour and lens-flare sun; jagged snowy alpine peaks; pristine wilderness with zero human trace; glossy CGI-monster horror; manicured lawns and new suburbia; chrome-and-glass urbanism; cozy hygge cabin warmth without precariousness; Halloween kitsch (skeletons, pumpkins); steampunk lantern excess; catalog-shiny outdoor gear; tropical or desert flora; Mediterranean architecture; high-key studio light; vaporwave or synthwave palettes.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Dark Naturalism:** nature sublime and menacing with no human economic residue; colder, placeless palette. Appalachian Gothic always keeps a human trace — rust, fence, mine, house — and a regional folklore.
- **Adventurecore:** aspirational recreation — crisp alpine light, bright gear, summit joy, human agency. Here gear is worn workwear, light is overcast, and the mood is aftermath, not achievement.
- **After Hours:** nocturnal urban/suburban liminality — neon, sodium vapor, parking lots. Appalachian night is rural dark with a single flame; its default register is daylight gloom, and any roadside sign stands among trees and ridges, not storefronts.
- **Cabincore:** curated cozy retreat — cocoa, knit blankets, warm interior glow. The Appalachian cabin is chinked, weathered, possibly abandoned; warmth is precarious, and dread or decay must leak in.
- **Cryptidcore:** creature-lore paraphernalia — investigation boards, red string, cryptid merchandise, playful conspiracy. Appalachian Gothic treats the supernatural as solemn lived folklore glimpsed in the landscape, not as collectible mythos.
- **Southern Gothic (near kin):** distinguish by topography and industry — rounded forested ridges and coal/lumber residue versus flat agrarian South and plantation decay.
