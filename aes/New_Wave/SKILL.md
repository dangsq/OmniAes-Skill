# New Wave

New Wave is the visual language of pop's turn toward the artificial. Born in the late 1970s in the UK and US as a pop-oriented evolution of punk, it kept punk's refusal of corporate rock but swapped raw aggression for "synthetic modernity": a celebration of the plastic, the geometric and the deliberately fake. Its natural habitats are the record sleeve, the concert poster and the early MTV frame. It synthesizes Bauhaus geometry, Constructivist diagonals and Pop Art boldness into a commercial package, then sabotages that package with irony — skewing the grid, misregistering the print, dyeing the hair neon blue. Everything in the style says: this is a product of a media-saturated world, and it is proud of it.

## Visual Identity

### Colour

The palette is a rule, not a swatch list: **fluorescent accents over a neutral or void ground, with tertiary tones doing the work primaries used to do.** Expect hot pink / magenta (roughly `#FF1F8E`–`#E5006E`), electric yellow (`#F2E900`–`#FFD500`), cyan (`#00CFE8`–`#00A7C4`), plus tertiary purple (`#6E2FBF`–`#9A4FD8`), orange (`#FF6B00`–`#FF8C1A`) and acid green (`#A6E22E`–`#C6F000`). These sit on black starfield voids (`#000000`–`#0A0A0A`), flat paper grounds (off-white, pale mint, grey), or photocopier greys (`#8A8A8A`–`#D8D8D8`). Pink is used deliberately as a signifier of artificiality, stripped of any gendered softness; it appears in equal force on men, machines and typography. Saturation is high and edges are hard — color behaves like printed ink or molded plastic, never like atmosphere. A legitimate minority wing is strict high-contrast black-and-white (the photocopier extreme of the same logic), where the "fluorescence" is replaced by brutal tonal contrast.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Floating geometry** — triangles, circles, squares and discs suspended in space or scattered in erratic, non-hierarchical patterns that mirror the music's jerky rhythms. Concentric orbit-discs and target rings are typical.
2. **Atomic-age retro-futurism** — 1950s Eisenhower-era kitsch recontextualized through satire: atom symbols, rockets, starfields, boomerang shapes, chrome fins, kitschy suburbia, science-diagram linework. The optimism of 1950s Americana collides with 1980s neurosis.
3. **The dysfunctional grid** — a modernist layout that has been tilted, layered illogically, cropped wrong or repeated until it misbehaves; skewed angles and stacked planes that defy perspective.
4. **Low-fidelity reproduction artifacts** — photocopier grain, harsh halftone, moiré and optical interference patterns from adhesive drafting films, visible misregistration.
5. **Mixed typography** — clean geometric sans-serif set against decorative script, stencil lettering or hand-drawn type; tiny cryptic data blocks, track lists and credit columns treated as pure texture; color-code strips standing in for words.
6. **The synthetic silhouette** — mod suit pushed to futuristic exaggeration: oversized squared shoulders ("big suit"), skinny tie, drainpipe trousers, buttoned shirt, thick-rimmed or angular sunglasses. Androgyny is default; the persona is intellectual-neurotic, not sexy.
7. **Inorganic hair and theatrical makeup** — neon blue, pink or orange dye; teased geometric wedges and asymmetrical cuts defying natural growth; makeup applied as harsh geometric planes, the face used as a canvas.
8. **Plastic objects and media hardware** — fluorescent vinyl, synthesizers, rotary phones, television sets, glitching video interfaces, mannequin-smooth skin.
9. **High-art appropriation** — a classical painting or academic image dropped intact into a stark graphic frame with minimal code-like markings; the "riotous mix" of high and low culture.

### Materials and textures

Airbrushed smoothness (gradient glows with no brushstroke), flat screen-print ink, glossy vinyl, synthetic fabric with a slight sheen, photocopier toner grain, halftone dot, moiré interference, starfield speckle, grid mesh and graph paper. Nothing is handcrafted, woven, weathered or organic. Surfaces either gleam like molded plastic or degrade like a third-generation xerox — there is no middle register of natural texture.

### Recurring subjects

Band portraits and group line-ups posed stiffly or robotically; solo androgynous figures against voids; object posters (a single phone, a single suit, a single flower painting); satirical suburban and space-age tableaus; club and film-still figures with spray-paint makeup; glitchy television personas. Bodies are treated as design elements: repeated in modules, tinted in flat monochrome blocks, or cropped by geometric shapes.

### Composition and lighting

Composition starts from modernist order and then breaks it: modules on a grid that slips, radial line-bursts converging behind a figure, diagonal rules slicing the frame, collage layers stacked without logical depth, figures floating in black space with no ground plane. Type is compositional, not captioning. Lighting is studio-bound and artificial: flat frontal studio light, hard flash with minimal shadow modelling, airbrushed radial glow, neon tube line-bursts, or high-key monochrome contrast. Note honestly: the style's lighting conventions survive mainly through sleeve and poster reproduction, so flatness and frontality dominate the evidence; naturalistic window light, golden hour and volumetric atmosphere are foreign to it.

## Mood & Values

Irony, camp and "authentic inauthenticity." The style values modernity over soul, artifice over nature, the plastic over the organic. Its personas are hollow-souled suburbanites and robotic observers — deadpan, twitchy, knowingly fake. There is a nervous, herky-jerky energy even in still images: angles instead of curves, staccato repetition instead of flow. It is art-school cool with a pop smile: intellectual enough to deconstruct the grid, cheeky enough to sell the result on a poster. Grief, sincerity and earthiness are exactly what it refuses.

## Era & Origins

Late 1970s, United Kingdom and United States, spreading internationally; peak visibility in the early-to-mid 1980s via MTV, vinyl and posters; commercial decline by the late 1980s, with revivals in the 1990s and 2000s. It reacts against two things at once: the denim-heavy, blues-rooted "organic" rock of the 1970s, and the clean corporate Swiss International Typographic Style whose grids New Wave designers — notably in Manchester and London — deliberately rendered dysfunctional. Regional variants (Neue Deutsche Welle, Movida Madrileña, Japanese technopop) share the philosophy of irony and modernity while differing in instrumentation and surface.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Make it look manufactured: plastic, ink, toner, airbrush — never organic material.
2. Build a modernist grid, then break it: skew, misregister, layer illogically, repeat until it stutters.
3. Light fluorescent color against a void or flat ground; let tertiary tones carry the harmony.
4. Quote the 1950s atomic age through a satirical 1980s lens: atoms, orbits, rockets, kitsch suburbia, rendered neurotic.
5. Dress figures in angular synthetic silhouettes; pose them stiff, robotic, deadpan or over-theatrical — never lounging or groove-swirling.
6. Treat type as image: sans-serif plus script or stencil, tiny data blocks, color-code strips.
7. Add one layer of analog degradation: photocopy grain, halftone, moiré, misprint offset.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may range from band portraiture, group line-ups and androgynous solo figures to pure object posters, still-life appropriation, suburban satire, robot/mannequin studies, club scenes and glitch-television characters. Composition may range from a single figure centered on a void, to radial line-bursts, modular repetition, floating shape-fields, or dense collage stacks. Lighting may range from flat studio frontality and hard flash to airbrushed glow, neon burst, or strict high-contrast monochrome. Color handling may run full fluorescent-on-black, tertiary-on-pastel-paper, or near-monochrome with one acid accent. Mood may slide from campy and quirky to cold and robotic to sleek early-MTV pop. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A still life, a cityscape, a robot or an abstract poster can all be perfectly New Wave with no band and no sleeve in sight.

**Avoid.** Earth tones, wood grain, foliage-as-texture and any hippie "natural/organic" warmth; denim, flannel, unkempt hair and 1970s rock scruff; blues-rock sincerity and soulful expressiveness; a properly functioning clean Swiss grid; solemn Bauhaus primary-triad modernism; Victorian mourning dress, candlelight and romantic gloom (that is Goth); chrome-and-sunset glamour, sports cars and Mediterranean sensuality (Italo Disco); patterned laminate furniture and terrazzo surfaces as the subject (Memphis); frills, satin, piratical dandyism and lush yearning (New Romantic); grunge distress, lens flare, HDR glow, soft bokeh and any contemporary digital-vector perfection without analog print artifact.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Electroclash** (2000s revival): colder, minimal, digital — crisp vector neon on black, club-sexual poses, glitch as clean pixel effect. New Wave keeps analog artifacts (xerox grain, airbrush, misprint), tertiary playfulness and 1950s kitsch quotation; electroclash drops the kitsch and the camp.
- **Goth**: black as mourning, not as void; backcombed black hair, velvet, silver religious iconography, romantic sorrow. New Wave uses black only as a ground for fluorescent color and answers sorrow with irony.
- **Italo Disco**: glossy airbrush too, but in service of sunset gradients, chrome, luxury and sensual romance; grids function, typography is sleek italic. New Wave's glow is neon-tube and starfield, its mood neurotic and satirical, its grid broken.
- **Memphis Design**: shares tertiary color and geometry but applies them to furniture, laminates and interiors as surface pattern (bacteria prints, terrazzo, ash-wood veneer); no music iconography, no photocopy grain, no atomic-age satire, and pastel confetti rather than fluorescent-on-void.
- **New Romantic**: lush, frilled, satin-and-eyeliner dandyism with romantic longing; the silhouette is soft, layered, historicist. New Wave's silhouette is angular, synthetic, mod-derived; its affect is deadpan irony. (Historically MTV blurred the two — discriminate on silhouette and mood, not on band name.)
