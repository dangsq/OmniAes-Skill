# De Stijl (Neoplasticism)

De Stijl ("The Style"), also called Neoplasticism, was founded in Leiden in 1917 around Theo van Doesburg and the journal of the same name, with Piet Mondrian as its chief theorist and Gerrit Rietveld as its builder in three dimensions. It is a program of total reduction: art stripped to straight horizontal and vertical lines, right angles, and rectangular planes of flat color, because those elements alone were believed to express the universal dualities (spirit and matter, vertical and horizontal) behind visible reality. Born during the First World War, it answers chaos with order: pure abstraction as a route to spiritual harmony, social renewal, and the eventual dissolution of art into everyday life. The movement fractured in the late 1920s and ended with van Doesburg's death in 1931, but its grammar survived in the Bauhaus, the International Style of architecture, modern graphic design, and endless revivals — color-blocked dresses, glassware, sneakers, stage sets — that borrow Mondrian's grid for applied design.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule, before any example: **three primaries plus three non-colors, nothing else.** Planes are saturated but not neon; primaries read as paint, not pigment powder. Observed range:

| Role | Hex range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Red | `#C8102E` – `#E0301E` | vermilion to deep signal red; usually the dominant mass when present |
| Blue | `#1B3F94` – `#2A4BC8` | cobalt to ultramarine; cool counterweight |
| Yellow | `#F2D300` – `#FFD900` | cadmium/lemon; often the smallest accent |
| White | `#F7F4EC` – `#FFFFFF` | off-white to pure white; active field, never mere background |
| Black | `#0B0B0B` – `#1C1A17` | soft, paintly black; used for line-bars and occasionally as ground |
| Gray | `#BFBFBF` – `#D8D8D8` | neutral plane color, subordinate to primaries |

Documented peripheral variation: early works (c. 1917, before the doctrine hardened) admit ochre/brown-orange (`#A65A28`–`#C07830`) and rose-madder (`#B0455C`–`#C4546E`) squares alongside cobalt; a few peripheral compositions and revival objects mute toward sage, slate and mustard. Treat these as the outer edge of the variation space, not the core: a canonical image lives in primaries + non-colors. Proportions vary freely — white-dominant fields with sparse accents, one overwhelming red mass against white, or (rarely) a black ground carrying scattered primaries.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the doctrine states them

1. **Straight horizontal and vertical lines only**, rendered as solid black bars of unequal width — lines are planes with their own thickness, not hairline strokes. They may run edge-to-edge or stop in T-junctions and crosses.
2. **Right angles and rectangular/square planes of flat color**, each plane a single uniform fill.
3. **Asymmetry and dynamic equilibrium**: balance achieved by unequal weights in unequal positions — a large color mass offset by small distant accents — never mirror symmetry.
4. **Non-colors as active space**: expansive white (or gray) fields that carry the composition as much as the colored planes do.
5. **The grammar translated into three dimensions**: open-structure architecture and furniture with interlocking slabs, linear posts and rails read as black lines, sliding walls, no decoration, no closed room layout (Rietveld's Schröder House, Red and Blue Chair).
6. **Integration of art and life / revival applications**: the grid applied to dresses, tumblers, footwear, interiors, stage backdrops — any object decomposed into orthogonal color planes.
7. **The discrete-element variant** (early compositions): separate axis-aligned squares and short black dashes scattered across an open ground, balanced by spacing alone rather than by a continuous grid. Both the continuous traversing-grid variant and this floating-element variant are canonical.

### Materials and textures

Matte oil paint laid flat on canvas, with faint hand-brush drag, slightly soft hand-ruled line edges, and fine craquelure on older surfaces — precision of intention, not machine precision of edge. In three dimensions: lacquered or painted wood and steel, flat satin finishes; glass, silk crêpe and leather appear in revival objects. Surfaces always read uniform and unmodulated within each plane; texture lives in the substrate, never in the color.

### Recurring subjects

Abstract composition itself is the primary subject. Beyond painting: house exteriors and interiors of interlocking white planes and linear members; chairs and furniture as colored line-and-plane constructions; fashion garments as wearable grids; household objects (glassware, sneakers) carrying the palette; interiors and stage sets where walls, floors and props become planes. A human figure may appear only in the revival/applied mode, stylized and color-blocked against or within the grid — never modeled naturalistically.

### Composition and lighting

Composition is relational and edge-aware: accents hug corners and margins, lines divide the field into unequal rectangles, scale contrast does the dramatic work. No perspective illusion, no vanishing point, no depth cue in graphic compositions; in photographs of buildings and objects, camera perspective is inevitable but the built forms themselves remain strictly orthogonal slabs and bars. Lighting is flat and even: paintings declare no light source at all; three-dimensional scenes use diffuse daylight or neutral studio light, matte surfaces, at most soft ambient shadow. No chiaroscuro, no golden hour, no specular drama.

## Mood & Values

Austere serenity, monastic clarity, utopian optimism. The style expresses peace and order as moral goods — a visual antidote to war and chaos — and carries a near-spiritual belief that reduction reveals truth behind appearance. It feels contemplative and balanced rather than aggressive; even its pop revivals (fashion, music video) borrow that calm grid while adding playfulness. Values encoded: pure abstraction, universality, order, reduction to essentials, integration of art and life.

## Era & Origins

Leiden, Netherlands, 1917; active until 1931. Founders and core figures: Theo van Doesburg, Piet Mondrian, Gerrit Rietveld, Bart van der Leck, Vilmos Huszár, Georges Vantongerloo. It reacts against naturalistic representation, academic ornament, and the half-abstraction of Cubism, and against the disorder of its wartime moment; it "starts from zero" in architecture. Its afterlife runs from mid-century modernism through 1960s fashion revivals to contemporary product and set design.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Every edge in the composition is 0° or 90°. No exceptions in graphic mode; in 3Dmode the built geometry obeys this even if the camera does not.
2. Each plane is one flat, uniform color fill. No gradient, no shading, no modeled volume.
3. Palette discipline: red, blue, yellow + white, black, gray. Ochre/rose only as an early-work flavor; muted sage/mustard only as a rare revival flavor.
4. Balance asymmetrically: unequal masses in unequal positions, held in dynamic equilibrium by spacing and scale contrast.
5. Treat white (or another non-color) as a composed plane, not leftover background.
6. Draw lines as solid bars with deliberate, varying widths; let them cross, T-joint, run off-edge, or stop short.
7. Reduce: delete narrative, ornament, texture-effects and any element that does not earn its place as line or plane.
8. Translate, don't decorate: when the subject is an object, building or garment, decompose its structure into orthogonal planes and linear members rather than printing a grid onto it.
9. Light flatly: even, diffuse, shadowless or near-shadowless illumination; matte surfaces.
10. Allow hand-made imperfection: slightly soft line edges, subtle brush drag, fine craquelure on painted surfaces.

**Variation axes.** Subject: abstract canvas → house exterior → open-plan interior → chair → dress → glassware → footwear → stage set → poster field → stylized figure within a grid. Composition: continuous traversing grid ↔ discrete floating squares and dashes; dense ↔ sparse; white-dominant ↔ black-ground; one dominant color mass ↔ distributed equal accents. Color handling: pure primaries ↔ early ochre/rose ↔ lightly muted revival tones; any proportion of non-color field. Scale and medium: monumental wall ↔ tiny object. Lighting: absolutely flat (graphic) ↔ diffuse daylight or neutral studio (spatial). Mood: contemplative austerity ↔ bright pop playfulness in revival mode. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Curves, circles, arcs, diagonals, rotated or tilted squares; perspective depth, vanishing points and illusionistic space in graphic compositions; gradients, shading, chiaroscuro, glossy specular highlights; expressive brushwork, impasto, drips, gestural marks; green, orange, purple, pastels, neons, metallics as plane colors; ornament, floral or filigree pattern, decorative serif or hand-lettered type; centered mirror symmetry; clutter and narrative detail; naturalistic figures, faces, landscapes or still life; machinery, photomontage and typographic dominance; diagonal agitprop energy; free-floating rotated geometry implying cosmic space.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Bauhaus** shares primaries and geometry but adds tubular-steel curves, workshop craft objects, sans-serif typography, photomontage and a playfully polychrome wing (Klee, Kandinsky); its images show functionalist industrial objects and often people at work. De Stijl never curves, never depicts machinery or type-driven layouts, and locks its palette to primaries plus non-colors with a spiritual rather than pedagogical program.
- **Constructivism** runs on diagonals: tilted axes, dynamic tilt, industrial materials, photomontage, red-black agitprop graphics with typography as a visual weapon. De Stijl is orthogonal, contemplative and apolitical in tone; its tension is balanced equilibrium, not revolutionary motion.
- **International Typographic Style (Swiss)** also uses grids and asymmetry, but the grid serves communication: sans-serif type, photography and legible white space carry content. In De Stijl the grid and color planes *are* the content; there is no typographic hierarchy and no photographic subject.
- **Modernism** broadly (white villas, free plans, concrete, chrome, curving stairs, ribbon windows, sun-cast shadows) is the umbrella; De Stijl is its strictest orthogonal subset. Curved forms, pilotis, bare concrete and broad material palettes signal general Modernism, not De Stijl.
- **Suprematism** floats rotated, tilted geometric bodies in an implied cosmic space, including white-on-white near-nothingness. De Stijl planes stay axis-aligned and relationally bound to the picture plane's edges and grid; nothing rotates, nothing drifts in depth. (Historically, van Doesburg's later introduction of the diagonal was a breakaway from Neoplasticism, not part of its canon — diagonals remain a disqualifier.)
