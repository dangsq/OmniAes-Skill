# Minimalism

Minimalism is subtraction made visible: a lack of clutter and unnecessary detail, pursued first as an art movement and later as a whole lifestyle. Its visual logic is not "few things" but "only necessary things" — every surviving element earns its place, and the empty space around it is treated as compositional material rather than leftover background. Solid colors dominate, patterns stay simple or absent, and color itself tends toward the muted end of any hue family. The style carries an ethical charge: quality over quantity, decluttering, attention to materials and textures, anti-materialism, freedom, environmentalism.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)

Any hue is permissible *if* it behaves: desaturated, flat, and applied as a solid field. In practice the palette clusters into five families, and a given image usually draws from one or two of them plus at most one accent:

| Family | Range | Typical hex band |
|---|---|---|
| Whites & off-whites | gallery white, cream, paper | `#FFFFFF` – `#F2EFE9` |
| Neutrals | beige, greige, taupe, warm grey | `#D8CFC3`, `#C9C4BC`, `#B7A99A` |
| Greys to charcoal | light grey planes to matte black anchors | `#E6E6E6` – `#6E6E6E`; `#2B2B2B` – `#141414` |
| Muted pastels (as seamless grounds) | powder blue, blush, lilac, mint, pale pink | `#B9D2DC`, `#EFC9CC`, `#CBBAD9`, `#CFE2D9` |
| Earthy ceramic tones | terracotta, olive, cognac leather, cream glaze | `#C0674A`, `#6F7048`, `#A9743F`, `#EFE6D8` |

Saturated primaries (red ≈ `#C8102E`, yellow ≈ `#F2C200`, blue ≈ `#0B57C4`) appear only in the De Stijl / Suprematism / Purism lineage: as flat rectangular blocks locked into a grid on a white field — facade panels, poster geometry — never as an overall atmosphere, never blended. Gradients, neons and busy multi-hue mixes are outside the style.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them

1. **Unadorned architectural volume.** White or concrete cubic houses, flat roofs, smooth rendered planes, facades with little or no ornament, long quiet corridors holding at most one framed work. The tradition reaches back to the pyramids' original smooth limestone skin and to plain Byzantine church exteriors; its modern form is the white cube house and the bare concrete shell.
2. **The solid color field.** A wall, a seamless studio ground, a painted plane — color arriving as area rather than object. Complex pattern is excluded by definition; a grid or a single stripe is the maximum complexity allowed.
3. **The everyday object elevated to specimen.** Ceramic vessels (Japanese tea bowls, glazed earthenware jugs), matte bottles, a kettle and mug, a chair reduced to a thin tubular frame and one honest panel. Museum-object framing: isolated, frontal, evenly lit.
4. **The quiet wardrobe.** Solid-color t-shirts (typically white or grey), collared and button-up shirts, belts, khaki or black pants, jeans, long unstructured coats in non-colors. No logos, no loud color, no statement print.
5. **Geometric grid and typographic restraint.** The Plakatstil and International Typographic Style inheritance: limited inks, small functional type, generous margins, modules aligned to an implicit grid; repetition of identical units (cubes, planters, keycaps) instead of variety.
6. **The decluttered interior.** Bare tabletops, one shelf, ordered storage, white bedding, empty counters — the lifestyle branch's rejection of consumerist accumulation.
7. **Image-evidenced extensions, thinly named in text:** a single plant as the lone organic note; pastel seamless-ground product still lifes; one crisp shadow cast across a flat colored plane. The text sanctions these only through "solid colors" and the decluttering values, so treat them as permitted variations, not core doctrine.

### Materials and textures

Matte over gloss. Raw or smoothed concrete, smooth plaster, pale wood, tubular steel, unglazed or softly glazed ceramic, cotton, linen, wool, plain leather. Texture is present but quiet: the value "attention to materials and textures" means material honesty — you should be able to name what something is made of by looking — not decorative surface pattern.

### Composition and lighting

Frontal, near-orthographic viewpoints; centered specimen or deliberately off-center subject with the void doing the balancing. At least half the frame reads as empty. Horizon low or absent; edges parallel to the frame; cropping clean. Lighting is even and non-theatrical: soft diffuse window or north daylight with feathered shadows; flat studio/museum light; or hard direct sun producing exactly one crisp shadow on a flat plane; occasionally dusk ambient with a single warm lit window. Highlights never blow out, chiaroscuro never dramatizes.

## Mood & Values

Calm, order, intentionality, quiet confidence. The image should feel like an exhale: room to think, nothing competing for attention. Beneath the look sit declared values — quality over quantity, decluttering, prioritizing what matters, anti-materialism, freedom, environmentalism — with a Buddhist undercurrent (aparigraha, non-possessiveness; mindfulness toward what one already owns). Restraint here is ethics, not merely taste; excess reads as moral noise as well as visual noise.

## Era & Origins

As an art movement, Minimalism emerged in post-WWII Western art and peaked in the 1960s through early 1970s (Carl Andre, Donald Judd, Dan Flavin, Sol LeWitt, Agnes Martin, Robert Morris, Kenneth Noland, Robert Ryman, Frank Stella, Anne Truitt), inheriting the reductive strand of Modernism — Suprematism, Purism, De Stijl — and reacting against both gestural Abstract Expressionism and a centuries-long maximalist mainstream. Earlier currents fed it: German Plakatstil and Swiss International Typographic Style in graphic design, American Precisionism's reduced depiction of the world, Japanese everyday ceramics in earthy tones, and the plain geometry of ancient and Byzantine building exteriors. As a lifestyle and fashion language it resurged after 2010 (The Minimalists, tidying culture, vendors like COS, Everlane, Cuyana), on a design lineage of Jil Sander, Helmut Lang and Calvin Klein in the 1990s through Phoebe Philo's Céline, The Row and Lemaire in the 2010s.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Subtract first: pick one subject, then remove elements until removal would break the image.
2. Color arrives as planes — solid fields, seamless grounds, flat blocks — never as gradients or busy prints.
3. Muted by default; saturation is rationed, spent only on flat geometric primary blocks or one small object accent.
4. Show one or two true materials with matte finishes; let surface honesty replace ornament.
5. Compose the emptiness: negative space is a subject, sized generously against the focal object.
6. Impose order: align to an implicit grid, or repeat identical modules; avoid incidental variety.
7. Light as even witness: diffuse, flat, or single-shadow hard sun; never theatrical contrast.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may range across architecture, interior, still life, product shot, fashion figure, poster, sculpture, even a single food object — a lone macaron on a pale ground qualifies. Palette position may slide from white-dominant to charcoal-dominant, to a pastel seamless plane, to earthy ceramic tones, to a white field punctured by primary blocks. Composition may be centered specimen, off-center subject against void, module grid, or frontal elevation. Lighting may be soft daylight, hard sun with one shadow, even studio light, or dusk with a warm window. Mood temperature may run cool-clinical or warm-quiet. Medium may be photography, render, museum object shot, or flat poster. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

**Avoid.** Clutter: piles, stacked objects, crowded shelves, tangled cables, busy backgrounds, crowds of people. Ornament: mouldings, florals, baroque scrollwork, patterned wallpaper, ornate frames, decorative typography (small functional type is fine). Complex patterns and loud prints. Logos, slogans, branding noise. Saturated multi-hue palettes, neon, gradients, glossy shine. Dramatic chiaroscuro, lens flare, heavy bokeh clutter. Themed decor — nautical, rustic, vintage kitsch. Distressed or grunge textures. Exaggerated cartoon figures. Anything that signals accumulation or status display.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Brutalism** shares plainness but keeps weight: board-marked raw concrete, monolithic mass, fortress-like repetition, structure worn as aggression. Minimalism smooths the plane, lightens the volume, and stays quiet; if the concrete looks poured-and-scarred and the mass intimidates, it is Brutalism.
- **Chic Modernist** keeps luxury signals — marble veining, brass, walnut, a sculptural statement piece, curated objets d'art. Minimalism deletes status signals: matte, anonymous, no piece shouting for attention.
- **Coastal Style** is themed: rope, rattan, weathered grey wood, blue-and-white stripes, shells, breezy layered linen. Minimalism is theme-less; a white room with a rattan basket and striped throw has left minimalism for the coast.
- **Corporate Memphis** is flat vector illustration with impossible-proportioned humans, blob shapes and loud non-naturalistic color. Minimalism's reduction is photographic, material, or hard-edge geometric with muted fields — never cartoon flatness in candy saturation.
- **Cyberminimalism** is digital-native: dark UI grounds, neon or gradient accent glows, screens, glassmorphism, futuristic sans. Minimalism is analog and material — daylight, plaster, ceramic, cloth — with no glow and no interface.
