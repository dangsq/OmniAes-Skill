# Gyaru

Gyaru (ギャル, "gal") is a Japanese subculture that emerged when Western trends swept 1990s Japan and crystallized in Shibuya, Tokyo. Its defining claim is that the style lives in the **makeup and the overall vibe**, not in any fixed garment list: a gyaru coord can be denim and leopard one day and lace-and-bows pink the next, and still read as gyaru because the face and hair carry the code. The subculture is deliberately oppositional — tanned or heavily made-up skin, bleached hair and loud accessorizing were forged as a rejection of traditional Japanese beauty standards of pale skin, dark straight hair and restraint. Beneath the flash and the faux fur it is a social, joyful style: gal circles (gyarusa), para-para dancing to Eurobeat, purikura photo booths and gyaru-moji text messaging are all part of its visual world.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** Gyaru color is high-key and saturated, anchored by a recurring quartet — hot pink, leopard tan-and-black, denim blue, and black-or-white base — with subgenre dials that push toward neon (ganguro/manba), pastel cream-and-pink (hime), or black-dominant with pink/purple/white accents (agejo, rokku). Hair supplies its own palette layer: honey blonde, platinum ash, silver, strawberry pink. Skin reads either lightly tanned warm beige or, in historical ganguro, a deep stylized tan contrasted with white or neon makeup; fair skin with heavy makeup is equally valid in modern/reiwa gyaru. Metallic gold hardware and rhinestone sparkle punctuate everything.

**Makeup — the non-negotiable core.** The text is explicit that makeup is what separates gyaru's many fashions from neighboring aesthetics. Every era shares eye-enlarging intent: droopy "tareme" eyeliner drawn down and out at the outer corner, circle lenses that enlarge the iris, mascara and layered false lashes (historically plus an exaggerated drawn-on lower lash line; modernly plus "eye bag" aegyo-sal / namida bukuro highlight), visible nose highlight and contour, concealer around eyes, lips and nose, and pale, nude or light-pink lips so the eyes dominate. Agejo pushes thick liner top and bottom with dolly circle lenses; rokku swaps to smoky dark eyes and dark lipstick; hime keeps classic gyaru eyes with soft lip color.

**Hair.** Bleached to unnatural blonde or dyed pink, silver, light brown; long, wavy-to-curly, voluminous — beehives, big rolled curls, twin-tails, teased up-dos — because long hair is easier to accessorize. Straight, flat, undyed black hair is the traditional standard gyaru defines itself against.

**Motifs and objects, ranked by how prominently the text documents them.** (1) Exaggerated eye makeup and circle lenses; (2) bleached/dyed curly hair; (3) over-accessorizing — long fake nails with heavy deco, brand-name bags and jewelry, oversized hair bows and clips, deco-covered cell phones; (4) animal print, especially leopard; (5) denim mini skirts and shorts; (6) fuzzy leg warmers and loose socks; (7) platform boots, thigh-high and knee-high boots, heels; (8) subgenre vocabulary: lace, bows, gingham, polka dot and floral (hime/himekaji), crosses, studs, chokers, leather and rips (rokku), slogan tees, baseball caps and beanies, sneakers (amekaji), corset lacing, garter stockings and visible fancy bra straps (agejo); (9) sparkle graphics — glitter stamps, star bursts, sticker overlays.

**Materials and textures.** Faux fur and fleece (leg warmers, coat trims), patent and smooth leather (boots, bags), raw and distressed denim, satin ribbons, lace trims, rhinestones and plastic deco charms, metallic chain and logo hardware, knit sweaters with deliberate distressing. As a *medium*, gyaru imagery often carries print texture: magazine-scan halftone grain, Japanese caption columns, price callouts and brand names (Egg, Popteen, Ageha-era layouts), or early-digicam and purikura gloss with stamped sparkles.

**Recurring subjects.** Solo full-body coordinate shots on urban sidewalks; gal-circle group poses — cheeks together, arms around shoulders, peace signs, duck faces; makeup close-ups and before/after face diagrams with annotation callouts; accessory still lifes (deco phones, ribbon-trimmed bags, floral trunks); para-para dance frames; magazine covers and spread collages; purikura booth strips. Male gyaru (gyaruo) — bleached hair, tan, styled streetwear — are valid subjects within the subculture.

**Composition and lighting tendencies.** Vertical, head-to-toe framing that always includes the footwear; centered subject against ordinary city furniture (railings, planters, vending machines, signage, concrete walls). Lighting is bright and unsubtle: direct on-camera flash with hard falloff for night and indoor shots, slightly overexposed diffuse daylight for street snaps, flat bright purikura booth light with sparkle stamps for close-ups. Group shots crowd the frame; close-ups crop tight on the made-up face. Snapshot candidness beats studio polish.

## Mood & Values

Outgoing, confident, unapologetically yourself — the infobox values are the mood board. Gyaru projects loud femininity as strength and fun: maximalism as generosity of self-expression, not taste failure. It is social and performative in a friendly way (gal circles, synchronized para-para arms, twin coords with a friend), blunt and honest in attitude, and proudly rebellious against modesty norms. Playfulness permeates: gyaru-moji's deliberately unreadable cute cipher, sticker-strewn purikura, trance playlists. The style's confidence should read as joy and self-possession; the "mean girl / bully" trope is an external stereotype, not the subculture's self-image, and sexual objectification is not the point — gyaru is not inherently sexual and many adherents are minors.

## Era & Origins

The word derives from the Japanese spelling of "gal," used since the 1970s for lively young women; the subculture itself formed in mid-1990s Shibuya after its predecessor Bodikon radicalized (chapatsu brown, red and blonde dye, tanned skin, minimal clothing), boosted by fans imitating Namie Amuro's Okinawan tan against a pale-skin norm. Peak visibility ran late 1990s through the 2000s — Egg and Popteen spreads, ganguro and manba extremes, para-para boots, gyaru-moji texting — then toned down through recession fatigue and ageing originals. Since the 2020s a Heisei Retro nostalgia boom revived interest, splitting "heisei gyaru" (flash, print scans, heavier tan and lashes) from tamer, online-native "reiwa gyaru" (circle lenses, aegyo-sal, fast-fashion coords, Instagram/TikTok framing). Both poles belong to the style.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the face first: eye-enlarging gyaru makeup (tareme liner, circle lenses, lashes, aegyo-sal, pale lips, nose contour) is the load-bearing element; clothing alone never makes gyaru.
2. Give the hair volume and chemistry: bleached or dyed, curled or teased, long enough to accessorize.
3. Over-accessorize deliberately: nails, bag, jewelry, hair bow, phone deco — more is correct, minimal is wrong.
4. Anchor color in the pink / leopard / denim / black-white family, then dial toward neon, pastel or black-dominant per subgenre.
5. Keep the medium honest to the culture: street snap, magazine scan with Japanese text overlays, purikura gloss, or flash-lit night frame; bright, saturated, slightly overexposed light.
6. Pose with confidence and play: peace signs, hip cock, cheek-to-cheek group huddles, mid-para-para arm positions — never passive or demure.

**Variation axes.** Subject: solo coord, gal-circle group, gyaruo, makeup diagram close-up, accessory still life, dance frame, magazine cover. Composition: vertical full-body street snap, tight face crop, crowded group frame, collage spread, flat-lay of goods. Lighting: direct flash, blown daylight, purikura flat light, sparkle-overlay night. Color handling: saturated hot pink, pastel cream-pink-mint, black-with-pink agejo, neon-on-tan manba, any-hair-color rokku with dark lips. Era: heisei extremity (heavy tan optional, print grain) versus reiwa softness (fair skin, circle lenses, simpler coords). Mood: exuberant, sweet, glam, rock-leaning — all inside gyaru provided the makeup core holds.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A still life of a ribbon-trimmed bag with leopard nails and a deco phone can be as gyaru as a full Shibuya street snap, and a pastel himekaji coord in mint coats is as valid as a neon manba frame — neither needs to reproduce a specific reference's framing.

**Avoid.**
- Bare-faced or "no-makeup" natural faces; missing eye-enlarging makeup breaks the style regardless of clothing.
- Flat, straight, undyed black hair with no volume or accessory (the traditional standard gyaru opposes); note rokku may use dark hair but keeps smoky gyaru eyes.
- Muted minimalist wardrobes: quiet luxury, Scandinavian neutrals, beige-on-beige restraint, single delicate accessory.
- Desaturated, moody, fine-art or golden-hour-subtle lighting; gyaru light is bright, flashy, saturated.
- Mean-girl sneer, bullying clique narrative, or phone-scrolling disdain as the default expression — that is an external stereotype.
- Overt sexualization as the image's point; confidence and glamor, not objectification.
- Deep tan rendered as racial caricature or blackface-like depiction; ganguro tan is a historical, stylized period look (usually paired with white/neon contrast makeup) and unnatural tans are rare today — treat as period costume or omit.
- Western bimbo bombshell clichés that erase the Japanese street/print context.
- Sweet Lolita silhouettes (structured petticoat dresses, bonnet-like headwear) with no gyaru makeup — different code despite shared bows.
- Sterile studio e-commerce framing with no cultural texture (no city, no print grain, no snapshot energy).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Girly Kei:** shares cute feminine garments — florals, frills, pastel dresses — but drops gyaru's signature face and rebellion: no circle lenses, no tareme liner stack, no bleached voluminous curls, no tan-or-flash attitude. Test the face and hair first; if the makeup is soft-generic and the hair natural, it is Girly Kei territory, not gyaru.
- **Heisei Retro:** an era-nostalgia umbrella (flip phones, Y2K props, 90s–2000s Japanese pop memorabilia) that can *contain* gyaru coords but does not require gyaru makeup or hair. Gyaru images center the made-up face and coord; Heisei Retro images can center nostalgic objects with casually styled subjects. Gyaru also exists outside nostalgia (reiwa gyaru), so period props alone prove nothing.
- **Himekaji:** the tamest pole of the gyaru spectrum itself — gingham, polka dot, plaid, floral, boots with knee-highs, comfy cuteness, simple gyaru makeup, plain or French nails. It tips out of core gyaru when volume curls, heavy deco nails, bold lashes and loud accessories all disappear at once; core gyaru keeps at least the makeup core plus accessorizing intensity.
- **Nanchatte Seifuku:** built on customized school-uniform pieces (blazer, pleated skirt, neck ribbon) worn as fashion with relatively natural hair and makeup. Gyaru may borrow uniform signals (rolled skirt, loose socks — the kogyaru lineage) but keeps bleached curls, gyaru eyes and street-fashion mixing; if the uniform is the whole statement and the face is clean, it is nanchatte.
- **PEEPS:** a gyaru-adjacent, internet-native revival micro-style (documented thinly here) that leans harder into neon rave-cyber Y2K graphics, digital-native styling and clubwear brightness. Gyaru stays rooted in its makeup tradition, animal print/pink/denim vocabulary, and street-snap or print-scan medium; if the image reads as neon graphic net-fashion without the gyaru face code, it is PEEPS-leaning rather than gyaru.

**Quick test order:** (1) eyes — circle lenses, tareme liner, lashes, aegyo-sal? (2) hair — bleached/dyed, curled, voluminous? (3) accessorizing density — nails, bag, bows, deco? (4) palette — pink/leopard/denim/black-white family or a sanctioned subgenre dial? (5) medium — snap, scan, purikura brightness? Three or more yeses: gyaru.
