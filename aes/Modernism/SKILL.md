# Modernism

Modernism is not one look but a discipline of looking: the conviction, formed between the 1890s and 1945 in Western Europe, that inherited forms could no longer carry modern experience, and that art must therefore be rebuilt from structure, function, and subjective perception outward. "Make it new" is its motto; rejection of historical revivalism, belief in progress, function over ornament, and the primacy of individual inner experience are its creed. Because it shelters Cubism, Surrealism, Dada, De Stijl, Bauhaus, Futurism and the International Style in architecture, recognition never rests on a single formula. It rests on a constellation: era-consistent means (plane, line, grid, fragment, machine, white volume), an anti-academic stance, and color used structurally rather than decoratively.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

Modernist color behaves in regulated families; saturation always has a structural job (define a plane, assert a grid, isolate an object), never a decorative one.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how strongly the text documents them)

1. **Abstraction and geometric form** — the cylinder, cone, grid, circle and rectangle as building blocks; figures and still lifes reduced to interlocking planes.
2. **Fragmentation** — fractured facets, multiple simultaneous viewpoints, shattered and reassembled space; the visual analogue of stream-of-consciousness writing. Collage and papier collé belong here: wallpaper, faux-bois, newspaper and printed paper inserted as literal fragments.
3. **Machinery and the modern city** — engines, bridges, factories, skylines treated as new subject matter and new syntax (text-prominent; in the sampled imagery this strand surfaces mostly as Precisionist architectural geometry rather than literal machines, so treat it as licensed but not obligatory).
4. **Subjective reality** — dream logic, displaced objects, melting or doubled forms, deadpan captions asserting paradox; the inner life made visible instead of the retinal world.
5. **Functionalist architecture vocabulary** — flat roofs, pilotis lifting the volume off the ground, ribbon windows, curtain walls, steel frames, reinforced concrete, asymmetrical massing, white stucco, roof terraces and ramps.
6. **Typography as design** — spare lettering (serif or geometric sans) centered or gridded on a single monochrome field; the book jacket as manifesto.
7. **The readymade and photomontage** — the manufactured object presented unaltered as art; cut-and-pasted press imagery as critique; Dada's deadpan gesture inside the Modernist umbrella.

### Materials and textures

Oil paint laid with controlled, visible facture or smoothed to illusionistic enamel; matte gesso and distemper; glued paper with printed pattern, wood-grain imitation and typographic fragments; bare canvas edges and exposed support in collage work; gelatin-silver black-and-white photograph with fine grain and full tonal scale; white-painted plaster and stucco, rolled steel, plate glass, poured concrete in built work; letterpress ink on cloth-bound board. Surfaces are worked, not glossy: no varnish shine, no digital sheen.

### Composition

Asymmetry is the default balance system. Depth is either flattened into shallow overlapping planes (Cubism, abstraction) or deliberately emptied into a vast horizon with tiny isolated objects (Surrealism); classical single-point perspective is denied or drained of meaning. An orthogonal armature — grid, axis, module — usually underlies even the most fractured canvas. Negative space is active: blank ground, bare wall, empty sky carry as much weight as form. Framing crops abruptly, off-center, as if the image were a section of a larger system. In architecture and typography: modular repetition (ribbon windows, bay grids), free plan, roof garden, ramp, and generous margins around type.

### Light

Three licensed modes: (a) **conceptual light** — no single source; shading acts as local tone, cast shadows suppressed (Cubism, abstraction, collage); (b) **even daylight** — diffuse, often overcast, shadowless or soft-shadowed, the light of architectural photography and white volumes on green lawn; (c) **metaphysical raking light** — low sun, long hard shadows, twilight gradients across an empty plain (Surrealism, Precisionism). All three are cool, analytical, unsentimental; none is golden-hour glow.

## Mood & Values

Intellectual urgency and utopian optimism tempered by interwar anxiety: the belief that design can reshape society, shadowed by the psychological shock of the First World War. Feeling is expressed through structure, not gesture — wit arrives deadpan (a pipe that is not a pipe, a urinal signed and exhibited), grief arrives as fractured planes or a bare horizon. Values: experimentation over mastery, function over ornament, the individual's subjective experience over collective convention, progress over nostalgia.

## Era & Origins

c. 1890s–1945, Western Europe, radiating globally. It reacts against academic realism, historicist revivalism and ornamental eclecticism, and is catalyzed by industrialization, urbanization, new physics and psychology, and the trauma of WWI. Its dominance ends with the devastation of WWII, which breaks its faith in progress and opens the door to Postmodernism. Touchstones: Picasso and Braque's planes, Matisse's reduction, Le Corbusier's and Mies van der Rohe's white volumes, Joyce's and Woolf's fragmented narratives, Duchamp's readymades, Dalí's and Magritte's paradoxes.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** Begin from a concept or a structure, not an appearance: decide what the image must *analyze* (form, function, perception, dream) before choosing a subject. Reduce the subject to planes, lines, grids or volumes; then either fragment it into simultaneous views or isolate it in emptied space. Let materials speak industrially or manually-but-austerely (collage paper, plaster, steel, silver print). Give color a structural role. Keep ornament absent unless it is the structure itself. Balance asymmetrically. Keep the surface era-plausible: matte paint, paper grain, photographic silver, stucco — never CGI gloss.

**Variation axes.** Subject: still life, portrait or nude, cityscape, machine, building interior or exterior, book jacket, dreamscape, pure non-objective composition, photomontage, readymade object. Composition: from dense faceted all-over field, to strict orthogonal grid, to a near-empty horizon with one or three small objects. Lighting: any of the three modes above. Color handling: doctrinal primaries, Cubist earth, machine white, metaphysical desert tones, or muted pastel geometry. Mood: utopian, analytical, anxious, or deadpan comic. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A garden, a bicycle, a staircase or a letterform can be fully Modernist if it is treated with fragmentation, reduction, functional honesty or dream-displacement.

**Avoid.** Historicist ornament (columns, pediments, cornices, moldings, acanthus); academic salon finish and sentimental narrative illustration; decorative symmetry used for luxury; gilding, lacquer, ivory, sunburst zigzags and streamlined chrome glamour (Art Deco); raw board-marked concrete monoliths and exposed brute massing (Brutalism, post-1945); neon, digital gradients, lens flare, CGI smoothness; post-1945 idioms such as Pop irony, Memphis patternplay or postmodern pastiche columns; pastoral picturesque clutter; uniform decorative polychromy with no structural role; golden-hour sentimentality.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Bauhaus** is a school *inside* Modernism: expect workshop objects, tubular-steel furniture, pedagogical diagrams, primaries applied to product design. Modernism at large also licenses painterly fragmentation, collage and Surrealist dream, which Bauhaus doctrine excludes.
- **Brutalism** (post-1945) shows raw béton brut, board-marked concrete, heavy monolithic weight and exposed services; Modernism pre-1945 favors white plaster, steel, glass, lightness, pilotis and painted surfaces.
- **De Stijl** is the strict subset: only orthogonal black lines and unmixed primaries on white. Modernism permits diagonals, curves, earth tones, figuration, illusionistic depth and collage.
- **Fauvism** keeps a continuous painterly surface and wild, sensation-first color with expressive brushwork; Modernism subordinates color to plane, grid and concept, and fractures or flattens the surface.
- **Art Deco** decorates: symmetry, luxury materials, zigzags, sunbursts, stylized figuration as ornament. Modernism strips: function first, asymmetry, industrial materials, ornament deleted rather than stylized.

When in doubt, ask: does the image look like an *experiment in structure or perception* made between the 1890s and 1945, confident that old forms had failed? If yes, it is Modernist.
