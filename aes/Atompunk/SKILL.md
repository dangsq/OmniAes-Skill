# Atompunk

Atompunk (also raypunk) is the retrofuturism of the 1950s and 1960s seen from its dark side: the era's glossy promises of nuclear power, space travel and hover-everything, retold with the seams showing. It borrows the entire visual vocabulary of Raygun Gothic, Googie and Populuxe — finned rockets, parabolic arches, chrome, starbursts — but refuses to celebrate it. The result is an aesthetic of gleeful dread: a bubble-helmeted family beside a reactor, a satin-bright spaceport over a dead landscape, a suburb of impossible appliances rendered with a straight face and one wrong note. Modern depictions tend toward a distinct, brightly colored illustration style descended from pulp magazine covers and film posters, but the style also admits desaturated post-nuclear registers and dense monochrome megastructure visions inherited from Metropolis-style Dieselpunk.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette (rule, not recipe).** Atompunk color behaves like poster paint: a limited set of saturated fields locked in complementary pairs, or smooth airbrush gradients between them, never photographic neutral grading. Three registers share one bridge of chrome silver and starfield black:

| Register | Typical hues | Hex range |
|---|---|---|
| Pulp saturation | atomic orange/vermilion against teal; mustard/amber against indigo-violet; magenta against chrome | #D94F1E–#FF8A3C, #1F9E8E–#8FD8C8, #D9A441–#FFD75E, #241B3E–#4E3D72, #C94F7C–#F2919B |
| Populuxe pastel | powder blue, mint, cream, coral, pale gold for suburbia and city vistas | #A8CBE0–#DCE9F2, plus coral/pink accents |
| Fallout desaturation | ash gray, bone, rust, ochre, sickly atomic green glow for post-nuclear scenes | #8E8B84–#D8D3C8, #8C4A26–#C98A4B, glow ≈ #A8E063 |
| Monochrome ink (minor facet) | graphite, ink black, paper white for Metropolis-like megastructure plates | grayscale |

A correct image may sit anywhere on this span — a pastel noon cityscape and a violet-sky orange desert are both Atompunk — but saturated complementary contrast or deliberate period pastel is the norm; muddy modern grading is not.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them.** (1) Nuclear-based power: reactors, radiation starbursts, atomic signage, mushroom-cloud memory, glowing core light. (2) Space travel: finned rockets standing on tripod gantries, ringed orbital stations, moon and planet landings, astronauts in silver suits and bubble helmets. (3) Hover technology: flying cars, saucer craft, monorails and sweeping elevated highways threading towers. (4) Googie and Populuxe architecture: needle spires, parabolic arches, domes, boomerang and starburst forms, dramatic cantilevers. (5) Satirized suburban Americana: the nuclear family home filled with absurd atomic appliances, diners, consumer gloss. (6) Pulp-comic apparatus: magazine-cover compositions, comic panel insets, blocky atomic-age display type, pin-up-inflected fashion of pulp sci-fi outfits and printed tee graphics. (7) Cities of tomorrow as megastructure — and its inverse, the post-nuclear empty landscape made in response to Cold War anxiety; the style explicitly need not be urban. (8) Analog control surfaces: dials, toggle switches, cathode-ray tubes, punch cards, vacuum-tube glow. (9) Secondary crossover: Art Deco ziggurat density and Dieselpunk gravity in the monochrome facet.

**Materials and textures.** Gouache and airbrush pulp painting with visible brush grain and offset-print tooth; polished chrome and painted steel; enamel, fiberglass and glass bubble domes; concrete terraces and terrazzo interiors; halftone dots and poster paper in the comic facet; clean flat vector gradients in the modern revival facet; dense crosshatched ink in the monochrome facet.

**Recurring subjects.** Spaceports and launch gantries; orbital stations above a planet limb with a second moon in the sky; aerial city vistas layered with traffic; desert test sites and alien landscapes under complementary skies; domestic interiors crowded with consoles; standing groups of pulp heroes in period futures-fashion; wastelands where the chrome has rusted.

**Composition and lighting tendencies.** Monumental low angles that make rockets and spires tower; god's-eye aerial vistas with receding terraces and highway diagonals; tiny human figures for scale; poster-emblem centrality with open sky reserved for type; horizon lines carrying two celestial bodies as period shorthand. Lighting is theatrical: hard single-source sun with crisp shadows, orange horizon glow under violet sky, atomic or lava underglow, starfield black with planet-shine, or flat pastel noon haze in the Populuxe register.

## Mood & Values

Atompunk holds two beliefs at once: endless technological advancement, and pessimism about the people wielding it. Its values are pulp comic exuberance, the iconography of the "traditionally American" nuclear family and suburban lifestyle, and a countercultural refusal to take either seriously. The mood ranges from deadpan satire and absurd consumer cheer to cold-war dread, post-apocalyptic melancholy and lonely machine grandeur. The emotional signature is the gap between the bright surface and the dark subtext — chrome with rust under it, a smile held a second too long.

## Era & Origins

The visual language was born in 1950s–60s American, European and Japanese pulp science fiction, Googie architecture, Populuxe design and Space Race graphics. The genre consciousness is the dark underbelly of that world: it emerges with Philip K. Dick's Solar Lottery and the dystopian pulp lineage (Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep, I Have No Mouth and I Must Scream, 2000 AD), coined as a term only after Cyberpunk existed, where "-punk" marks counterculture rather than a music scene. It reacts against the utopian celebration of Raygun Gothic and against conformist 1950s consumerism; modern carriers include the Fallout series, Futurama, The Outer Worlds, Prey, We Happy Few and Atomfall. Visual evidence for fashion and music exists in text (pulp outfits, suggestive comic-cover tees, analogue synth and psychedelia-adjacent rock) but is thin in imagery; the monochrome Metropolis facet and the Japanese magazine illustration facet are real but minor branches.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

1. **Era lens first.** Every object must read as a 1950s–60s prediction: fins, needles, rings, domes, chrome, analog dials. No object may look invented after ~1965.
2. **Pick a register on the optimism–dread axis and commit.** Sincere pulp brightness, satirical pastel suburbia, or post-nuclear desolation. The brightest scenes keep one wrong note (rust, emptiness, ominous signage, absurdity); the darkest keep one period-bright accent (a candy-colored console, a starburst logo).
3. **Poster color logic.** Limited saturated palette in complementary pairs, flat fields or airbrush gradients; pastel Populuxe and desaturated fallout registers are equally valid.
4. **Period medium texture.** Gouache brush grain, offset print tooth, halftone, or clean mid-century chart-style vector; the image should feel printed or painted, not photographed.
5. **Scale rhetoric.** Dwarf humans against machines and megastructures, or trap them in domestic interiors of absurd appliances.
6. **Analog only.** Cathode-ray tubes, dials, toggles, punch cards; atomic glow as motivated light source.
7. **Period typography when type appears:** blocky atomic-age display sans, chrome bevels, starburst logos.

**Variation axes.** Subject: spaceport, orbital station, suburb, wasteland, interior vignette, desert test site, city vista, poster figure group, monochrome megastructure. Composition: aerial vista, low-angle monument, interior crowding, poster emblem. Lighting: pastel noon, complementary sunset, starfield, atomic underglow, flat monochrome. Color: any point on the pulp-saturation → Populuxe-pastel → fallout-desaturation span, including grayscale. Mood: sincere, satirical, dread, melancholy. Medium: gouache pulp, comic flat color, clean vector, ink plate. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a kitchen, a billboard, a graveyard of rockets or a station over an unnamed planet all qualify if the rules hold.

**Avoid.** Contemporary digital technology: smartphones, flat-panel screens, LEDs, quadrotor drones, modern UI holograms. Cyberpunk neon-rain night cities and dense contemporary signage. Steampunk brass, steam and clockwork; Victorian ornament. Pure untroubled utopian gloss with no irony, decay, satire or dread — that is Raygun Gothic or Space Age, not Atompunk. Modern minimalist sci-fi: seamless white curves, glass-box skylines, Apple-store interiors. Gritty photoreal contemporary war imagery. Organic biotech or grown architecture. Fantasy or medieval elements. Present-day cars, planes or fashion. Muddy desaturated modern film grading without period intent.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Mid-Century Modern** is the era's actual domestic design: wood, organic furniture forms, muted earth tones, no speculative technology and no narrative dread. Atompunk adds rockets, reactors and a story.
- **Raygun Gothic** shares the entire vocabulary but celebrates a utopian future sincerely. Atompunk is the same gloss with the lie exposed: satire, rust, emptiness, menace, absurdity.
- **Rocketpunk** is sober space-race technical realism — procedural rocketry, muted technical illustration, optimistic competence. Atompunk is pulp color, Googie ornament and social critique.
- **Space Age / Atomic Age** are period design movements in real products and graphics: clean white plastic, sputnik motifs, starburst patterns, sincere optimism. Atompunk is a retrospective fictional narrative scene, darker or satirical, with illustration texture.
- **Googie** is an architecture and signage style alone; Atompunk embeds Googie forms inside scenes with mood and consequence.
- **Dieselpunk / Decopunk** run on interwar diesel machinery, noir grime or deco luxe; Atompunk's machines are postwar, atomic, chrome-and-fin, and its anxiety is nuclear rather than industrial.
