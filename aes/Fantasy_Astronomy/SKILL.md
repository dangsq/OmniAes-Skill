# Fantasy Astronomy

## Overview

Fantasy Astronomy is the aesthetic of astronomy as it would be practiced in a storybook: the instruments, plates and dress of 16th–17th century Scientific Revolution observers and 19th-century popular astronomy books, transplanted into fictional settings with magic systems, feudal politics and whimsical observatory architecture. Its astronomy is deliberately simpler and more magical than the real science — mapping constellations, reading fate in star positions, scrying through glass, watching the moon through a brass tube — so the style always carries two registers at once: the scholarly (plates, legends, textbooks, bookshelves) and the enchanted (glowing flame, crystal spheres, stitched comets, impossible domes). It sits inside the broader Academia family of aesthetics but swaps the warm library browns for night-sky blues and adds an explicit magic layer.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette (rule, not recipe)

The field is night. Establish a deep blue or violet ground first; everything else is applied on top as light, metal or paper.

| Role | Hex range | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Night field | navy `#0E1B33`–`#1E2A4A`, midnight `#0A1020`–`#101A38` | Dominant background for scenes, textiles, chart circles |
| Violet night | bluish purple `#2E2A50`–`#4A3F7A` | Alternate dominant; gowns, nebulous washes, dusk skies |
| Antique gold | `#B08D2E`–`#D4AF37` | Line-work, gilt instruments, embroidery, constellation figures on blue |
| Starlight silver | `#C9CEDC`–`#EDEFF5` | Constellation dots and lines, moonlight, glitter, metallic thread |
| Parchment paper | `#EFE6D0`–`#E2D5B8` | Ground for plate/engraving mode; margins, page stock |
| Ink accents | oxblood red-brown `#7E3B2A`, deep teal `#12303A` | Ornamental banners, plate backgrounds, small print; never dominant |

Rules: metals and starlight appear as *lines, points, glitter and thin highlights*, never as large flat metallic fields; parchment appears as paper ground or margin, never as an overall warm tone (that drifts into ordinary Academia); bright saturated hues other than blue-violet are excluded. A desaturated silver-monochrome treatment and teal-shifted gradients are acceptable edge cases within the range.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Constellations and star maps** — connected star lines, celestial hemispheres drawn as circles, stippled Milky Way bands, numbered key legends in small serif type. The single most diagnostic element.
2. **Telescopes** — brass-and-wood refractors on tripods, handheld spyglasses, telescope slits in domes.
3. **Planets, moons, stars, comets, galaxy artwork** — especially cratered moons rendered as engravings or halftone prints, and streaking comets.
4. **Astrolabes and brass kin** — armillary spheres, orreries, celestial globes, sextants; gilt, patinated, ornamental.
5. **Observatory and study interiors** — bookshelves, ladders, desks strewn with charts, domed rooms, and (text-documented, image-rare) painted ceiling frescoes of the heavens; architecture allowed to be whimsical, curved, impossible.
6. **Astronomy texts and research papers** — open books showing moon plates, textbooks, marginalia, folio plates with running heads and plate numbers.
7. **The magic layer** — astrology and divination: crystal spheres, celestial flame, moon-phase talismans, stars behaving as living or prophetic things.
8. **Celestial fashion and accessories** — Victorian and 1920s–60 silhouettes: capes, cloaks, blazers with elbow patches, waistcoats, pinafores, Peter Pan collars, pleated and split skirts; star and constellation prints or embroidery on velvet and tulle; newsboy caps, top hats, monocles, pocket watches, satchels, brogues, Mary Janes, lace-up boots. Note the text's explicit allowance: celestial motifs need *not* appear on every garment — plaid, gingham and hound-tooth are in-canon plain fabrics.
9. **Scholarly small goods** — regulator and bracket clocks, spectacles, ink and pen, paper fans of charts; the clutter of a working observer.

### Materials and textures

Aged paper with plate marks and slight foxing; copperplate and stipple engraving; brass, gilt wood, patinated bronze; glass lenses and crystal spheres; velvet, tulle, wool, embroidery floss and metallic thread; candle wax and lamplight; wood panelling; fine glitter standing in for star dust. Surfaces should read hand-made: drawn, printed, stitched or gilded.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Recurring structures: the **circular medallion** (a planisphere or hemisphere centered in a rectangular field); the **framed plate** (image inset in parchment margins with ornamental border, ribbon banner, two-column legend, plate number); the **nocturnal interior** (low-key chiaroscuro, one warm candle or oil-lamp source against cool ambient night, stars visible through a window or dome slit); the **fragment collage** (grids of charts, instruments, architectural details); the **full-length costume figure**; and the **all-over scattered pattern** of moons, stars and comets on blue. Negative space is a populated void: deep blue carrying sparse points of light.

Three lighting modes are canonical: flat even "archival scan" light for plates and prints; candle/moon chiaroscuro with glowing magical accents for scenes; soft diffused daylight for couture presentation, where motif and palette carry the style alone. Nighttime is the default atmosphere for anything depicting a scene.

## Mood & Values

Reverent curiosity: wonder treated as scholarship and scholarship treated as wonder. The style values quiet nocturnal devotion, the romance of pre-modern science, elegance over utility, and the coexistence of reason and magic — charting a comet and reading a fate are the same act. There is whimsy in the architecture and gravity in the dress; the emotional temperature is contemplative, ceremonial, occasionally secretly occult, never frantic or horrific.

## Era & Origins

Visual sources span the Scientific Revolution (16th–17th c.) observer culture and the engraved plates of 19th/early-20th-century popular astronomy (the Flammarion-era amateur astronomy book), filtered through Victorian and 1920s–60 vintage fashion. As a named aesthetic it is a product of online aesthetic-wiki culture, cataloguing how fantasy media — games, animation, films like *A Trip to the Moon* — reimagine astronomy with magic systems and feudal settings. It reacts against both hard sci-fi space realism and against plain Academia by insisting the sky be enchanted and the palette be night.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. **Night field first.** Lay down navy, midnight or violet-blue (or parchment, if making a plate) before any content.
2. **Metal as line.** Gold and silver function as constellation lines, engraving, embroidery, glitter and instrument bodies — thin, precise, luminous against dark.
3. **Hand-made cosmos.** Stars are drawn, stitched, engraved or painted: connected dots, stippled bands, appliquéd moons. The sky is an artifact, not a photograph.
4. **One instrument or text minimum.** Unless the image is pure pattern or couture, include a telescope, astrolabe, armillary, chart or open plate book to anchor the scholarly register.
5. **Magic, gently.** Divination glow, sentient stars, impossible domes are welcome; the magic astonishes rather than threatens.
6. **Ornate framing logic.** Serif or engraved lettering, legends, borders and scrollwork for plate-like images; curved, whimsical architecture for interiors.
7. **Period dress, optional stars.** Victorian-to-midcentury silhouettes; celestial print is common but not mandatory on every piece.
8. **Stay pre-digital.** No screens, no modern machinery (star-map apps belong to the activity canon, but the visual language remains analog).

Variation axes: subject (folio plate, moon map, observatory interior, instrument still life, divination act, costume figure, textile pattern, collage); composition (medallion, framed plate with marginalia, single-object still life, wide interior, full-length figure, close-up textile, grid collage); lighting (flat scan light, candle chiaroscuro, cool moonlit ambient, soft daylight couture); color handling (navy-dominant with gold line, violet-dominant, silver-monochrome, parchment-dominant with navy insets, teal-shifted gradients); mood (quiet study, ceremonial grandeur, occult secrecy, playful whimsy); medium look (engraving, watercolor, oil, photograph of objects or garments, embroidery close-up, digital painting in antique manner). **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.**

Avoid: rockets, satellites, astronauts, control rooms and any modern space technology; photorealistic astrophotography nebulae as the sole content; neon cyan/magenta, glitch effects and digital-glow nightcore energy; horror occultism (skulls, blood, gore); laboratory glassware, electrical arcs, green potions and manic invention clutter; warm brown tweed libraries with no celestial content; minimalist flat vector infographics and sans-serif branding; large holographic or iridescent fields (only small accents are canonical); cartoon sci-fi kitsch such as smiling planets or ray guns; bright daytime sky-blue as the dominant field; modern casual sportswear.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Nightcore (aesthetic):** also night-loving, but digital and urban — neon signs, anime stylization, electric cyan and magenta, headphones, nightlife energy. Fantasy Astronomy is analog: paper, brass, velvet, candlelight, engraved stars; no neon, no screens, no city.
- **Spacecore:** the real cosmos and modern exploration — photographic nebulae, planets as photographs, satellites, astronauts, awe of vacuum and scale. Fantasy Astronomy's cosmos is hand-made and inhabited by magic; if an image could pass as a space-agency poster, it is Spacecore.
- **Wizardcore:** generic magic academia — spellbooks, runes, potions, candles, staves, herbal and earthy props, jewel-tone robes. Overlaps in magic-plus-study; the test is specificity of sky. Star maps, telescopes, moon phases, observatories and celestial embroidery mark Fantasy Astronomy; candles and runes with no astronomical content mark Wizardcore.
- **Mad Scientist:** laboratory chaos, electricity, cobbled gadgets, manic energy, green and amber glass, horror-comedy grit. Fantasy Astronomy is contemplative and elegant; its instruments observe heavens rather than experiment on bodies, and its mood is reverence, not frenzy.
- **Plain Academia / Science Academia:** share books, scholarship and vintage dress, but keep warm daylight palettes and lack the night-blue field, celestial motif and magic layer; add enchanted stars and the palette flips, and it becomes Fantasy Astronomy.
