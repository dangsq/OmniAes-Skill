# Impressionism

Impressionism is a nineteenth-century painting movement formed in Paris in the 1870s–1880s by artists breaking from the French academic system. Its contract with the viewer is perceptual: paint not the object but the visual sensation the object produces in a specific moment of light. That contract produces everything else — the dabbed stroke, the bright unmixed palette, the violet shadow, the cropped frame, the refusal of varnish and polish. The name was coined as an insult by the critic Louis Leroy after seeing Monet's *Impression, soleil levant* at the first independent exhibition of 1874; the group embraced it and held eight exhibitions through 1886. What the Salon called "unfinished" is the style's core finish: a surface of discrete touches that only blend in the eye.

## Visual Identity

### Color language (the rule, not a swatch)

Color is keyed to one observed light condition and never to local description alone. The backbone is cool: cerulean, cobalt and blue-violet carry sky, water and — crucially — shadow. Shadows are colored, typically violet, lavender or blue-green, lifted by reflected light from sky or water; black paint and bitumen browns are avoided, and the deepest darks are plum, indigo or blue-green. Warm notes sit on top as accents rather than as ground: cream and light-struck white, coral or vermilion in a hat or sash, ochre in straw, paths and harvested fields, chrome-yellow flicks in sunlit grass. In overcast or misty keys the whole harmony desaturates toward pearl grey-lilac; in noon keys the same hues appear as saturated, unmixed dabs placed side by side ("broken color") so the eye mixes them optically. Muddy palette-blending and smooth gradients are foreign to the style.

### Brushwork, surface, medium

The signature is the small, thin, yet visible stroke: dashes, commas and dabs whose direction follows the thing described — swirling curves in cloud, horizontal drags in water, flicks in foliage, parallel hatches in a dress or a background. Stroke scale varies within the style, from broader, flatter planes in Manet's manner to dense flickering touches in Monet's, but the stroke always stays legible and the surface always reads slightly sketch-like by academic standards. The look is matte and unvarnished; thin passages let the ground or canvas show, and there is no glaze shine. A dry, friable variant exists within the movement's range — layered parallel pastel-like hatching, most associated with Degas and Cassatt — which keeps the same broken-color logic in a dustier, more linear hand; the wiki records painting as the movement's medium, and this hatched handling is best treated as a surface treatment inside oil or pastel practice rather than a separate style. Edges are lost-and-found: contours dissolve where light strikes and re-form in shade.

### Signature motifs and subjects (ranked by the text)

1. **Effects of changing light and atmosphere** — the primary motif: water surfaces, skies, mist, haze, reflections, smoke in moist air. Light itself is the subject; boats, trees and figures are its carriers.
2. **Modern leisure** — boating, promenades, parasols, garden seats, cafés and dance halls; suburban weekends and river outings.
3. **Urban scenes of Paris** — boulevards, quays, streets, carriages, crowds reduced to flicks of color.
4. **Rural and suburban landscape** — riverbanks, tree allées, fields, orchards, windmills, harbors; modern intrusions such as factory chimneys, masts and carriages appear inside landscape as accents of contemporary life, not as narrative.
5. **Domestic intimacy and portraits-as-light-studies** — mothers with children, women sewing or singing, dancers backstage; figures caught mid-gesture rather than posed, identity secondary to the light on them.
6. **Contemporary dress and props as light-catchers** — straw boaters, parasols, ribbons and sashes, white flannels, long dresses: costume valued for how it holds daylight.

### Composition

Open composition is definitional. The frame crops arbitrarily, like a glance: a sail or parasol shaft cuts a corner, a figure is halved at the edge, the viewpoint runs low (looking up under a parasol into cloud) or high (looking down at dancers or water). Focal points sit off-center; figures are incidental and small; foregrounds of grass, gravel or water may stay empty. Receding perspectives — allées, quays, boulevards — are allowed but are dissolved in light and air rather than ruled by geometry. At one extreme the horizon disappears entirely into an all-over field of water and reflection; at the other, deep aerial perspective pales and cools the distance. Closed, centered, pyramidal tableau arrangements belong to the academic tradition this style rejects.

### Light

Always natural daylight in a single consistent condition: pearly overcast, dappled leaf-shadow, bright noon, or hazy morning and mist. There is no chiaroscuro, no spotlight, no studio backdrop, no candlelit blackness. Whites glow but keep their stroke; shadows stay luminous and colored; air is visible between planes, and distance cools toward the sky's hue.

## Mood & Values

The style values the fleeting moment over the finished story: visual sensation over fine detail, perception over invention, observation over moral. It asserts that ordinary modern life — a boat ride, a street, a child's bath, a rehearsal — is worthy of serious art, and that academic tradition (varnish, polish, history painting) falsifies experience. The resulting mood is breezy, ephemeral and unheroic: sunlit calm, pearly quiet, intimate but unsentimental, with the freshness of first sight and a slight productive haste, as if the light were about to change.

## Era & Origins

Paris, 1870s–1880s. Roots lie in Eugène Boudin's and Johan Jongkind's studies of sky and light and in Édouard Manet's bridge from Realism; the first group exhibition of 1874 (Monet, Degas, Renoir, Pissarro, Morisot and others) drew the derisive name, and eight exhibitions followed through 1886. Two material conditions enabled the look: painting outdoors, *en plein air*, to observe changing daylight directly, and new synthetic pigments — cobalt, cerulean, viridian, chrome yellow, synthetic ultramarine — that made bright unmixed color possible, combined with a deliberate rejection of the toning varnish that had greyed earlier painting. The movement liberated color and brushwork from purely descriptive roles and paved the way to Post-Impressionism.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Decide the light before the subject: one time of day, one weather, one consistent source, and key every surface to it.
2. Build form from broken color — adjacent dabs of unmixed hue that blend optically — never from smooth gradients or blended transitions.
3. Keep the stroke visible and directional: sky swirls, water drags, foliage flicks, hatched dresses; let edges soften and dissolve in light.
4. Color every shadow violet, lavender or blue-green; reserve the darkest notes for plum, indigo or blue-green, never black.
5. Crop like a passerby: off-center focus, elements cut by the frame, incidental figures, empty luminous foregrounds.
6. Keep the subject ordinary and modern for its moment: leisure, street, water, garden, domestic quiet; no heroes, no allegory.
7. Finish "unfinished": matte, unvarnished, sketch-vivid; stop while the sensation is fresh.
8. Let distance cool and pale; paint the air between planes, not only the planes.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may span water-and-sky light studies, urban boulevards and cafés, garden or river leisure, daylight domestic interiors, portraits-as-light-studies, and rural fields, orchards, harbors or windmills with or without industrial accents. Composition may place the horizon high, low or absent; crop figures or keep them whole; use a receding allée or a diagonal intrusion; view from above or below. Lighting may run from pearl-grey overcast through dappled shade to noon brilliance to misty dawn, backlit or side-lit, so long as it is natural and single. Color handling may sit anywhere between a near-monochrome cool harmony and a full-spectrum dappled key, and between wet oil dabs and dry hatched pastel-like touch. Mood may be breezy leisure, quiet domesticity, atmospheric solitude or urban flicker. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a rooftop in rain or a market street never painted by the historical group can be fully Impressionist if it obeys the light, stroke, shadow and composition rules above.

**Avoid (style-breakers).** Black paint, bitumen browns and varnish toning; grey-brown muddy shadows. Smooth airbrushed gradients, invisible brushwork, uniform photoreal crispness. Hard contour outlines enclosing flat color planes. Chiaroscuro, spotlighting, candlelit night with black shadow, studio backdrops. Historical, mythological, religious or allegorical subjects, heroic nudity, moralizing anecdote, costume-pageant "types." Systematic uniform pointillist dots, sculptural impasto swirls, geometric faceting of form. Arbitrary non-observed hue chosen for effect rather than for perceived light. Closed centered compositions, vignetted or fully finished edges on all four sides. Glossy varnish shine or digital smoothness.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Japonisme** shares the asymmetry, high viewpoints and bold cropping, and Impressionism borrowed those devices; but Japonisme keeps flat, unmodulated color planes, crisp enclosing outlines and patterned surface decoration, with no atmospheric broken stroke. If the crop vibrates with dabs of optically mixed color and visible air, it is Impressionism; if it sits in smooth flat areas behind a line, it is Japonisme.
- **Post-Impressionism** keeps the bright palette but restores structure: contour, geometric order and faceted planes (Cézanne), emotive or symbolic color and heavy directional impasto (Van Gogh), cloisonnist flats (Gauguin). Impressionist stroke dissolves form into light; Post-Impressionist stroke rebuilds form into structure or feeling.
- **Realism** (the movement's parent, with Manet as hinge) uses earth palettes, black or brown shadows, smoothed finish and socially legible narrative — laborers, typed figures, a story. Impressionism swaps narrative for sensation and earth tones for spectrum; its figures are anonymous moderns, not social arguments.
- **Costumbrismo** paints local custom as crisp genre anecdote: costumed types, legible story, warm academic tone, fine detail throughout. Impressionism sacrifices anecdote and detail to light; its customs are contemporary and incidental, never staged as folklore.
- **Fauvism** liberates color from observation entirely — arbitrary green faces, red skies, broad saturated flats with bold outline. Impressionist color, however broken, always reads as a record of natural light at a specific hour; if the hue cannot be justified as perceived daylight, it has left Impressionism for Fauvism.
- A useful extra tell: **Neo-Impressionism/Divisionism** mechanizes the dab into uniform systematic dots; Impressionism's touch stays varied, handwritten and responsive to the motif.

**Quick recognition check:** visible thin broken strokes + colored (violet-blue) shadows + cropped open composition + ordinary modern subject in one consistent natural light + matte unvarnished surface. Five of five: Impressionism.
