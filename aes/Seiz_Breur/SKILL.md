# Seiz Breur

Seiz Breur ("The Seven Brothers"), active in Brittany from 1923 to 1947, was a collective attempt to forge a *modern* Breton national art: wood engravers, poster artists, cabinetmakers, ceramicists, glassmakers and embroiderers who fused Arts and Crafts handcraft ethics, Art Deco geometry and Bauhaus functionalism with Celtic myth, Breton saints' lives and the daily reality of rural and maritime Brittany. The movement explicitly warred on two enemies: commercial tourist kitsch (the mass-produced clichés they called "biniouseries") and sweet Sulpician church art imported from Paris. Everything in the visual language follows from that fight — the style is deliberately stark, carved, emblematic and serious, yet warm with folk narrative.

The surviving visual record is dominated by graphic work: wood engravings, woodcut posters, illustrated book plates and magazine covers (the review *Kornog*, later *Keltia*). The written record documents the same principles extended into furniture, ceramics, stained glass, embroidered banners, liturgical metalwork and architecture; when generating three-dimensional objects in this style, extrapolate from the graphic rules — angular Celtic motif, flat fielded color, honest material surface — rather than inventing a separate object language. Black-and-white archival photographs of the members exist as historical documentation only; they are not evidence of the style's look.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The palette is a printing-shop palette: paper plus a small number of flat inks.

| Role | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground / paper | warm off-white, cream, oat `#f2ead8` – `#e6dcc3` | the default field; aged paper warmth, never cool white |
| Dominant ink | near-black `#0d0d0d` – `#201a17` | carries silhouettes, carved lettering, black fields |
| Sacred / emblem accent | muted ochre-gold `#c9a227` – `#d9b23a` | rays, halos, geometric cover art; flat, never metallic-shiny |
| Blood / slogan accent | brick red to dusty rose `#b33a3a` – `#d98a8a` | banner lettering, horses' trappings, corner motifs |
| Secondary earth | sage green `#9aa88c` – `#b5c2a6`, pumpkin orange `#dd7a26`, umber `#6b4a2f` | occasional third ink in costume fields and backgrounds |

Rule: **one to three flat inks over the paper ground, never more; no gradients, no tonal modeling.** A piece may invert (black ground with cream and gold figures) but stays within the same ink set. Mutedness is constitutional: colors read as hand-mixed print inks on absorbent paper, slightly grainy at the edges.

### Signature motifs, ranked by documentary prominence

1. **Triskelion / triskele and spirals** — the movement's core sign; spirals also appear coiled inside coiffes and as background whirls.
2. **Ermine (hermine) spots** — the Breton moucheture, used as scattered field marks, corner ornaments and border units.
3. **Saw-tooth / zig-zag patterns, peacock feathers, palettes** — the listed characteristic ornaments; saw-teeth edge borders, skirts and ray fields.
4. **Celtic knotwork** — interlace bands framing compositions or clothing hems.
5. **Waves and marine life** — scalloped wave rows, crabs, fish, sails; the coast as identity.
6. **Religious and folklorical imagery** — saints with linear halos, angels, pardons and pilgrimage processions, Breton saints and biblical scenes recast in regional dress.
7. **Nature as emblem** — the gnarled old tree, forest of Brocéliande, stylized foliage cut in silhouette.
8. **Breton costume** — the tall lace coiffe translated into carved white pattern, embroidered vests, wide collars and sweeping scarf shapes.
9. **The book and the slogan** — open books, carved Breton-language captions ("Ar Brezhoneg er Skol", "Komzit brezoneg d'ho pugale"); language revival as subject and as typography.
10. **Heroes and legend** — Nominoë, Anne of Brittany, Arthurian matter, mounted warriors with raised swords; history rendered as myth.

### Materials, texture, line

The defining medium is **relief print**: wood engraving and woodcut. Think in cut planes — black shapes whose interiors are opened by white gouge lines, hatch bundles and knife-flick flecks; edges are faceted and slightly irregular, as if the block resisted the tool. A second legitimate surface mode is the **flat poster / lithograph mode**: clean cut-paper planes, crisp geometric facets, no visible gouge, but still absolutely flat ink. Embroidery and stained-glass variants replace gouge with satin-stitch fields and lead-line contours. Paper grain, slight misregistration and ink starvation at plane edges are welcome; glossy perfection is not.

Line is angular and economical: contours are cut, not drawn — straight chisel segments and tight arcs rather than flowing calligraphy. Figures simplify toward silhouette or mask-like profile; faces are planes with a few carved marks, never modeled flesh.

### Recurring subjects

Saints and blessers; mounted heroes and crowning scenes; mothers in coiffe with children; comforting or grieving pairs in regional dress; peasants, pilgrims and pardon processions; sailors, boats and shore creatures; the old tree and forest undergrowth; open books and teaching scenes; abstract emblem covers (compass stars, ray bundles, geometric armatures) for reviews and manifests; pattern sheets of ermine, wave and knot units.

### Composition and lettering

- **Radial burst**: lances, rays or beams converging on or exploding from a sacred or heroic focal point — light as straight cut lines.
- **Arched portal frame**: compositions shaped like a church window or doorway, with ornamental border bands.
- **Symmetrical emblem layout**: central lozenge, shield or medallion with mirrored corner motifs — folk broadside grammar modernized.
- **Diagonal thrust**: tilted books, rearing horses, spear cones; dynamic but always planar.
- **Full-bleed silhouette**: a black figure or beast against a cream or rayed field, or its inverse.
- **Lettering as image**: hand-carved blocky capitals, wedge-cut counters, uneven baselines, set in bands across top or foot or locked into the composition as shapes; Breton text is typical and authentic. Never a clean digital typeface.

### Light

Light is **linear and symbolic, never atmospheric**. Illumination means radiating white lines on black, or gold lines on black, issuing from halos, swords, stars, books. Outside those bursts the field is evenly lit: no cast shadows, no chiaroscuro, no horizon haze, no time of day. Night pieces are black grounds with cut white stars and rays.

## Mood & Values

Solemn vow and quiet pride. The style carries militant cultural revival — "wind behind, wind against, straight ahead" — expressed as dignity rather than aggression: upright figures, frontal saints, raised swords and open books held like standards. Alongside the militant register run devotional tenderness (mother and child, a hand on a bowed shoulder) and a spare folk playfulness (a crab centered in a lozenge like a coat of arms). Underlying values: authenticity against kitsch, handcraft against mass production, a living evolving culture against museum repetition, and the synthesis of local tradition with European modernism. The mood is always earnest; irony, cuteness and sentimentality are foreign to it.

## Era & Origins

Brittany, France, 1923–1947/48. Founded around Jeanne Malivel (wood engraver and decorator), with René-Yves Creston, Suzanne Candré-Creston, James Bouillé and later Xavier de Langlais, Jorj Robin, Pierre Péron, Robert Micheau-Vernez among others; the core meeting is dated to the 1923 pardon du Folgoët. The group debuted at the 1925 Paris Exposition des Arts Décoratifs (Ty Breiz pavilion), theorized itself in *Kornog* (1928) and *Keltia* (1931), built much of the Brittany Pavilion at the 1937 Paris exposition, and issued its thirteen-point manifesto in 1940. It reacts against tourist cliché, Sulpician religious art and academic stagnation while absorbing Arts and Crafts, Art Deco and Bauhaus currents. It dissolved after WWII amid scrutiny of some members' wartime collaboration — a contested political history that belongs to the movement's biography, not to its visual grammar.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Design as a relief printer: every white line is a gouge out of a black plane; every shape must be cuttable.
2. Limit the ink set: paper ground plus one to three flat inks from the palette table; inversion (black ground) counts as one handling, not extra colors.
3. Let a Breton emblem structure the piece — triskelion, ermine spot, spiral, saw-tooth, knot, wave — as border, corner unit or field pattern.
4. Make light linear: rays and beams of cut lines from the focal point; otherwise flat even illumination.
5. Carve the lettering: blocky angular capitals with knife-cut counters, Breton or French slogans as compositional bands; text is image, not caption.
6. Type the figures: coiffe, embroidered vest, wide collar, silhouette or profile mask; individual likeness is irrelevant, cultural dress is essential when people appear.
7. Keep the surface honest: paper grain, ink flecks, cut-edge irregularity in woodcut mode; crisp flat planes in poster mode. Choose one mode per image.

**Variation axes** — a correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

- *Subject*: saint, legend, historical hero, peasant or sailor daily life, mother and child, pilgrimage, tree or forest, marine creature, abstract geometric emblem, typographic slogan poster, review cover, textile or ceramic pattern, furniture panel design.
- *Composition*: radial burst, arched portal, symmetrical emblem with corner motifs, diagonal montage, full-bleed silhouette, allover pattern field.
- *Color handling*: pure black-on-cream; cream with black plus red; cream with black plus gold; black ground with gold and white; rare sage or orange third ink.
- *Light*: white rays on black, gold rays on black, uniform flat light, inverted silhouette.
- *Mood*: militant, devotional, lyrical-natural, folk-playful.

**Avoid**

- Photorealism, continuous photographic tone, halftone photo texture, bokeh, cinematic lighting, 3D render gloss.
- Gradients, airbrush blending, soft shadows, glowing bloom; light must be lines, not haze.
- Tourist kitsch and "biniouseries": pastel watercolor genre scenes, prettified lace realism, sentimental Breton clichés.
- Sulpician religiosity: soft-modeled saints, porcelain cheeks, sweet naturalism.
- Art Deco luxury codes: chrome, lacquer sheen, metallic gradient gold, skyscrapers, jazz-age glamour.
- Bauhaus internationalism: primary-color grids, geometric sans typography, pure non-representational abstraction with no emblem or narrative.
- Arts and Crafts botanical density: flowing acanthus and vine scrolls, warm crowded polychromy.
- More than about four colors; full CMYK continuous-color printing look.
- Clean vector smoothness where carved angularity belongs (the flat poster mode is exempt from gouge texture but not from cut-plane logic).
- Generic fantasy-Celtic neon, dragons, or medievalist pageantry unrelated to Breton matter.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared ground | Seiz Breur tells itself apart by |
|---|---|---|
| **Art Deco** | Sunbursts, flat planes, angular geometry, stylized figures | Deco's sunburst glorifies speed, machine and metropolitan luxury in metallics and pastels; Seiz Breur's burst is sacred or heroic light around a saint, hero, book or tree, in paper-and-ink earth tones with carved texture, regional dress and Breton-language lettering |
| **Arts and Crafts** | Handcraft ethics, folk revival, anti-industrial stance | A&C ornament is flowing medievalist flora in warm dense polychromy; Seiz Breur ornament is angular Celtic geometry (spiral, triskele, saw-tooth, knot) over stark black-white dominance, with modernist reduction and slogan typography A&C never uses |
| **Bauhaus** | Modernist reduction, functional design ambition, geometric covers | Bauhaus is internationalist, anti-ornament, primary-colored, set in geometric sans, empty of myth; Seiz Breur is always narrative or emblematic — folk-sacred content, carved lettering, earth palette, even on its most abstract review covers |
| **Celtic tourist kitsch / Sulpician art** | Breton and religious subject matter | The style's founding enemies: kitsch is sentimental, cluttered, pastel and naturalistic; Seiz Breur is austere, planar, carved and modern — recognition test: if it looks like a souvenir plate or a plaster saint, it is not Seiz Breur |

Quick recognition checklist: flat cut planes in one to three muted inks on cream or black ground; white gouge lines or crisp cut edges; triskelion, ermine spots, spirals or saw-teeth somewhere in the field or border; linear rays as light; carved blocky capitals, ideally in Breton; figures typed by coiffe and vestment or reduced to silhouette; solemn, earnest mood.
