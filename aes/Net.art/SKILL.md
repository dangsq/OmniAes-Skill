# Net.art

Net.art (also Internet Art or Telematic Art) is the art movement that took the World Wide Web itself as medium and subject from the mid-1990s onward. Artists such as JODI, Olia Lialina, Alexei Shulgin, Vuk Ćosić, Heath Bunting and Mark Napier bypassed galleries entirely and built work out of the web's raw components: browser windows, HTML source, hyperlinks, form widgets, ASCII characters, bot-extracted text. It is explicitly **not one unified look** but a family of registers — interface literalism, terminal/ASCII monochrome, glitch-code chaos, naive pixel GIF color, plain-text conceptualism, and deadpan documentation of physical nodes (flyers, CRT monitors, white-cube installations). What unifies them is stance, not surface: the image must behave like a critical artifact of networked communication, not like a designed picture.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette (rule, not recipe).** Color in net.art is always *default* or *unmanaged* — never a designed harmony. Four acceptable registers:

| Register | Hex range | Typical carrier |
|---|---|---|
| Paper/terminal monochrome | `#FFFFFF` paper with `#000000` serif ink; `#000000` screen with phosphor green `#00FF00`–`#66FF66` or white characters; dithered gray ramps via 50% dither | flyers, ASCII renders, bot text on black |
| System GUI | beveled gray `#C0C0C0`/`#808080`, title-bar navy `#000080`, desktop teal `#008080`, link blue `#0000EE`, visited purple `#551A8B` | browser chrome, form art, windowed screenshots |
| Naive neon | unsaturated-by-nothing primaries: `#FF0000`, `#00FF00`, `#00FFFF`, `#FF00FF`, `#FFFF00` in hard pixel or character cells | pixel GIF scenes, JODI-style chaos grids |
| Corporate-interface white | `#FFFFFF` field with a single intervened logo or skewed element | browser-art interventions on search pages |

Gradients are forbidden except as dithering; any smooth blend breaks the period and the ethos.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the source text documents them.** (1) The web browser window: title bar, URL field, beveled buttons, window-within-window framing. (2) HTML/JavaScript source code as visible texture — lines of markup filling the frame. (3) Hyperlinks: underlined blue text used as narrative structure. (4) Online form widgets: radio buttons, checkboxes, text inputs, submit buttons arranged as composition (form art). (5) Glitches and shredded page fragments: scattered text, code and image shards exposing a page's raw data. (6) ASCII art and character-rendered imagery: portraits, animals, figures built from glyph density, green-on-black or white-on-black. (7) Plain institutional or bot-generated text blocks: sentences set bare on black or white, dot-leader telephone tables, serif announcements. (8) Early website typography: Times/serif body on white, table layouts, leader dots, wireframe globe logos. (9) Digital data recombination: photomosaics, dithered high-contrast triptychs. (10) Physical-node documentation: CRT monitors glowing in dark rooms, sparse gray objects on gallery walls, paper flyers for phone-ins and cybercafes.

**Materials and textures.** Pixel grid and character cell as the fundamental grain; 1-pixel beveled GUI relief; dither and photocopy contrast; scanner-flat paper; CRT scanline glow and bezel plastic in documentation shots. Everything feels either emitted by a screen or photocopied from a printout.

**Composition and lighting.** Frontal, orthographic, screen-as-canvas: the frame is a capture, not a viewpoint. Frame-within-frame (window on desktop field), full-bleed character or pixel grids, table alignment or its deliberate collapse, text blocks treated as solid masses, split panels/triptychs, and — in the documentation register — a single monitor in a dark room or a sparse object on a white wall. Lighting is emissive and shadowless for screen registers, flat scanner light for paper, dim ambient plus monitor glow for installations. There is no sun, no studio light, no atmosphere.

## Mood & Values

Connectivity, interactivity, immateriality. Net.art is deadpan, ironic and anti-commercial: ugliness and defaultness are strategic refusals of the commodity object. It feels bureaucratic-poetic (bot sentences, phone lists), machine-literal (code as portrait), or naively playful (pixel scenes), but always self-aware and critical — of galleries, of corporate control, of surveillance, of the assumption that a webpage is neutral. Process over object; the network over the author.

## Era & Origins

Mid-1990s, Europe and the early web: the term crystallized around 1995 from a garbled email received by Vuk Ćosić; Pit Schultz's 1995 Berlin exhibition gathered Ćosić, Shulgin, JODI and Bunting; Shulgin's Moscow WWWArt Centre (1995) and Rhizome (1996) institutionalized the scene. Landmarks: Bunting's *King's Cross Phone-In* (1994), JODI's `wwwwwwwww.jodi.org` (1995), Lialina's *My Boyfriend Came Back From the War* (1996), Shulgin's *Form Art* (1997), Napier's *The Shredder* (1998), Ćosić's *Deep ASCII* (1998). Roots reach back through telematic art: Ascott's ARTEX collaboration *La Plissure du Texte* (1983), Auber's Minitel *Poietic Generator* (1987), Kac's Videotexto poems. Later practitioners (Kazemi, !Mediengruppe Bitnik, Dullaart, Rozendaal) extend it into bots, darknet commerce and intervened corporate interfaces. It reacts against the physical gallery, the art market, and broadcast one-way media.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. The canvas is a screen, a printout about the network, or a document of a networked object — never a window onto a fictional world.
2. Text is image material: markup, underlined links, form labels, glyph-density ASCII, bare bot sentences.
3. Use default, unmanaged color from one register above; keep it flat and period-correct.
4. Frame frontally; light emissively or by scanner/monitor glow.
5. Break the interface legibly: shred a layout, rotate a corporate logo, misalign a table, substitute characters for pixels — the damage must reveal structure, not merely decorate.
6. Keep the tone deadpan: institutional flyer, machine output, or naive amateur GIF.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any webpage, form, terminal session, ASCII render, pixel scene, bot text, phone-in flyer, intervened search page, or installation document. Composition: full-bleed grid, window-in-window, split panels, text mass, sparse gallery wall, dark desk with CRT. Color handling: terminal monochrome, paper white, system gray, neon chaos, naive pixel saturation, dithered grayscale. Lighting: flat emissive, flat scanned, dim room glow. Mood: bureaucratic deadpan, chaotic, naive-playful, critical-melancholic. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Modern UI (rounded corners, glassmorphism, material design, minimal sans-serif dashboards); smartphone/app interfaces; emoji and contemporary icon sets; smooth gradients, soft shadows, bokeh; cinematic or natural lighting; unmediated photorealistic people or landscapes; hand-drawn or painterly marks; organic textures; contemporary social-media feeds; high-gloss 3D renders; liminal dreamlike photography; spiritual or mystical iconography.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Demoscene** shows off real-time virtuosity — smooth gradient fills, scrolling credit text, polished pixel/3D intros. Net.art never virtuosic; it embraces default ugliness and keeps the browser/HTML context visible.
- **Glitch Art** isolates corruption as a beautiful image (datamosh, pixel-sorted photographs). Net.art's glitch lives *inside* web artifacts — code on screen, shredded HTML in a window — and means "revealing the constructed interface," usually monochrome-green or character-cell chaos with legible text fragments.
- **Programmer Art** is sincere naive output of utility software. Net.art may look equally naive but is intentional and self-referential: look for interface self-awareness, conceptual text, or intervention on a known web object.
- **Weirdcore** uses low-res liminal photographs with eerie personal captions and dream-dread. Net.art has no liminal spaces; its bare text is institutional or bot-generated, set on flat black/white, and its imagery is interface-literal rather than photographic-surreal.
- **Xpiritualism** pursues digital transcendence — auras, crystals, Y2K chrome mysticism, gradient glow. Net.art is materialist about code and anti-transcendent; its palette is default, its mood critical.
- **Old Web** recreates period homepages sincerely; net.art *intervenes* on them — the critical gesture (shredding, mirroring, rotating, form-abuse) is the tell.

*Evidence note:* the physical-installation and paper-flyer registers appear in documentation photographs rather than as the artworks' native surface; treat them as a secondary, contextual register, while the screen-native registers above carry the style's core identity.
