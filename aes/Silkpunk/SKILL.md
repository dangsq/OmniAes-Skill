# Silkpunk

Silkpunk is the East Asian answer to steampunk: an alternate-history technology aesthetic in which the machine age arrives through bamboo, paper, silk, lacquer and bronze rather than iron, coal and steam. The term was coined by the author Ken Liu for his 2015 novel *The Grace of Kings* (the Dandelion Dynasty series), describing "a blend of science fiction and fantasy that draws inspiration from classical East Asian antiquity," with a technology vocabulary based on materials historically important to East Asia. Visual art adopted the word more loosely than the literature: any steampunk-grade machine dressed in silk, timber structures and East Asian ornament tends to be labelled silkpunk. Its visual lineage reaches back through *Avatar: The Last Airbender* (2005) and forward through *The Legend of Korra*, Ōkami, *Tears of the Kingdom*, *Nine Sols*, the Xianzhou Luofu of *Honkai: Star Rail*, and Love, Death & Robots' "Good Hunting." Note one boundary from the source definition: Ken Liu's literary version includes Pacific seafaring materials (coconut, feathers, coral), but visual-art usage does **not** treat Pacific seafaring elements as part of the look — the aesthetic reads as continental, courtly and river-valley Chinese, with broader East Asian inflections acceptable.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** The infobox keys are gold, bronze and red, and the corpus obeys: a dominant pair of metallic gold-brass and lacquer vermilion, laid over a ground of ink black, xuan-paper parchment, or misty jade-teal. Cool moonlit blues appear only as night keys; silk pinks and the occasional flat magenta appear only as cover-grade background fields. Saturation stays high on lacquer and gold, low on mist and distance.

**Signature motifs, ranked by textual authority.** (1) Pagodas — tiered towers, sometimes improbably mechanized. (2) Mechanical objects of wood, bamboo and paper: automata, wooden gear trains, paper-and-rib sails. (3) Silk fabrics — robes, canopies, kite skins. (4) East Asian timber architecture and ornament generally: upturned eaves, dougong brackets, lattice windows, covered bridges. (5) Compasses and gunpowder devices — rockets, fire-lances, signal fireworks — as the named inventions of the genre. (6) Image-corroborated extensions of the same grammar: hanging red paper lanterns, tassels and Chinese knotwork, phoenix-crown headdresses and court regalia, calligraphic banners and red seal stamps chopped into the corner of illustrations, kites and silk-skinned airships and mechanical birds, bronze ritual vessels and porcelain. (7) Occasional: feather plumes and trained birds (an echo of the literary feather vocabulary), moon and night skies, walking or legged architecture, canal-side waterfront cities.

**Materials and textures.** Everything reads hand-finished: visible wood grain and joinery without nails, segmented bamboo canes, paper and silk stretched over ribbing so the frame shows through, glossy lacquer, brass and bronze with a soft rubbed patina, twisted rope and cord, tassel fringe, porcelain glaze, feather down. Metal appears as fittings, hinges, cog rims and breastplates — never as boiler plate. Surfaces are carved, painted, knotted or embroidered; nothing is extruded, riveted or injection-moulded.

**Recurring subjects.** Automata living ordinary lives (attendants, walkers of dogs, throne-machines); regalia still-lifes of crowns, headdresses and ceremonial harness; terraced mountain cities and fortress monasteries; crowded interior halls and airship holds strung with lanterns; flying machines over water and mist; character-design lineups of courtiers, engineers and soldiers; annotated schematics of dragon-shaped devices; emblem-like book-cover compositions.

**Composition tendencies.** Vertical, icon-like symmetry for regalia and covers, often centred on a dark field with a glowing halo behind; multi-plane atmospheric depth for landscapes, with mist separating ridge, pagoda and sky; cutaway interiors where hanging lights lead the eye through timber machinery; flat, evenly lit design-sheet layouts on plain ground with a red seal stamp; parchment schematics in orthographic line; processional horizontals for carts, convoys and street scenes. Foreground framing silhouettes — eaves, branches, rigging — are common.

**Lighting tendencies.** Warm point sources: lantern glow pooling in dim wooden interiors. Backlit halos that rim ornate objects against black. Moonlit cool exteriors punctured by sparse warm windows. Hazy gold dusk over water and scaffolding. Flat, shadowless illustration light on design sheets. Mist acts as the universal diffuser and depth cue. (The wiki text is terse on light and composition; these are distilled from the corpus as tendencies, not dogma.)

## Mood & Values

Silkpunk feels ingenious, warm and courtly at once. Its machines are craftsman's objects — extensions of joinery, weaving and papermaking — so technology signals continuity with antiquity rather than rupture against it. There is play in it: birds perched on automata, kites overhead, dogs on leashes beside brass legs. There is also melancholy distance: mist-wrapped peaks, moonlit fleets, ruins of wooden grandeur. The values expressed are reverence for material and handwork, elegance as engineering, and a futures-past optimism in which the organic world supplies the machine.

## Era & Origins

Coined in the 2010s (term attached to *The Grace of Kings*, 2015) by Ken Liu, who insisted it not be confused with Chinese science fiction generally. It reacts to steampunk by swapping Victorian iron-and-steam for East Asian organic engineering, and it inherits a visual lineage older than the word (*Avatar: The Last Airbender*, 2005). Literary canon: the Dandelion Dynasty books, *Celestial Matters*, *The Black Tides of Heaven*, *The Tea Master and the Detective*, *Cinder*. Screen and game canon as listed above.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Organic machine rule: every device resolves into bamboo, timber, paper, silk, rope, bronze; power comes from wind, kite-lift, springwork, muscle or gunpowder — never coal steam.
2. Timber architecture rule: roofs curve upward at the eaves; structure is post-and-bracket, tiered, terraced.
3. Ornament-as-culture rule: finish machines and figures with tassels, knots, frog closures, banners, calligraphy, seal stamps, headdress regalia.
4. Palette rule: gold-brass plus lacquer red over ink, parchment or jade mist; cool blue only for night, magenta only as a flat field.
5. Light rule: lantern points, backlit halos, or mist-diffused dusk; keep warmth inside the frame even in night keys.
6. Craft surface rule: painterly concept-art, flat cover illustration, ink line, or sepia schematic — always a hand-made look.
7. Scale play rule: the same grammar works at compass scale, human scale (automata), and architectural scale (walking pagodas, airship fleets).

**Variation axes.** Subject may range from a single regalia object to a city panorama to a schematic page. Composition may be symmetrical icon, atmospheric landscape, cutaway interior, flat design sheet, or processional. Lighting may be lantern night, halo backlight, moonlit cool, flat sheet light, or gold dusk. Color handling may run saturated lacquer-on-black, parchment-with-red-seal, misty teal-green, night blue with red accents, or cover-grade magenta. Render may be painterly, vector-flat, inked, or blueprint-sepia. Mood may tilt playful, courtly, melancholy or heroic. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a silkpunk scene of, say, a paper-winged ferry crossing a jade river in fog is fully valid even though no reference shows it.

**Avoid.**
- Steampunk hardware and dress: iron boilers, riveted steel plate, coal smoke, steam plumes as the power signature, brass goggles, top hats, corsets, Victorian brick and clockwork pocket-watches.
- Sinofuturist or cyberpunk signals: neon, chrome, holograms, LEDs, screens, glass megatowers, cables, drones.
- Pure historical reenactment with no speculative machine (period Tang or Ming painting); silkpunk always inserts an anachronistic device.
- Pacific seafaring material culture as subject: outriggers, coconut fibre, coral, island maritime kit (the visual usage explicitly excludes these); East Asian canals and harbours as setting are fine.
- European chinoiserie: blue-and-white willow-pattern dominance, porcelain figurines in rococo rooms.
- Dieselpunk scrap, rust-belt post-apocalypse, modern plastics, photoreal product-render sheen.
- European medieval or gothic architecture: stone castles, spires, gargoyles.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Overlap | Silkpunk tell |
|---|---|---|
| Steampunk | Gears, brass, alternate-history analog machines | Construction material is bamboo/paper/silk/timber; palette adds lacquer red and jade; roofs are pagoda tiers; ornament is tassels, knots, calligraphy; power is wind, kite, gunpowder — no steam, no Victorian dress |
| Sinofuturism | Chinese signifiers fused with speculative technology | Silkpunk is pre-electronic and handcrafted: no neon, chrome, screens or megacity; it imagines a futures-past, not a digital future |
| Tang Dynasty / historical Chinese aesthetics | Silk, pagodas, gold-and-red court regalia | Silkpunk always inserts speculative engineering — automata, airships, legged architecture, schematics — and renders in concept-art or cover illustration idioms rather than period painting modes |

Quick check: wooden gears plus silk plus pagoda equals silkpunk; brass goggles plus steam equals steampunk; neon hanzi plus chrome equals Sinofuturism; regalia and pagodas with no machine at all equals a historical dynasty aesthetic.
