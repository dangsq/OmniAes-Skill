# Goth

Goth is first and foremost a music subculture — gothic rock, dark wave, deathrock, cold wave, all descending from British post-punk — and its visual language grew out of club nights rather than fashion houses. The look fuses three lineages: 19th-century Victorian mourning attire, BDSM/fetish wear filtered through punk boutiques, and the androgynous theatricality of glam rock, all sunk into black. What holds the imagery together is not any single garment but a consistent grammar: black as the ground of the image, pale skin and silver as its only bright notes, hair with deliberate architecture, and a mood of melancholy that is pleasurable rather than despairing — gloom that dances.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

Black is the substrate, not an accent: near-blacks and charcoals (`#0a0a0a`–`#1c1c1c`) should occupy the majority of any frame, with grey midtones carrying monochrome work. Accents come from a small, disciplined jewel set — deep purple (`#2b1b3d`–`#4a2666`), crimson and oxblood (`#5e0b15`–`#8a0303`), emerald and forest green (`#0b3d2e`–`#14543c`; documented in the style's canon but rarer in practice than purple or red). Pale values appear only as bone-white and ivory (`#e8e4da`) in skin, lace, shirt collars and bone jewelry, plus cool silver metal (`#b9b9b9`). A single shock of punk-residue hair color — acid green, yellow, flame red — is acceptable as one loud note over black cloth. The rule: at most one or two jewel accents per image; white never floods the background, it punctuates.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the culture documents them)

1. **The head-to-toe black ensemble** — velvets, lace, fishnets, leather "tinged with scarlet or purple."
2. **Silver occult and religious jewelry** — ankh pendants, crucifixes, skull rings, bone necklaces, heavy chains, worn in layers.
3. **Fishnet** — on legs or arms, frequently ripped, worn under torn layers or alone.
4. **Corsetry as outerwear** — tightly laced, over shirts or bare skin; wide belts and buckles at the waist.
5. **Victorian mourning vocabulary** — high collars, lace jabots and cuffs, long black skirts, veils, cameo brooches (strongest at the romantic end).
6. **Fetish hardware** — studded or hand-painted leather jackets, leather gloves, precarious stilettos, pointed winklepicker boots.
7. **Teased, backcombed hair** — the deathhawk or shag: enormous volume at the crown, jagged silhouette, often falling across the face.
8. **Graphic eye makeup** — heavy kohl liner, smudged or drawn into sharp geometric arcs; dark or blackened lips; pale or fully white face paint at the deathrock extreme.
9. **Horror and vampire props** — bats, candles, crosses, coffin-dark interiors; the vampire pallor as complexion ideal.
10. **The club context** — smoke, cramped interiors, flash-lit dancefloors (the Batcave lineage); gig stages and microphones.

### Materials and textures

Matte black cloth that swallows light: velvet, wool, cotton jersey. Openwork textures that catch it: fishnet mesh, lace, torn hosiery. Hard surfaces: matte and painted leather, rubber or latex at the fetish end, silver and bone metalwork. Satin or silk shirts appear occasionally as a glam residue. Texture stacking is characteristic — at least two of fishnet, lace, velvet, leather, silver should coexist in a fully dressed figure.

### Subjects, composition, lighting

Subjects range from a single androgynous figure in nothing but a black sweater and enormous hair, to full Victorian mourning regalia in a forest or graveyard, to informal groups of friends in a club, on a street, or in an ordinary wood-panelled living room. Any gender presentation is legitimate; fluid dress and makeup on men is structural to the scene, not exceptional. Composition favors vertical portraits, head-and-shoulders or three-quarter, with either a confrontational direct gaze or an averted, introspective one; group shots are casually arranged, slightly awkward snapshots rather than styled editorials. Lighting tendencies: high-contrast monochrome with deep shadow and hot highlights; flat on-camera flash in color snapshots; soft overcast or window grey for pensive portraits; low candle or chandelier light for romantic work; club strobe through smoke. Glossy, even, flattering beauty light is foreign to the style.

### Medium

Grainy 35mm film, push-processed black and white, and faded 1980s–90s consumer color (warm cast, softened saturation, slight broadcast blur) are the native treatments. Clean digital rendering is acceptable at the modern minimal end only if the palette discipline holds.

## Mood & Values

Melancholy as pleasure. The culture values individualism, creativity, intellectualism, introspection, dark romanticism and anti-conformity; its relationship to death, the occult and horror is aesthetic, not doctrinal. There is a rejection of forced cheerfulness — no beaming commercial smiles — but not of joy: the dancefloor is central, and movement, smoke and crowded bodies are as authentically goth as the solitary pensive pose. A wry, self-aware camp (kitsch horror, in-joke theatricality) is permitted. Androgyny and sexual ambiguity are treated as ordinary, not provocative.

## Era & Origins

Emergent 1979–1984 in the UK: post-punk (Joy Division, Siouxsie and the Banshees, early Bauhaus) crystallizing into gothic rock with Bauhaus's "Bela Lugosi's Dead," concurrent with London club nights like the Batcave (1982). It reacts against post-hippie drab denim and mainstream brightness, and against first-wave punk's blunt aggression by preferring atmosphere. Through the 1980s it spread into American deathrock, the German Grufti scene and its Leipzig festivals, Spanish and French cold wave, and Japanese pre-Visual Kei goth. Modern descendants (nu-goth, corporate goth) simplify the silhouette but keep the palette.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Black is the ground of the image; everything else punctuates it.
- Accent discipline: one jewel tone, or one shock hair color, at a time.
- The face is the focal point: pale matte skin, dark architecturally drawn eyes.
- Hair has structure: backcombed volume, asymmetry, jagged silhouette — never sleek salon hair.
- Stack at least two signature textures (fishnet + lace, leather + velvet, silver over black).
- Keep handmade imperfection: ripped tights, smudged liner, DIY studs, hand-painted leather.
- Light for mood, not flattery: chiaroscuro, flat flash, candle glow, grain.
- Pose carries introspection, theatrical aloofness, or dancefloor motion — never cheerleader energy.
- Default to film emulation: monochrome grain or faded period color cast.

**Variation axes.** A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. Subject may vary from minimal (plain black top, hair, makeup) to maximal mourning regalia; from solo portrait to couple to crowded club group; from studio seamless to street, domestic interior, graveyard, forest or stone architecture as backdrop. Composition may vary from tight headshot to full-length environmental to awkward snapshot crop. Lighting may vary from soft grey studio to harsh flash to candlelit low-key to strobe-and-smoke. Color handling may vary from pure monochrome through muted color to faded warm snapshot to a single vivid hair accent. Era register may vary from 1979–84 batcaver through 90s to contemporary minimal nu-goth (black tank, choker, occult sigil print). Mood may vary from pensive to theatrical to camp to euphoric-on-the-dancefloor.

**Avoid**

- Neon or UV color, goggles, cyberlocks, gas masks, techwear straps (cybergoth/industrial).
- Pastel frills, bonnets, crinoline bell silhouettes, doll blush (Gothic Lolita).
- Glitter bodysuits, candy satin, sequins, bright platform boots (glam rock).
- Denim vests with patches, band t-shirts with jeans and sneakers, sportswear, caps (metalhead/casual).
- Beige minimalism, Scandinavian warmth, clean commercial beauty lighting, airbrushed skin.
- Multiple simultaneous jewel tones or rainbow palettes; white or bright backgrounds flooding the frame.
- Medieval cathedrals, armor or heraldry as the *subject* (stone as backdrop is fine).
- Brass goggles, clockwork corsetry (steampunk).
- Sleek, gelled, or neatly blown hair; sunny daylight leisure settings; broad grinning cheer.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Telling difference |
|---|---|
| **Deathrock** | Heavier white face paint, rawer ripped DIY clothing, horror-punk kitsch (skeletons, drips), faster punk posture; goth trad keeps velvet/lace/fetish finish and androgynous glamour. Heavy overlap — deathrock is a goth subgenre, so treat paint thickness and punk rawness as the dial. |
| **Glam Rock** | Glitter and saturated satin lead the palette, peacock posing, sci-fi camp; goth borrows the androgyny but keeps black leading and the mood funerary rather than celebratory. |
| **Gothic (literary/architectural)** | Cathedrals, pointed arches, medieval statuary, no fishnets, no teased hair, no club context; goth subculture always carries post-punk hair, makeup and fetish materials even in Victorian dress. |
| **Gothic Lolita** | Doll-like precision: crinoline bell silhouette, bonnets, neat curled hair, modest frilled blouses, porcelain doll blush; goth is adult, androgynous, DIY-messy, fetish-inflected. |
| **Industrial / Rivethead** | Military surplus, utilitarian straps, masks and goggles, cropped hair, neon accents, circuitry motifs; goth keeps romantic materials (lace, velvet, fishnet) and silver occult jewelry, with big soft-teased hair. |
| **Metalhead** | Long natural hair, patched denim, band tees, little or no makeup; goth defaults to makeup and constructed hair volume. |

Evidence note: emerald green is canonically listed but scarcely visible in surviving imagery; color snapshot mode rests on a handful of period amateur photos; substyles like corporate or military goth are wardrobe variants rather than separate visual languages, and should be rendered as palette-consistent adjustments, not new aesthetics.
