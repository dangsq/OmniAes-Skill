# Neoclassical Pomo

## Overview

Neoclassical Pomo is the classical mask of Postmodernism: an architectural and graphic design language prominent from the late 1970s through the 1990s that quotes Roman and Greek elements — pediments, columns, arches, obelisks, symmetrical facades — not reverently but with the ironic, playful, eclectic spirit of postmodern theory. Where modernism stripped the building to a glass box, this style piles on history as signage: a pediment becomes a freestanding screen wall, a column becomes a pedestal for a tape drive, a colonnade becomes the frame of a department store. It flourished in corporate offices, civic centers, large complexes and postmodern public plazas, and it leaked into graphic and digital art — early vaporwave-adjacent, computer-generated imagery of floating digital columns and classical statues. Its built touchstones include the M2 Building in Japan, the Piazza d'Italia in Italy, and Les Espaces d'Abraxas in France; its named practitioners include Hugh Newell Jacobsen, Robert A.M. Stern, Michael Graves, Ricardo Bofill, and Charles Moore. The style's essential act is double-coding: every frame contains both an antique quotation and a late-twentieth-century material or object that undercuts it.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

White is the style's declared key colour and its grounding rule: ivory, bone and gallery white dominate walls, columns and ceilings, reading as factory-new rather than aged. Around that ground sit three sanctioned families: stone neutrals (greige granite, warm beige marble, pale travertine tones, and occasionally a heavy terracotta or oxblood massing); bright pastels (blush, peach, powder blue, sage, pale yellow) applied to walls, ceilings and painted classical elements; and synthetic accents — neon magenta and coral tubing, reflective blue-grey curtain-wall glass, and black-and-white checkerboard flooring. The graphic branch adds an airbrushed dusk gradient: deep zenith blue falling through coral and magenta to a warm horizon orange.

The rule, not any single instance: a light, white-led field, one stone or pastel family carrying the mass, and at most one synthetic accent doing the irony.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how strongly the style documents them)

1. **Columns and colonnades**, stylized and exaggerated: colossal engaged columns on facades, stubby oversized columns in lobbies, single freestanding columns used as sculpture or pedestal.
2. **Pediments and aedicules**, especially freestanding: a pedimented portal or screen wall detached from any building, framing sky or void like a stage prop.
3. **Axial symmetry**: mirrored wings, centered portals, enfilades running through colonnades.
4. **Exaggerated arches**: stretched, flattened, or multiplied beyond structural sense.
5. **Obelisks and faux-ruin fragments**: classical pieces placed as composed objects — but pristine, lit, and staged next to modern furniture, never decayed.
6. **Neon on classical form**: tubing tracing capitals, entablatures, or sculptural columns.
7. **Material clash**: marble and stone looks diffused with glass, neon, synthetic finishes, bright pastels.
8. **Floating digital columns and classical statues** in computer-generated or airbrushed graphic space.
9. **Dramatic lobbies and rotundas**: domed halls, circular fountains, coffered ceilings, checkerboard floors, with modern furniture and display fixtures inserted.
10. **Public plazas and complexes**: paved axial courts, manicured geometric lawns, monumental blank walls punctured by small classical portals.

Secondary, image-observed but not text-named, and therefore optional: coffered ceilings and domes with oculus light, checkerboard marble floors, rotunda fountains, capitalized serif signage set into entablatures, and the flat, shadowless surface quality of marker-and-airbrush architectural renderings.

### Materials and textures

Polished marble and granite that read machined rather than quarried — uniform grain, crisp arrises, no patina; smooth painted stucco and lacquer in pastel; curved and flat reflective curtain-wall glass; neon glass tubing; synthetic laminates and polished plaster; glossy floors that mirror columns and light coves. In the graphic branch, the "material" is the airbrush and early raster CGI: soft gradient skies, smooth plastic highlights on hardware, matte poster typography in serif capitals.

### Recurring subjects

Corporate office and civic facades; hotel, mall and museum lobbies; boutique and brand-store interiors with classical kit around modern merchandise; fountain rotundas; public plazas and monumental housing or office complexes; freestanding portal sculptures; product advertisements placing computers and equipment on classical pedestals; posters and cover art of columns, statues and comets in gradient dusk.

### Composition and lighting

Composition defaults to the axis: frontal flat elevations treated like theater flats, one-point perspectives down colonnades and corridors, low-angle shots that let colossal columns crop out of frame, and centered freestanding pediments framing a slice of sky. Scale jumps are structural to the style — a tiny pediment against a giant facade, a giant column in a small room. Spaces are empty or nearly so: blurred shoppers, a few distant figures, or nobody at all. Lighting is even and theatrical: diffused daylight from skylights and clerestories, shadowless rendering light, warm atrium glow held against a dusk exterior, neon as the sole source in night interiors, and airbrushed gradient skies in graphic work. Hard directional sun with long natural shadows is uncommon; the light behaves like set lighting.

## Mood & Values

Ironic monumentality: grandeur delivered with a wink. The style expresses corporate and civic optimism of the late twentieth century — history as brand asset, culture as interior decoration — alongside a genuine theatrical pleasure in space. Its emptiness gives it a second, cooler register: sterile, dreamlike, faintly eerie, like a set waiting for actors. Values encoded: wit over correctness, commerce over purity, memory over progress, and the conviction that a column can be a joke and a monument at the same time.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in the late 1970s and dominant through the 1990s, in reaction against modernist orthodoxy and its glass-and-steel reductionism. It belongs to postmodernism's broader return to ornament, quotation and double-coding, and it coincides with the rise of consumer spectacle — malls, flagships, casino interiors — and with the first wave of commercial computer graphics, which supplied its poster-and-advertisement branch and makes it a distant ancestor of vaporwave. Built anchors: M2 Building (Japan), Piazza d'Italia (Italy), Les Espaces d'Abraxas (France).

## Generation Guidance

### Principles

1. **Double-code every frame.** Include at least one classical element (column, pediment, arch, obelisk, statue) and at least one anachronistic modern element (curtain-wall glass, neon, track lighting, laminate fixtures, computer hardware, serif commercial signage).
2. **Break proportion on purpose.** Columns too colossal or too stubby, pediments detached from walls, arches stretched past sense. Historical correctness is the setup; the distortion is the joke.
3. **Symmetry first.** Axial plans, centered portals, mirrored wings. Asymmetry is allowed only when the asymmetric thing is itself a composed classical fragment.
4. **Keep everything new.** Polished, painted, factory-fresh stone and plaster. No weathering, no patina, no repairs.
5. **Hold the palette discipline.** White or ivory ground; one stone-neutral or pastel family carrying the mass; at most one synthetic accent (neon tube, pastel pop, or dusk gradient in graphic work).
6. **Light as stagecraft.** Diffused, even, shadowless, or theatrical glow; gradient airbrush skies in the graphic branch. Avoid hard documentary sunlight with gritty contrast.
7. **People as scale props.** Absent, tiny, or motion-blurred. Never portraits, never crowds.
8. **Medium is free.** Photograph, marker-and-airbrush architectural rendering, or early raster CGI advertisement are all authentic carriers; rendering flatness and slight artificiality are features, not faults.

### Variation axes

- **Subject matter:** office or civic facade; lobby, rotunda or fountain hall; museum, boutique or brand-store interior; public plaza or monumental complex; freestanding portal or column sculpture; product advertisement on classical pedestals; poster or cover art with floating columns, statues, comets.
- **Composition:** flat frontal elevation; one-point enfilade; low-angle colossal crop; centered void-framing portal; floating collage in gradient space.
- **Lighting:** skylight diffusion; even rendering light; warm atrium against dusk; neon-lit night; airbrushed sunset.
- **Color handling:** all-white sterility; pastel polychromy; granite-plus-glass; terracotta massing with pale portal; dusk gradient with neon.
- **Mood:** corporate solemnity; playful whimsy; eerie emptiness; consumer glamour; techno-nostalgia.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A white boutique interior with one oversized pediment and a neon accent, or a CGI poster of an obelisk over a gradient sea, are equally valid even if neither resembles a known building.

### Avoid

- Weathering, cracks, moss, ivy, water stains, broken members — decay belongs to ruins modes, not here.
- Earnest, archaeologically correct, proportionally restrained classical orders (that is revivalism, not postmodern quotation).
- Pure modernist glass boxes or minimalist interiors with no classical citation.
- 1970s earth tones: harvest gold, avocado, dark wood paneling, shag, brick rusticity.
- Restrained corporate wood-and-leather sophistication with only token ornament.
- Baroque curl, Rococo gilding, Gothic pointed forms — the grammar is Greek and Roman.
- Parametric curves, fluid contemporary facades, exposed steel structure as the hero.
- Crowds, candid street life, traffic, cluttered desks.
- Cyberpunk cues: rain-slick streets, holograms, chrome typography, glitch artifacts, scanlines.
- Grime-heavy photorealism: lens dirt, heavy vignettes, desaturated teal-orange grading.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Against Neoclassicism:** the revival is earnest, proportional, and aged — correct orders, patinated stone, warm natural light, no glass curtain walls, no neon, no pastels, no scale jokes. Neoclassical Pomo exaggerates proportion, mixes anachronistic materials, and keeps every surface new; its stone looks manufactured.
- **Against Pomo Faux Ruins:** both stage classical fragments, but Faux Ruins stages decay — broken pediments, erosion, overgrowth, melancholy. Neoclassical Pomo's fragments are pristine, lit, painted, and placed beside modern furniture and signage; the mood is wit, not mourning.
- **Against Frasurbane:** Frasurbane is restrained late-century corporate urbanity — smoked glass, wood veneer, leather, muted browns and greys, understated good taste. Neoclassical Pomo is loud about its history: overt columns, pediments, pastels and neon where Frasurbane would place a veneer panel.
- **Against Hellenic:** Hellenic is an earnest Mediterranean ideal — white marble in strong sun, correct Greek orders, olive groves and sea light, no irony and no synthetic materials. Neoclassical Pomo may also be white, but pairs that white with curtain walls, checkerboard floors, neon tubing and proportion jokes.
- **Against Silicon Dreams:** both share early-CGI gradient skies and smooth raster surfaces. The test is citation: if the dreamscape contains columns, pediments, obelisks or statues, it is Neoclassical Pomo's graphic branch; if it is purely chrome, grids and techno-futurist forms with no classical grammar, it is Silicon Dreams.

**Evidence note:** the graphic and digital branch is documented in the style's own definition but survives in comparatively few artifacts (advertisements and posters), so treat its motif set — floating columns, statues, gradient dusk, serif caption type — as a real but narrower register than the architectural one. Checkerboard floors, coffered domes and rotunda fountains recur across interior evidence without being named in the defining text; use them as supporting, not defining, features.
