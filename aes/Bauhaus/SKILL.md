# Bauhaus

The Bauhaus was a German art and design school (Weimar 1919–1925, Dessau 1925–1932, Berlin 1932–1933) founded by Walter Gropius, and by extension the visual language it exported to the whole twentieth century. Its founding ambition was the *Gesamtkunstwerk*: dissolving the border between fine art and applied art so that painting, weaving, metalwork, joinery, typography, and architecture obey one discipline. That discipline is summarized as "form follows function" — the object's purpose generates its shape, and ornament is deleted rather than designed. Unlike the Arts and Crafts movement before it, the Bauhaus embraced the machine: its ideals are rationality, simplicity, social purpose, and mass-producibility. The school's preliminary course (*Vorkurs*) drilled materials, color theory, and composition before any specialization, which is why a Bauhaus chair, a Bauhaus rug, and a Bauhaus poster share one grammar of primitive shapes and restrained color. Faculty and alumni — Gropius, Mies van der Rohe, Kandinsky, Klee, Breuer, Moholy-Nagy, Anni Albers — carried this grammar worldwide after the Nazis closed the school as "degenerate," seeding the International Style and modern industrial design (its commercial afterlife runs through Knoll and Braun).

## Visual Identity

### Color

The infobox fixes the core: primary red, yellow, blue, plus black, white, gray. In practice the neutrals are warm — lithograph paper and plaster read as cream, not optical white — and the weaving workshop legitimately extends the primaries into a secondary accent set used in small, flat areas.

Rule: primaries plus neutrals carry the compositional weight; extended accents appear as bounded flat patches (a rug rectangle, a table top, a mosaic square), never as atmosphere, gradient, or wash. A muted, grayed-down mosaic mode (Klee-style checkerboards of desaturated greens, reds, blues) is a documented secondary color regime.

### Signature motifs and objects, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Primitive geometry** — circle, square, triangle, listed first among key motifs. They appear as composition (abstract paintings built from ovals, arcs, and crossing bars), as poster shapes, as rug blocks, and as object logic: a hemispherical teapot body, a spherical glass lamp shade, a circular arc handle.
2. **Clean lines and absence of ornament** — the silhouette is the decoration. Exposed frames, visible joints, continuous tubular curves; nothing applied, carved, or printed onto a surface for decoration alone.
3. **Sans-serif typography** — grotesque capitals set in tight blocks, aligned to bars and rules, sometimes rotated on a diagonal; type behaves as geometric material in posters.
4. **Abstract forms** — non-objective painting and drawing: floating ovals, ruled lines, grids of squares; geometry as subject rather than decoration.
5. **Industrial materials** — steel, glass, concrete named in the infobox; in the object record this means chrome-plated tubular steel, clear borosilicate glass, nickel and brass, solid silver, lacquered and solid wood.

Recurring object families (corroborating evidence): cantilever and club chairs in chrome tubing with canvas, leather, cane, or woven-strap seats; slat chairs of oak with taut fabric straps; nesting and stacking tables in graduated sizes; woven rugs, carpets, and gobelins with horizontal banding and knotted fringe; glass-and-metal table lamps and tea services; metal teapots and candle branches reduced to hemisphere, cone, and rod; white cubic school buildings with horizontal ribbon windows; lithograph exhibition posters.

### Materials and textures

Chrome plating with a single soft specular streak; clear glass showing its own thickness and refraction; nickel, brass, and silver with satin rather than mirror polish; ebony and oak with honest, straight grain; matte lacquer on table tops; wool and hemp weave with visible weft, selvedge, and hand-knotted fringe; cream lithograph paper with flat ink and slight tooth; white plaster and concrete. Texture is always a byproduct of material and process — weave, bend, weld, cast — never applied pattern.

### Composition and lighting

Composition is orthogonal order with one deliberate break: a grid, a stack of horizontal bands, or a modular nesting series, interrupted by a single diagonal, an off-center primary block, or a cantilever curve. Balance is asymmetric; negative space is generous and structural. Product and object views are frontal or slight three-quarter on a seamless pale ground; textiles are shown full-face as flat fields; posters organize type and shape along invisible grids; architecture is read in straight-on or low oblique elevations. Lighting is diffuse and even: shadowless studio light with a soft contact shadow under objects, overcast or north daylight on buildings, and no modeled light at all in graphic work — color arrives as flat plane, not as illumination.

## Mood & Values

Rational optimism and democratic intent: design as social tool, beauty as correctness rather than richness. The mood is clinical but not cold — the weaving workshop's pinks, teals, and fringes, and the playful color mosaics of the painting masters, keep a pedagogical warmth inside the grid. Values expressed: unity of art and industry, truth to materials, reproducibility, clarity, and the belief that a chair, a lamp, and a poster can share one ethical standard.

## Era & Origins

Weimar, Germany, 1919; closed under Nazi pressure in Berlin, 1933. Three phases under three directors — Gropius (Weimar, early Dessau), Hannes Meyer, Mies van der Rohe — shifting emphasis from craft-unified Gesamtkunstwerk toward industrial protocol and architecture. It reacts against nineteenth-century historicist ornament and against Arts and Crafts' rejection of the machine, while absorbing the abstraction of Constructivism and De Stijl and redirecting it into functional objects and teaching. Its diaspora produced the International Style in architecture and the baseline of modern graphic, furniture, and industrial design.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
- **Function first.** Name what the object does; let structure generate form. Expose the frame, show the joint, delete everything else.
- **Reduce to primitives.** Circles, squares, triangles, straight lines; the continuous cantilever curve is the only licensed flourish, and it is structural.
- **Budget the palette.** Primaries plus black, white, gray, cream carry the image; teal, pink, orange, ochre enter only as small flat patches in textile or lacquer contexts.
- **Color as plane.** Matte, flat, bounded areas. No gradients, glows, or atmospheric color.
- **Honest materials.** Chrome reads as chrome, glass as glass, wool as wool; no faux finishes, no veneer imitation.
- **Grid with one break.** Orthogonal order plus exactly one diagonal, off-center block, or curve to create tension.
- **Type as geometry.** If lettering appears: grotesque sans capitals, tight blocks, aligned to bars and rules; never script, never serif display.
- **Even light.** Diffuse studio light on a seamless pale ground with soft contact shadows, soft daylight on architecture, or fully flat graphic light in poster work.

Variation axes:
- **Subject:** any functional object or abstract composition within the school's unity of arts — seating, tables, storage, lighting, tableware, textiles (rugs, hangings, upholstery), posters and typographic layouts, abstract painting and drawing, architecture exterior and interior, household metalware, candle branches, toys. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.
- **Composition:** frontal elevation, three-quarter product view, nested or stacked modular series, full-face flat textile field, diagonal poster arrangement, banded horizontal register, straight-on architectural elevation.
- **Lighting:** shadowless studio diffuse; soft overcast daylight; or zero modeled light in graphic pieces.
- **Color handling:** strict primaries mode; cream-ground textile mode with accent patches; muted mosaic mode; monochrome chrome-glass-and-black mode.
- **Mood:** from clinical precision to workshop playfulness, never to romance or drama.

Avoid (style-breakers):
- Applied ornament: scrollwork, florals, beading, moldings, tufting, tassels, patterned veneers (woven fringe on a textile edge is a structural finish and is allowed).
- Serif or script display type, hand-lettering, decorative ligatures.
- Gradients, lens glows, haze, vignettes, chiaroscuro, candlelight, golden-hour warmth.
- Luxury signifiers: marble, gilt, shagreen, exotic woods, polished monumentality, symmetrical grandeur.
- Rustic handcraft cues: tool marks, wobble, organic asymmetry, folk or natural motifs.
- Art Deco zigzags, sunbursts, stepped setbacks; Art Nouveau whiplash curves.
- Agitprop urgency: photomontage, wedge-and-arrow propaganda dynamics.
- An all-over black-line primary grid on white with no object, no circle, no diagonal — that reads as De Stijl, not Bauhaus.
- Postmodern confetti patterning, ironic quotation, skeuomorphic gloss.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Constructivism:** shares geometry and diagonals, but Constructivism is agitprop — photomontage, red-black political urgency, the artist as engineer addressing the mass. Bauhaus keeps the calm of product photography and pedagogy, centers functional objects and woven color, and uses diagonals as composition, not as a shout.
- **De Stijl:** pure orthogonal abstraction — primaries separated by black lines on white, no circles, no diagonals, no curve. Bauhaus licenses the circle, the cantilever curve, diagonals in posters, an extended textile palette, and a whole object culture; if the image is only a rectangular primary grid, it is De Stijl.
- **Art Deco:** embraces ornament, symmetry, zigzag and sunburst motifs, and luxury materials (lacquer, gilt, exotic veneer) for glamour and speed. Bauhaus bans ornament, prefers asymmetry, and tells the truth about steel, glass, and plywood.
- **Arts and Crafts:** reveres the hand — medievalism, leaf and bird motifs, warm earth tones, quartersawn oak with joinery as ornament. Bauhaus shares only the ideal of unified art and craft; its hand serves the machine, its motifs are geometric, its colors primary.
- **International Typographic Style (Swiss):** post-1945 graphic manner — photography as the dominant image, mathematical asymmetric grids, Helvetica-family type, restrained red-black-white editorial spreads. Bauhaus graphics are lithographic and flat, using geometric abstraction *as* the image with a fuller primary-plus-textile palette, and Bauhaus is equally a culture of chairs, lamps, rugs, and buildings, not of editorial layouts.

Quick recognition tests: a chrome tube bent into a continuous cantilever under a canvas or cane seat; a fringed wool rug of horizontal color bands with one triangle; a glass sphere on a metal stem over a clear glass foot; a silver hemisphere teapot with a ebony disk and arc handle; grotesque capitals locked to a diagonal bar on cream paper; a white cube pierced by a ribbon window. Any one of these, held in the palette and lighting rules above, is Bauhaus.
