# Brutagrafix

Brutagrafix — also called Nu-Brutalism, Graphic Brutalism or Brutalist Utilitarian — emerged in the late 2010s and peaked into the early 2020s; the term was coined by the CARI Institute. It transplants the heavy, raw, architectural language of 1950s post-war concrete-minimalism into digital graphic design, then diffuses that mass with playful, bright, screen-native graphics. Its declared enemy is boring, smooth, template-driven minimalism: Brutagrafix wants interfaces, posters, products and websites to look loud, bold and real. Unlike older web brutalism, it keeps clear layouts and normal, user-friendly navigation — the rawness is a designed stance, not neglect. Around this core orbit four documented satellite currents that share its anti-polish ethos: Anti-Design (chaotic, clashing, rule-rejecting layouts), Naive Design (childlike doodles, wobbly lines, intentional imperfection), Neofolk Design (folk motifs and mystical symbols in minimalist or grunge systems), and Zinegeist (DIY scrapbook, raw editorial tactility).

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule
The ground is polarized: stark white (`#FFFFFF`–`#F2F2F0`) or near-black (`#0A0A0A`–`#161616`); a paper cream (`#EFE6D0`–`#E8DCC0`) appears only in the zine and neofolk registers. On that ground, structure and outlines are pure black (`#000000`). The clash set is drawn from the style's key colours — green (`#00C853`–`#8CFF00`), red (`#E32219`–`#FF2D2D`), blue (`#0026FF`–`#2E7BFF`) — plus white; the text's license for "high-contrast neon" extends the set to hot magenta (`#FF2EC4`), acid yellow (`#E8FF00`) and violet (`#6A00FF`). Two to four of these collide per layout. The binding rule: colour exists as flat, unmodulated fill; contrast comes from hue clash and figure/ground polarity, never from lighting or blended gradients. The only permitted "blends" are translucent flat overlays (a panel at roughly 40–70% opacity over another fill) and halftone or dither screens inside a shape.

### Typography (the style's first motif)
Heavy, large, raw sans-serif — grotesque or neo-grotesque, condensed or extended — set so big it behaves like architecture: edge-to-edge, cropped by the canvas, stacked in slabs. Scale range is extreme and deliberate: canvas-filling headlines against micro monospace or system-UI caption columns. The infobox's "repeating number of words" motif shows up as the same word or label repeated at different sizes and positions, duplicated tags, volume/issue numbers and numbered lists scattered as structural dots. Hand-lettering, wobbly script or naive marker lines may overlay a composition as an accent voice (collage and naive registers), never as the primary voice; serif appears only as sparse contrast.

### Signature motifs, ranked by documentation
1. **Oversized raw sans-serif type** as the structural material.
2. **Hard shadows**: solid, sharp, single-direction offset slabs behind shapes, type and buttons — never blurry or soft.
3. **Thick outlines**: heavy black borders wrapping images, buttons and text boxes.
4. **Boxy layouts**: rigid, clear grids of rectangles and squares; checker blocks; visible column rules.
5. **Halftones**: coarse dot-screen or dither treatment of photography — classical statue fragments, concrete façades, profile portraits, cropped mouths — crushed to duotone or monochrome.
6. **Translucent flat elements**: semi-transparent colour panels overlapping type and images.
7. **Repeated words and number tokens** as rhythm and ornament.
8. **Concrete-minimalist mass**: raw building photography, monolithic blocks, or type set as monolith.
9. **90s-internet nostalgia with modern polish**: pixel checker strips, dithered fills, QR and barcode blocks, ticker-like micro-text strips, system-dialog framing — rendered crisply, not actually degraded.
10. **Satellite register motifs**: anti-design clash and rotated collage strips; naive doodles (wobbly suns, flowers, birds, line-drawn hands); neofolk woodcut totems, stars and mystical symbols; zinegeist taped photos, scanner grain, stickers, starbursts, barcodes.

### Materials and textures
Flat vector fills, coarse halftone dots, dither, photocopy/scanner grain, paper crease and tape in the zine register, pixel checker patterns. Nothing glossy, chrome, airbrushed or rendered in 3D.

### Recurring subjects
Event and club posters, exhibition and opening identities, EP/album covers, zine spreads, website and UI mockups, manifesto pages, packaging and label sheets. Depicted content ranges widely: architecture and sculpture, halftone portraits, doodled flora and fauna, folk birds, punctuation glyphs enlarged to icons, numbers, grids, barcodes.

### Composition and lighting
Two committed poles with a middle scatter: (a) the **utilitarian grid** — orthogonal modules, clear hierarchy, legible entry point, usability intact; (b) **controlled chaos** — overlapping collage, rotated bars, cropped type, clashing layers, yet still offering one readable anchor. Between them sits the **scrapbook scatter** of the zine register: taped and pinned elements on a paper ground. Lighting is essentially absent as a photographic phenomenon: this is flat graphic light. The only light cue is the consistent direction of the hard offset shadow (pick one vector, e.g. down-right, and keep it). Photographs are lit flat and then crushed to duotone or halftone so they read as printed matter, not windows.

## Mood & Values

Boldness first: the style wants to feel loud, real and honest against a sea of identical template design. Concrete-minimalism supplies weight and seriousness; cooperatism supplies the communal, flyer-for-everyone spirit; playfulness supplies the doodles, neon and repeated games. Imperfection is intentional and human — wobbly lines and scanner grain say "a person made this" — but the core register keeps a usable, considerate structure, so the rebellion reads as generosity, not hostility.

## Era & Origins

Late 2010s, flourishing into the early 2020s; named by the CARI Institute. Double lineage: 1950s post-war béton-brut architecture (raw concrete, monolithic mass, minimal ornament) and 1990s raw internet style (default boldness, dither, primitive web graphics), the latter recalled with modern polish. It reacts against smooth corporate minimalism and template-driven sameness, and sits in a family with Acid Design, Brutalism and Flat Design while absorbing the anti-design, naive, neofolk and zinegeist currents.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. **Type is the building.** Start with a heavy raw sans at architectural scale; let it crop, stack and block.
2. **Hard edges only.** Shadows are solid offset slabs in one consistent direction; outlines are thick black; corners stay square or barely rounded.
3. **Clash on a stark ground.** Stark white or near-black field; two to four flat clashing colours from the green/red/blue/neon set; cream paper only for zine/neofolk.
4. **Flat fills, licensed translucency.** Overlap semi-transparent panels and halftone/dither screens; never blend with soft gradients.
5. **Choose a pole and commit.** Rigid usable grid, controlled collage chaos, or scrapbook scatter — but always leave one legible entry point.
6. **Spend a rawness budget.** Halftone dots, dither, scanner grain, tape, pixel checkers; keep the execution crisp and intentional.
7. **Repeat yourself.** Duplicate a word or label at multiple scales; scatter numbered micro-captions and monospace columns.
8. **Nostalgia with polish.** 90s-web artifacts (barcodes, QR blocks, tickers, checkers) drawn cleanly, as quotation rather than decay.

Variation axes: **subject** — any poster, cover, spread, UI, label or manifesto content, from flowers to firmware; **composition** — grid ↔ chaos ↔ scatter; **colour handling** — white-ground primary clash, black-ground neon clash, two-tone zine, cream-ground folk; **lighting** — flat graphic light with only shadow-direction as cue; **mood** — deadpan utilitarian ↔ playful naive ↔ rebellious collage; **register** — core, anti-design, naive, neofolk, zinegeist. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a bakery flyer, a software landing page or a folk album cover can all be perfect Brutagrafix.

Avoid: blurry or soft drop shadows and depth-of-field; glassmorphism, frosted blur, glossy 3D, chrome and airbrushed gradient-mesh backgrounds; muted earthy or tonal pastel harmonies; delicate serif or script as the primary voice; realistic photographic lighting (golden hour, cast shadows, studio gloss) as the image's light logic; stock-photo polish and clean product renders; watercolor washes, calligraphic flourishes or ornate historical ornament; purely unreadable noise with no entry point; and grey, unstyled, palette-less brokenness (that is web Brutalism's bareness — Brutagrafix always commits to a colour plan and a layout intent).

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Brutagrafix |
|---|---|
| **Brutalism** (web/art) | Brutalism is deliberately unfinished and unusable: default system fonts, monochrome greyness, broken navigation, no colour plan. Brutagrafix keeps usability, clear hierarchy, modern polish and a committed clashing palette; its rawness is designed. |
| **Acid Design** | Acid is liquid and psychedelic: chrome type, warped 3D, iridescent gradients, Y2K gloss on dark fields. Brutagrafix is flat, printed and boxy — neon arrives as solid blocks and halftone, edges stay hard, shadows stay offset slabs. |
| **Flat Design** | Flat Design is quiet and harmonious: friendly limited palette, generous whitespace, no shadows, no texture. Brutagrafix keeps flatness but adds clash, hard offset shadows, thick outlines, oversized raw type and halftone grain. |
| **Internal satellites** | If doodles or scrapbook tactility dominate *without* heavy sans mass, hard shadows or outlines, you are in the naive/zinegeist satellite rather than the core; core Brutagrafix always lets type and grid carry the structure. |

Note on evidence: photographic lighting is essentially undocumented and absent — this is a graphic, print-logic style; and the neofolk register, while text-documented, is the sparsest pole, so treat its woodcut totems and mystical symbols as an optional accent rather than a defining feature.
