# Casuals

Casuals (also "dressers," "Perry boys," "trendies," "scallies") is the British football subculture in which terrace style became a competition in expensive, exclusive designer sportswear. Born in the northwest of England in the late 1970s — Perry boys in Manchester and Salford, scallies in Liverpool — it replaced traditional fan attire (scarves, club colours) with continental brands brought back from European away trips, and replaced obvious hooligan markers with smart, brand-literate dressing that could evade police while signalling status to rival firms. The look is mod-descended: competitive, peacockish, detail-obsessed, but expressed through piqué polos, piping, knitwear and trainers rather than suits. Visually it survives as period snapshot photography: groups of young men packed onto terrace steps, train seats and street corners, in faded or slightly colour-shifted consumer film prints.

## Visual Identity

**Colour palette.** The style's colour logic is brand-coded blocks, not a designed scheme: each garment carries its label's signature colourway, so a crowd reads as a patchwork of discrete saturated garments on desaturated ground. Core garment range: tennis cream and ivory (#F2EBD9, #E8E0C8), oxblood/burgundy (#6E1423, #7A1F2B), bottle and forest green (#1F4D3A), navy (#1B2A4A), petrol teal (#1F6F6B), ice blue and pastel mint (#A8C8E0), and optical white (#F5F5F0) collars and plackets tipped with a contrasting red, green or navy stripe. Outerwear and trousers supply the earthy layer: olive-khaki and fawn parkas (#6B6B47, #8A8B6C), tan and beige (#C8A878), brown corduroy (#6B4A2F), denim from stonewash to mid indigo (#7A9CC4 to #4A6A8A). Ground and sky stay muted: concrete grey, red brick, peeling green painted wood, bleached white overcast. White leather trainers are the recurring bright accent at the bottom of the frame. Any of these hues may lead a given image; what must hold is the contrast of clean branded colour blocks against drab urban ground.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the source documents them.**
1. Designer sportswear tops: polo shirts and track tops with contrast-tipped collars, button plackets, chest stripes or chevrons, and small embroidered logos (laurel wreath, crocodile, box logo, chevron). Fred Perry, Peter Werth, Sergio Tacchini, Lacoste, Fila, Ellesse, Kappa, Lyle & Scott, Adidas.
2. Smart knitwear: Pringle-style jumpers, cricket and tennis V-neck knits with cable panels and contrast trim, baggy jumpers (Liverpool variant).
3. Trousers: corduroy — Lee cords, corduroy flares (Manchester) — and Lois jeans or straight stonewash denim.
4. Footwear: low-profile white leather trainers, Adidas Stan Smith type, training shoes; often the focal point of cropped detail shots of feet lined up on a ledge or step.
5. Outerwear: sheepskin coats and parkas (Liverpool), Harrington-style jackets; Burberry/Aquascutum check only for late-1980s and 1990s readings.
6. The wedge haircut: medium-length, feathered fringe and sides.
7. Terrace and travel context: concrete steps, stands and concourses, train carriage interiors, brick walls, car parks; the group, or "firm," as subject.
8. Conspicuous absence of club identity: no scarves, rosettes, replica shirts or flags — the brand logo replaces the club badge.

**Materials and textures.** Piqué cotton polos; wool and acrylic knits with cable ribs and raised trim; corduroy wales; stiff stonewash denim; shearling, suede and glossy nylon track-top faces; smooth leather trainer uppers with gum or white soles. Settings contribute rough concrete, painted wood, brick and corrugated roofing. The image surface itself is part of the texture: 35mm consumer colour print grain, faded dyes, slight colour cast, occasional flash glare; a minority of period monochrome prints exist, but colour snapshots dominate.

**Subjects, composition, lighting.** Typical subjects are groups of young men — three to a dozen — congregating: leaning on walls, perched on steps or ledges, squeezed into train seats, standing in loose horizontal rows or diagonal lines, with mixed gazes (some challenging the camera, some ignoring it). Two-shots and cropped garment-or-trainer details are equally valid. Framing is eye-level, amateur, sometimes awkwardly cropped; nothing is styled like an editorial. Light is flat overcast northern daylight, low hard winter sun, or direct on-camera flash at night and in carriage interiors.

## Mood & Values

Exclusivity, status, regional identity and competitive dressing: "looking the part" as seriously as fighting. The mood is cool, territorial pride — unsmiling or half-smirking faces, relaxed but packed body language, a peacock restraint in which wealth is signalled by a collar tipping or a chest logo rather than by overt display. Belonging to the firm and one-upmanship over rivals drive every outfit choice; the clothing is armour, passport and scorecard at once.

## Era & Origins

Late 1970s–early 1980s, British northwest, from mod and Northern Soul "soulboy" roots; scallies absorbed European sportswear via Liverpool FC's continental away trips and the Italian Paninari. Peak in the mid-to-late 1980s (Pringle jumpers, then Burberry and Aquascutum), temporary eclipse by Acid House and rave, resurgence in the 1990s alongside Britpop and Euro 96 with Stone Island, C.P. Company, Armani and Prada, and a 2000s revival fed by films like *The Football Factory* and *The Firm*. The style reacts against both traditional working-class fan dress and the obvious aggression-markers of earlier hooligans.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
1. Dress every figure by period brand logic: one hero branded garment (contrast-tip polo, piped track top, cricket knit), earthy or denim trousers, white low-profile leather trainers, small chest logo.
2. Keep colour in discrete branded blocks against desaturated urban ground and sky.
3. Use group grammar: congregation, leaning, perching, mixed direct and averted gazes; territorial ease, not posed cheerfulness.
4. Shoot it as period snapshot: film grain, faded or shifted print colour, overcast flatness or harsh flash; amateur framing allowed.
5. Set it on terraces, concourses, trains, streets, walls, car parks — with zero modern infrastructure or props.
6. Wedge or feathered fringe haircuts, clean-shaven or light moustache.

Variation axes: subject (match-day crowd, away-train journey, posed crew portrait, intimate two-shot, trainer-and-jeans detail, night street); composition (wide terrace row, tight cluster, diagonal line-up, cropped feet); lighting (overcast, winter sun, night flash, carriage interior); colour handling (faded pastel print, saturated early-80s print, monochrome); mood (defiant, celebratory, bored, tense); sub-era (late-70s Perry-boy sharpness, mid-80s European sportswear, 90s deluxe). A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

Avoid: replica football shirts, club scarves, badges, flags, banners or pyro; modern chunky-soled or knit trainers, smartphones, earbuds, smartwatches, skinny jeans; Madchester baggy flares, bucket hats, tie-dye and rose-tinted glasses; Ultras tifos, drums and club-colour face paint; skinhead or punk boots, braces, cropped or mohican hair; suits and ties (mod formality is a cousin, not this style); neon acid-rave palette and smiley iconography; glossy studio lighting or crisp digital HDR rendering; sleeve tattoos, modern fades and full beards; self-ironic or slapstick posing.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbour | Tell that separates it from Casuals |
|---|---|
| Madchester | Baggy silhouettes, bucket hats, tie-dye/paisley, round glasses, indie-dance mood; Casuals stays fitted, smart, brand-coded, pre- and post-rave |
| Ultras | Continental tifo culture: flags, banners, pyro, club colours displayed; Casuals deliberately wears no club insignia, only brand insignia |
| Blokecore | 2020s ironic revival in replica shirts, baggy jeans, socks-and-sandals; Casuals is the original, designer, status-competitive, never replica |
| Britpop | Mid-90s music-led mod revival with Union Jacks, parkas over polos, editorial gloss; Casuals is terrace-led snapshot realism with logos over national iconography |
| Lad Culture | 90s magazine laddishness: replica shirts, pub interiors, boozy grin, scruffy irony; Casuals is prouder, sharper, street- and terrace-based, without self-parody |

Note on evidence: the written source defines brands, garments, regions and values but is silent on photographic medium; the snapshot-film characteristics above are read from surviving period photographs of the scene and should be treated as the style's documented visual record rather than its doctrine.
