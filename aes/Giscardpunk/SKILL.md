# Giscardpunk

Giscardpunk names a French nostalgia-for-progress: the visual world of French modernism as lived and advertised during Valéry Giscard d'Estaing's presidency (1974–1981), extended backward into the 1960s and forward into the late 1980s. Coined in 2013 by artist Florent Deloison as both a visual aesthetic and a music genre, it celebrates — sincerely, satirically, or both at once — the era's faith in technology, state-led industry, and consumer modernity: the TGV, the Minitel network, nuclear power, new-town megastructures, and the graphic design that sold them. An alternate-history lore (Giscard re-elected in 1981, a cybernetic successor regime, pharaonic canal cities) circulates alongside the aesthetic, but imagery need not reference it; much of the style is simply period admiration. The result reads as a lost archive of French technocracy: magazine spreads, posters, postcards, maps and product photos from a country that believed concrete, orange plastic and videotex were the future.

## Visual Identity

### Palette
The rule is a cool field with loud warm punctures. Dominant surfaces sit in sky and rendering blues (#7FA6C6–#B9CFDE), concrete and asphalt greys (#8E9295–#C4C7C8), and paper cream/off-white (#F1EBDC–#FAF7EF). Against this quiet coolness, a mandatory accent of state orange (#D9531E–#F07820) appears as train livery, car bodies, telephone shells, poster bands. Secondary accents, used sparingly: poster red (#C8202A–#E03A2F), videotex teal (#1E6E68–#3E9C93) for pixel screens and pool water, terminal browns and beiges (#6B4A2F, #C9B491), and diagram black (#0E0F10) as a ground for network maps. Saturation may drift from sun-faded postcard to vivid poster chrome, but the ratio holds: large calm cool areas, small assertive orange events.

### Signature motifs (ranked by how centrally the culture documents them)
1. **Modernist and brutalist architecture** — the style's declared key motif: stepped and wave-form housing blocks, curved concrete megastructures, vast esplanades, tower clusters in the La Défense manner, sports palaces and drum-shaped halls. Buildings appear new, clean, sunlit — never weathered.
2. **Minitel / TELETEL videotex terminals** — beige-brown plastic wedges with curved CRTs showing teal pixel text and blocky directory grids, paired with orange or beige telephones on wood-veneer desks; the bridge to cassette futurism.
3. **TGV and rail networks** — orange-nosed trainsets, station canopies, and above all the diagrammatic network map: colored lines (blue, orange, green, red) on cream or black, sans-serif uppercase labels, service codes in the "3615 SNCF" register.
4. **Nuclear power and state industry** — cooling towers and reactor domes on the horizon, framed as civic pride and energy independence.
5. **French automobiles** — mass-market hatchbacks of the Renault 5 type and wedge-shaped concept cars of the Citroën Karin type, presented centered on empty ground.
6. **Period advertising and poster graphics** — bold grotesque uppercase in horizontal type bands, hero product shots, slogan lines, masthead strips.
7. **Retrofuturist urban renderings** — airbrushed bird's-eye panoramas of planned cities: riverfront slab towers, heliports, elevated highways, model new towns; the never-built or speculative city (canal cities, annexed districts) belongs here.
8. **Political and campaign iconography** — portrait-plus-slogan poster layouts and tricolor-adjacent stripe graphics, used as period texture rather than partisan statement.
9. **Candid period street and leisure life** — postcard scenes of municipal pools, esplanades, holiday crowds, parked cars under flat blue sky.

### Materials and textures
Board-formed and precast concrete; tinted glass curtain wall; white painted steel tube and railings; beige and brown ABS plastic; wood veneer and laminate desks; chrome bumpers and trim; vinyl, wool and polyester textiles. Carrier surfaces matter as much as subjects: glossy magazine paper, newsprint halftone, chrome postcard stock. Finish cues include halftone dot grain, slight print misregistration, airbrush gradients and gouache flat washes in renderings, mild fade and color drift in postcards, and film grain in candid footage.

### Composition and lighting
Four composition families recur: high-horizon bird's-eye or axonometric panoramas of urban plans; frontal, centered, symmetrical object shots (car, terminal, tower) on empty ground; full-bleed diagrams and maps; poster grids with horizontal type bands above or below a hero image. Lighting is predominantly clear-sky midday daylight with crisp short shadows and flat postcard evenness; renderings add atmospheric haze toward the horizon; interiors use even tungsten or fluorescent office light. A minority nocturne mode exists — high-contrast black-and-white tower night shots — but it is thinly evidenced and optional. Geometry stays legible: verticals kept vertical, forms documented or promoted, never dissolved in mood.

## Mood & Values
Technocratic optimism: progress as civic joy, administered by the state and delivered as consumer goods. The style expresses social and economic liberalism, industrial pride, pro-nuclear confidence, and the empowerment of France on the world stage — filtered through 2010s nostalgia and, often, deadpan satire. Its emotional register is the bureaucratic sublime: orderly esplanades, confident slogans, leisure made municipal (pools, holiday trains, model cities). Satire, when present, comes from scale and deadpan administration, not from grime or irony-heavy decay. Affection and mockery coexist; a sincere postcard and a parody poster share the same grammar.

## Era & Origins
Subject era: France, roughly 1960s–late 1980s, centered on 1974–1981. Style era: coined 2013 in France by Florent Deloison, growing from online meme and alternate-history culture into a named aesthetic with a music strand. It reacts to and revives French modernism and the period's own concept of "progress," reconstructing it through the print media that originally advertised it. Related brands anchor authenticity: Minitel/TELETEL, Thomson, TGV, SNCF, Renault, Peugeot, Citroën.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
1. Choose a period carrier first: magazine spread, poster, advertisement, postcard, network map, architectural rendering, or candid photo — the image should feel like an artifact, not a free-floating scene.
2. Apply the palette rule: cool blue-grey-cream field, at least one assertive orange accent, optional red or teal secondaries.
3. Keep technology analog and videotex-level: curved CRTs, chunky keys, coiled cords, teal pixel text; computing is terminal-based, never graphical-web.
4. Make France legible: French-language signage and slogans, SNCF/RATP-style logotypes, French brand names, French urban fabric.
5. Let architecture lead or frame: concrete megastructure, esplanade, or tower cluster present as subject or backdrop.
6. Finish with print physics: halftone grain, paper tone, airbrush haze in renderings, postcard fade in photos.
7. Set the mood dial between sincere nostalgia and deadpan satire; satire via bureaucratic scale, never via ruin.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across architecture, rail and road transport, telecom and computing, nuclear infrastructure, advertising layouts, leisure and street life, space-program hardware, or speculative mega-projects from the lore. Composition may range aerial to frontal to diagrammatic. Lighting may range flat postcard daylight to office interior to the rare black-and-white night. Saturation may range faded to chrome-vivid. Narrative layer may range from pure period documentation to lore elements (model cities, canal projects, cybernetic officials) treated with period-plausible deadpan. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**
- Cyberpunk noir: neon, rain-slick night streets, holograms, dystopian grime.
- Contemporary digital tech: smartphones, flat panels, web browsers, sleek minimal UI.
- Wrong-national retro: American googie/diner chrome, Soviet Ostalgie, British rail branding.
- Steampunk or dieselpunk brass, rivets, steam.
- Decay, rust, graffiti, ruin porn; Giscardpunk concrete is clean and new.
- Earthy harvest-gold/avocado seventies interiors dominating without the cool blue-grey field.
- Generic sci-fi chrome, lens flares, specular futurism beyond the period airbrush idiom.
- Anonymous international glass towers with no French signage or state branding (drifts into plain Modernism).
- Cartoon or comic line work; keep photographic or print-rendering realism.
- Lore creatures or cyborgs played as fantasy illustration rather than deadpan period documentation.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell-tale difference |
|---|---|
| Brutalism | Shares raw concrete, but Brutalism is monochrome, austere, often weathered and photographed today; Giscardpunk adds period print color, orange accents, consumer and state branding, and optimistic narrative. |
| Cassette Futurism | Shares beige CRT analog tech; Cassette Futurism is gadget- and interface-centric and nation-agnostic, while Giscardpunk is France-specific, architecture- and state-centric, and includes posters, maps and political iconography. |
| Cyberpunk | Night versus day; neon versus print orange; corporate dystopia and grime versus clean state technocracy and leisure. |
| Modernism / International Style | Straight archival documentation of built work; Giscardpunk is a nostalgic reconstruction that admits never-built renderings, satire, and print-artifact framing. |
| Pixel UI | Chunky pixel screens are only one motif here, always embedded in a physical period terminal; Pixel UI is screen-only and lacks architecture, print grain and French state branding. |
| Politicalwave | Political poster grammar appears, but as one texture among technocratic motifs, held at ironic or nostalgic distance rather than partisan immediacy. |

Where evidence is thin, say so: clothing and fashion are only implicitly documented (period defaults, suits, holiday wear), and the nocturne mode rests on scant examples — treat both as optional extensions, not core rules.
