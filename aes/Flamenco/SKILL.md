# Flamenco

Flamenco is a musical and cultural expression from Andalusia — singing (cante), guitar (toque) and dance (baile) bound together by the rhythmic cycles of compás — whose visual language crystallized around the *traje de flamenca*, the festival dress of Seville, Jerez de la Frontera and Cádiz. It is an art of the body in percussive motion: footwork nailed into shoes, handclaps, a skirt thrown open on the beat. Its imagery spans three recurring arenas — the black-void stage, the smoky studio composite, and the glossy feria-fashion runway — plus the daylight fairground where the day dress is worn. Rooted in Andalusian folk culture with decisive Romani (Gitano) authorship, it carries the gravity of a marginalized community's expression even at its most festive; UNESCO listed it as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2010.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is a saturated core triad with sparing accents: **red, black, white**, plus **gold, yellow, green** as secondary notes. Reds run from oxblood `#6E0B0B` through scarlet `#D22B2B` to a carmine-magenta `#C41E3A`; blacks are near-true (`#0A0A0A`–`#1E1E1E`) rather than soft grey; whites are crisp `#FFFFFF` or ivory `#F5EEDF`. Accents appear as embroidery, flowers or shawl silk: gold `#C9A227`, saffron `#E8B004`, green `#2F6B4F`. Contemporary feria fashion (the text sanctions "a range of colors") extends the dress hue to teal `#1F6F6B`, plum `#5E2A40`, mustard `#B8860B` and taupe-grey `#8B7D6B` — but even then the image should read red/black/white-dominant overall, with darkness or white supplying the counterweight. Flat, desaturated or pastel color handling does not occur in this style.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them)

1. **The traje de flamenca**: form-fitting bodice and hips flaring into tiered ruffled skirts (*volantes*), ruffled sleeve trim; the day cut flares from mid-thigh, the dance cut from higher on the hip; the staged *bata de cola* adds a long train.
2. **Polka dots (*lunares*) and floral prints**, roses above all — the dotted *traje de lunares* is the frequent choice; solid black or red runs a close second.
3. **The shawl**: Manila shawl / *mantón* with silk fringe and embroidery, worn over the shoulders or whipped through the air in the *baile de mantón*.
4. **Hair architecture**: tight bun crowned with flowers and a decorative comb (*peineta*).
5. **The fan**, held open against the bodice or snapped shut in the hand.
6. **Castanets** — a listed key motif and a Romani-inherited instrument, but an optional prop; staged flamenco dance more often signals itself through hands, feet and shawl than through castanets.
7. **The music apparatus**: classical guitar with spruce top, clapping hands (*palmas*), the cajón; heeled shoes with nails in toe and heel for *zapateado*, heeled boots for male dancers.
8. **Contextual settings, lowest rank**: feria fairground and caseta lineage, *café cantante* interiors, Andalusian skyline spires as backdrop. The tradition documents the places (Seville, Jerez, Cádiz); architectural silhouettes are a permissible poster device, never a requirement.

### Materials and textures

Sheen against matte: satin or crepe bodices catching a hard key light; printed cotton and poplin ruffles; silk embroidery and knotted silk fringe on the mantón; leather and suede shoe uppers with steel nails; warm wood of the guitar soundboard; tortoiseshell comb; rose petals; glossy lacquered runway floors throwing mirror reflections; smoky haze in studio backdrops. Texture contrast is structural — a flaming red satin must have something lightless (black void, black shawl, charcoal smoke) to burn against.

### Recurring subjects

The *bailaora* or *bailaor* mid-piece; the *cantaor* singing raw and ornamented, face contracted; the guitarist bent into a *falseta*; a circle of palmas and *jaleo*; feria daytime crowds in day dresses; runway models presenting new feria collections; still-life arrangements of shawl, fan, comb and shoes; small stage or peña performances with singer, guitarist and dancer in tight proximity.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Figures dominate the frame; the skirt acts as compositional mass — a bell, a bloom, a thrown arc occupying the lower half. Arms build arches and angles above the head (*braceo*), wrists broken, fingers splayed; faces often in profile or downcast three-quarter. Deep negative space (stage black, umber smoke) isolates a single figure; a seated guitarist at the frame edge is a classic counterweight; tableau rows of dancers in profile and runway center-lines with floor reflections are other common architectures. Lighting is almost always directional and contrasty: a single hard key carving the figure out of near-black, a saturated color-wash backdrop on catwalks, or strong Andalusian daylight for feria daywear. Flamenco rarely sits in flat, shadowless mid-tone light.

## Mood & Values

*Duende* — the "mysterious power that everyone feels and that no philosopher explains": emotional expression raised above technical prettiness. In flamenco slang, *bonito* ("pretty") is faint praise. The values are emotional honesty (pain, loss, longing in the deep palos; joy in the festive ones), cultural heritage and Gitano memory of persecution, and artistic virtuosity made visible — compás locked between footwork, claps and guitar, tension rising to release. Pride and defiance sit under the posture even in celebration; nothing about the stance is passive or merely decorative.

## Era & Origins

Modern form from the late 18th century in agrarian Lower Andalusia (Jerez holds the earliest written record), layered from Romani song and dance carried from Rajasthan, Moorish and Sephardic strands, Castilian sung poetry and African rhythmic influence. The 19th century's *cafés cantantes* professionalized the singers; post-War-of-Independence *casticismo* and the *majo*/*cañí* archetype fed Andalusian regionalism. The traje de flamenca itself dates only from the late 19th–early 20th century, descending from livestock-fair vendors' workwear, officialized at the 1929 Seville Exposition and reinvented annually at Seville's feria-fashion salons. The style therefore reacts twice: originally an expression of a marginalized community, later a form resisting its own co-option into Franco-era national symbol and tourist *españolada*.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:

1. **Costume grammar first.** If a dressed figure appears, build the silhouette: fitted through bodice and hips, flaring into ruffled tiers; ruffles at sleeves or hem; dots, florals or solid black/red.
2. **Palette discipline.** Anchor red/black/white; let accents and contemporary hues ride on top, never replace the anchor.
3. **Light carves out of darkness.** One hard key, deep shadow, saturated color; or runway gloss with mirror floor; or bright feria daylight. Never flat, even, shadowless light.
4. **Freeze the compás.** Poses are percussive stillness: planted foot, arched back, broken wrist, splayed fingers, skirt caught mid-throw. Tension, not repose.
5. **Texture contrast.** Satin sheen, silk fringe, matte black void, wood, petals — at least two opposing surfaces in play.
6. **Signifier density.** At least two motifs from the ranked list (dress ruffles, dots, shawl fringe, bun-with-flower, fan, guitar, palmas, nailed heels) so the style reads without caption.
7. **Duende over pretty.** Facial and bodily expression carries grief, pride or fierce joy; avoid vacant glamour.

Variation axes: **subject** (dancer, singer, guitarist, palmas circle, feria crowd, runway model, costume still-life, café stage); **composition** (full figure, cropped skirt-or-hands study, profile tableau row, runway center-line, poster composite with architecture); **lighting** (stage spot on black, smoky studio gradient, saturated catwalk wash, feria daylight); **color handling** (core triad solids, lunares, floral prints, or contemporary teal/plum/mustard dresses counterweighted by black or white); **mood** (cante jondo gravity to alegrías festivity); **medium** (photographic, cinematic, graphic poster). A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

Avoid: pastel, beige or desaturated minimal palettes; shadowless high-key lighting; tulle tutus, sequined ballroom or salsa gowns, can-can frills; 18th-century castizo costume (short embroidered jackets, knee breeches, hairnets) as the subject — that is Majismo; the bullfighter's traje de luces as central dress; limp wrists, rounded ballet port de bras, symmetrical passive standing; sneakers or casual streetwear inside a costumed scene; neon, cyberpunk or fusion-scifi props; sun-and-sangria postcard *españolada* kitsch that strips the Gitano-Andalusian gravity.

## Discrimination Cues: Flamenco vs Majismo

Majismo is the parent picturesque archetype — the 18th–early 19th-century *majo/maja* street elegance of castizo Spain, known from Goya's genre scenes: short silver-trimmed jackets, knee breeches, hairnets and lace over basquiñas, bullring and meadow settings, painterly daylight, gallant leisurely wit. Flamenco shares lineage (the *cañí* style grew from majo individualism; both use fans, combs, roses) but diverges in silhouette, light and tension.

| Cue | Flamenco | Majismo |
|---|---|---|
| Silhouette | Ruffled tiered flare from hip or thigh; bata de cola trains | 18th-c. jacket-and-breeches; lace mantilla and hairnet over straight skirts |
| Pattern | Lunares dots, rose prints | Embroidered castizo trim, tapestry-like florals |
| Light | Stage chiaroscuro, smoky studio, runway gloss | Painterly daylight or warm interior, rococo evenness |
| Body | Percussive tension, broken wrists, zapateado plant | Poised stroll, gallant gesture, static genre pose |
| Props | Guitar, palmas, fringed mantón, nailed heels | Guitar as serenade prop, cape, bullfight paraphernalia |
| Mood | Duende: grief, pride, release | Picturesque defiance, wit, leisure |

Decide by silhouette and light first: ruffled flare plus dots or fringe plus performance tension reads flamenco; hairnet lace and 18th-century jacket in painterly calm reads Majismo. A secondary trap is generic tourist-flamenco: sun, sand and fan clichés without the ruffled grammar, the dark contrast or the emotional weight flatten the style into españolada, which the tradition itself rejects.
