# Sukeban

Sukeban (スケバン, roughly "girl boss" — from *suke*, slang for girl, plus *banchō*, boss of a delinquent gang) is a Japanese subculture and the fashion language derived from rebellious girl gangs. It emerged in the 1960s as a refusal of the expectation that young women behave and dress well-mannered: delinquency, anti-social posture and violence were wielded as a crude form of feminism. Because most gang members were still of school age, the uniform became the canvas — every garment is a school uniform *wrong on purpose*: lengthened, cropped, re-lettered. Older sukeban often rode with bosozoku biker gangs, and the style shares the bosozoku's embroidered tokkō-fuku coat, but its center of gravity is always the modified sailor uniform and the all-female gang. The word was popularized by 1970s–80s films (Norifumi Suzuki's On'na Banchō series), TV shows (*Sukeban Deka*, *Shoujo Commando Izumi*) and manga (*Hana no Asuka Gumi!*), which fixed the stereotype of the blue sailor suit with a floor-sweeping skirt; echoes survive in fiction (Sailor Jupiter's original conception, Saki Nikaido in *Zombieland Saga*, the gang in *Shenmue*) and in modern revival vendors such as Towa no Uta.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is **one solid ground color per garment, lettered in high-contrast thread**. Grounds sit in a narrow, deliberate range: deep navy (the style's key color, roughly `#16243D`–`#2A3B66`), ink black (`#0B0B0D`–`#1A1A1E`), off-white or bone (`#EDEAE0`–`#F5F2EA`), and saturated royal blue (`#24359C`–`#2B3FA0`). Revival-era garments may substitute pastel grounds — candy pink (`#F49AC1`–`#FFB6D2`) or lilac (`#C6B4E6`) — without losing the style, because the lettering and silhouette carry the identity. Embroidery thread supplies the accent layer: vermillion red (`#C8102E`), red-orange (`#E4572E`), gold (`#C9A227`), plus white-on-dark or black-on-light reversals. Hardware and trim stay minor: silver chain, black leather belt, white cotton headband. Hair color is free — natural black in period renderings, dyed teal, blonde or two-tone in revival looks. Never a printed pattern fabric: the ground cloth is plain, and all visual noise comes from stitching.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how strongly the tradition documents them

1. **The modified school uniform.** Sailor-collar blouse or middy top cropped to expose the bare waist; pleated skirt lengthened to mid-calf or ankle (the opposite of the regulation knee); loafers replaced by sneakers. This is the style's irreducible core.
2. **The tokkō-fuku long coat.** A calf- to ankle-length work-coat silhouette with wide sleeves, stand or sailor collar, and snap or button front, worn open or closed over the uniform. Its back panel, sleeves and hem are the billboard.
3. **Vertical kanji lettering.** Columns of chain-stitch script running down sleeves, back panels and skirt hems: gang names, territorial claims, feminist symbology and anarchic messages. Back yokes carry crest patches — name cartouches, rising-sun discs, flag emblems — and occasional secondary Latin script. The lettering is content, not decoration; it reads as a manifesto worn on the body.
4. **The sailor collar itself.** The blue sailor suit with long skirt is the mediated stereotype and remains the fastest recognition cue even without a coat.
5. **Colorful eye makeup and deliberate hair.** Heavy, saturated eye makeup and long, teased or tied hair (high ponytails, twin tails) are documented across fiction and period imagery; the ponytail lineage is visible even in sanitized descendants.
6. **Gang formation.** Sukeban appear as rows or clusters of girls: synchronized raised arms or fists, linked shoulders, or backs turned in unison so the embroidered back panels face the viewer.
7. **Optional accessory layer** (present in the visual record, lightly documented in text): white cotton hachimaki headbands, chain belts slung at the hip, plush bag charms and face masks in revival street snaps. Treat these as seasoning, never as load-bearing.

### Materials and textures

Matte cotton twill or drill — the sturdy workwear cloth of tokkō-fuku — with raised, glossy chain-stitch and satin-stitch embroidery sitting on top of it; appliquéd felt or cloth patches; metal snap buttons; rubber-sole canvas or leather sneakers; soft cotton headband cloth. The texture story is **flat matte ground plus dimensional thread**: light catches the embroidery before it catches anything else. Period images add film grain, flash flare and faded dye; revival images add crisp digital snap or deliberate glitch/chromatic fringe.

### Recurring subjects

School-age girls and young women, solo or in gangs of three to five; mirror selfies and street snaps in the revival mode; runway or stage tableaux in the show mode; manga/anime cel renderings as a legitimate medium for the style. Settings cluster around graffiti-tagged walls, alleys, arcades and night streets, school interiors, and bare stage floors.

### Composition and lighting

Full-body or three-quarter standing figures dominate, because the silhouette (crop + maxi skirt + coat length) is the argument. Back views are privileged: the embroidery lives on the back, so turning away from the camera is an act of display, not hiding. Group shots align bodies in a row with one shared gesture. Lighting tends to harsh on-camera flash at night or in cramped interiors, flat frontal stage light, or overcast daylight street snap; period renderings carry 35mm grain, slight motion blur and washed color. Framing is usually vertical and unpolished — snapshot grammar, not editorial grammar.

## Mood & Values

Defiance, solidarity, and pride. The style expresses rebellion as a value in itself: a refusal of well-mannered femininity, performed by taking the uniform of compliance and stretching, cropping and re-lettering it until it threatens. The gaze is confrontational or deliberately indifferent; the body language is chin-up, arms crossed, fists raised, backs turned in unison. Wearing your slogans stitched down your sleeves means the clothing is speech. Underneath the toughness sits sisterhood — the gang as chosen family — and a feminist claim to space, volume and menace.

## Era & Origins

Emergent in 1960s Japan as girl-gang street culture; named and mythologized through 1970s–80s film, TV and manga; residual presence in 1990s fiction; revived from the 2010s onward as a fashion statement by vendors and street communities who rebuild the tokkō-fuku in pastel grounds. The style reacts against postwar expectations of feminine propriety and against the school uniform as an instrument of that expectation. Period renderings should feel like found film photographs; revival renderings may feel like contemporary digital street snaps or lookbook images, but the garment rules stay identical.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the silhouette first: sailor or work collar, cropped top baring the midriff, pleated skirt at mid-calf to ankle, sneakers. If the skirt is short, the image has left the style.
2. Make the lettering meaningful: vertical columns of kanji (gang name, slogan, feminist or anarchic statement) placed on back panels, sleeves and skirt hems, with one crest patch at the back yoke. Lettering contrasts hard with the ground cloth.
3. One solid ground color per garment; keep the palette to navy/black/white/royal blue, or pastel pink/lilac in revival mode, with vermillion, gold, white or black thread.
4. Give the body an attitude: stare, slouch, crossed arms, raised fist, or a unified turned back. Neutrality reads as costume, not culture.
5. Choose a snapshot grammar: direct flash, film grain, vertical frame, unstyled background — or flat stage light for a show tableau.

**Variation axes.** Subject matter may range from a single girl in a bedroom mirror selfie to a five-girl gang row on a night street, a runway walk, a school-roof standoff, or an anime cel illustration. Composition may be frontal, profile, mirror-reflected, or fully from behind; solo or group; cropped at the knee or full-length with the coat hem in frame. Lighting may be night flash, overcast daylight, stage flat-light, or manga flat color. Color handling may lean period-faded or revival-saturated; coat grounds may move anywhere within the stated palette. Mood may run from cold menace to playful gang camaraderie. Setting may be alley, arcade street, graffiti wall, stage, classroom, or domestic interior. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A pink-coated solo girl shot on a clean digital camera is as valid as a black-coated gang row on grainy film, provided the silhouette, lettering and posture obey the rules above.

**Avoid.**
- Short pleated skirts, thigh-highs or zettai ryōiki framing (that is gyaru or uniform-fetish imagery, the exact opposite gesture).
- Loose socks and loafers; the style replaces loafers with sneakers.
- Motorcycles, helmets, jump suits or road-convoy framing as the focal subject (that is bosozoku; a bike may at most sit in background context).
- Male-led or mixed-gender gang centering; sukeban is girls' culture.
- Tanned skin with bleached brows and glam-cute styling (gyaru).
- Western brand logos, athleisure, bodycon dresses, contour-makeup glam (baddie / Asian Baby Girl).
- Frills, lace, bows or fairy-kei pastel layering without the coat-and-lettering structure.
- Horizontal Latin-logo dominance; kanji verticality is the lettering grammar, Latin script only incidental.
- Sexualized posing or allure-oriented framing; the style's register is intimidation and pride.
- Pristine e-commerce studio lighting with passive posing; the snapshot or stage grammar is part of the identity.
- Traditional kimono or yukata forms; the style is uniform- and workwear-derived.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared surface | What separates sukeban |
|---|---|---|
| **Bosozoku** | The embroidered tokkō-fuku coat itself | Bosozoku centers bikes, helmets, flags, jumpsuits and mixed-gender crews on the road; sukeban centers the sailor collar, cropped blouse and ankle-length skirt on an all-female body, with no vehicle required |
| **Gyaru** | School-age girls, styled hair, heavy makeup | Gyaru shortens the skirt, tans the skin, bleaches the hair, adds loose socks and glam-cute accessories; sukeban lengthens the skirt, keeps plain or dyed-but-untanned skin, and swaps cuteness for menace |
| **Yanki** | Delinquency, kanji lettering, period grain | Yanki is the gender-neutral umbrella (male tanran, track jackets, perms); sukeban is specifically the female, uniform-derived branch with the crop-plus-maxi silhouette |
| **Asian Baby Girl** | East Asian girl-gang energy, street snap grammar | ABG is 2010s diasporic: acrylic nails, lash extensions, brand logos, cargo and techwear; it has no sailor uniform, no tokkō-fuku, no kanji embroidery |
| **Baddie** | Confident stare, crop tops | Baddie is Western internet glam: bodycon fits, neutral beige-black palette, contour makeup, no uniform lineage and no stitched lettering |

**Fast recognition checklist:** sailor or work collar + cropped top + ankle-length pleated skirt + sneakers; long coat with vertical kanji columns and a back crest patch; solid navy/black/white/blue (or revival pink/lilac) ground with vermillion or gold thread; all-female solo or gang framing; flash-lit, grainy, unpolished frame; posture of open defiance. Three or more of these, with the silhouette intact, is sukeban.
