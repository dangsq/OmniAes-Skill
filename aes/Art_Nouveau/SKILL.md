# Art Nouveau

Art Nouveau was the international style of art, architecture and applied art that flourished from the 1890s to the 1910s as a deliberate revolt against 19th-century academic historicism. Its philosophy was the *Gesamtkunstwerk* — the total work of art in which painting, ironwork, furniture, lettering and light fixtures all speak one organic language — and its visual grammar was borrowed from nature: growing stems, unfurling petals, insect wings, streaming hair. It wore different names in different cities (Jugendstil, Secessionsstil, Modern Style / Glasgow Style, Modernisme, Tiffany Style) and never fully dominated its era, coexisting and sometimes blending with historicist revivals; but wherever it appears, it appears as line in motion.

## Visual Identity

### Palette
The rule is a **muted, faded, earthy field with metallic accents**, never saturated primaries and never high-contrast chrome glamour. Work within these bands:

| Role | Range | Example anchors |
|---|---|---|
| Ground / field | cream, ivory, oat, pale pink-apricot | #F2EBD9, #EDE3C8, #E8C4A8 |
| Greens (signature) | olive, sage, moss, celadon | #556B2F, #6B7C3F, #9CAF88, #7FA8A0 |
| Yellows / browns | mustard, ochre, umber, walnut | #C9A227, #D4A017, #6E4B2A, #8B5E3C |
| Cool whispers | lilac, lavender, dusty violet | #B79BC4, #C8A8C8 |
| Metal accents | brass, gold, occasional silver-pewter | #B08D3E, #C9A227, #A9A9A9 |
| Contour | deep brown-black or dark olive outline | #2E2A26 |

Two licensed high-key exceptions belong to the style's geometric flank: Vienna Secession **white plus gold** (#F5F2EA with gilded laurel), and Glasgow **gesso white/grey with sparse rose-lilac**. Monochrome ink line on paper (bookplates, linear posters) is also within range. Poster color behaves like Japanese woodblock prints: flat unmodulated planes, faded earthy tones, visible outlines.

### Line and motif (ranked by how firmly the text documents them)
1. **The whiplash curve** — the style's icon: an asymmetrical, sinuous S-line suggesting growth and movement, drawn into iron railings, plaster moldings, stair strings, hair and plant stems.
2. **Stylized plant ornament**, most characteristically carried in *ironwork*: flowers, vines, leaves; named species include cyclamen, iris, orchid, thistle, mistletoe, holly and water lily.
3. **The female figure** — young women, sometimes femmes fatales, in flowy gowns with flower crowns; hair and drapery dissolve into the whiplash line; posters often seat her inside a circular halo or ring of ornament.
4. **Animals and insects**: peacock and peacock feathers, swans, dragonflies, butterflies.
5. **Glass-and-metal architecture**: exposed, celebrated iron structure (metro entrances, canopies), and round or oval windows descended from Japanese moon gates.
6. **Hand-lettering** integrated into graphic work; letterforms organic and drawn, not typeset.
7. **Gold and silver accents** — gilded dome grids, brass fittings, Klimtian gold fields in the Austrian branch.
8. **Regional geometry** (secondary tier): straight lines and gentle floral decoration in Glasgow and Vienna; folkloric pattern and glazed tile roofs in Hungary; Viking interlace knots in Scandinavia.

### Materials and textures
Wrought and cast iron painted olive-green or left as brass; opalescent, stained and favrile glass; carved wood with marquetry and floral inlay; glazed ceramic tile and folkloric roof ceramics; molded plaster and stucco that melts into curve; bronze, enamel, pearl and semi-precious stone in jewelry; mosaic and parquet floors. Surtures read hand-wrought but precise: smooth glass, satin metal, matte plaster, glossy glaze.

### Recurring subjects
Posters and bookplates with hand-lettered bands; stairwells and dining rooms where railing, mural, light and floor share one line; apartment facades with curvilinear balconies and sculpted door surrounds; metro entrances and park pavilions of iron and glass; vases, lamps and chandeliers whose stems are plants and whose shades are blossoms; jewels shaped as insects and orchids; the dressed woman of the 1890s–1910s, whose S-curved silhouette echoes the style (fashion itself is only indirectly documented — treat period dress as echo, not doctrine).

### Composition and lighting
Asymmetry by default: the whiplash line leads the eye diagonally or spirally through the frame. Circular and oval framing devices (halos, round windows, gilded sphere domes) punctuate the asymmetry. Interiors are total designs — no element escapes the line language. Light is either **warm incandescent globe light** (opal bulbs on brass stems, the new electricity worn as ornament), **soft diffuse daylight** on facades, or **flat shadowless poster light**. Elongated vertical proportion is licensed in the Glasgow branch; cubic white mass with a gilded crown in the Secession branch.

## Mood & Values

Sensual elegance, dreamy Belle Époque decadence, and an optimistic creed that the modern age deserves its own ornament. Values expressed: unity of fine and applied art; rejection of copying past eras; nature as the legitimate source of form; modern materials (iron, glass, electricity) displayed proudly rather than hidden; craft dignity extended to every object of daily life.

## Era & Origins

Western Europe, 1890s–1910s. Catalysts: Japonisme (flat color, outline, moon-gate windows) and the British Arts and Crafts movement's floral craft ethic; motive: anti-historicism against Neoclassical, Gothic and Rococo revivals and the Beaux-Arts academy. Regional bodies: French/Belgian whiplash foliage (Horta, Guimard, Gallé); British Glasgow geometry (Mackintosh, Macdonald); Viennese Secession white-and-gold (Olbrich, Hoffmann, Klimt); Catalan Modernisme (Gaudí); Hungarian folkloric tile (Lechner); American Tiffany and Sullivan ironwork. The style died after WWI, displaced by Art Deco's machine geometry, and was resurrected visually by 1960s psychedelia — a later afterlife, outside the core era.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
1. Begin with the line, not the object: lay a whiplash spine through the composition and let structure (railing, stem, hair, molding) grow from it.
2. Stylize nature into ornament — flatten, elongate, curve; never copy a botanical plate.
3. Honor the Gesamtkunstwerk: every visible element, down to lamp globes and floor mosaic, shares the same curve vocabulary.
4. Keep color muted and earthy; reserve gold/brass for accents and fittings, not fields (Secession white-gold excepted).
5. In graphic work: flat color planes, visible contour, hand-drawn lettering integrated into the ornament.
6. Celebrate modern structure: expose iron and glass; let electric bulbs glow as pearls on brass stems.
7. Default to asymmetry; use symmetry only inside the licensed Secession/Glasgow geometric variant, and then with discipline (white ground, gold foliage, rectilinear grid).

**Variation axes.** Subject: poster, bookplate, jewelry, vase, lamp, metro entrance, stairwell, dining room, facade, pavilion, figure study — anything the style touched. Composition: curve-driven asymmetry, circular halo centrality, or rectilinear Glasgow/Secession grid. Lighting: warm globe incandescence, diffuse daylight, flat poster light. Color handling: earthy muted, pastel pale, white-plus-gold, or monochrome ink line. Mood: decadent, dreamy, elegant, folkloric, or austere-secessionist. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a dragonfly brooch, a subway canopy and a stairwell mural are equally valid carriers of the same line.

**Avoid**
- Art Deco geometry: chevrons, zigzags, sunbursts, stepped symmetry, chrome, black-lacquer-and-gold glamour.
- Machine-age minimalism: unornamented straight steel, Bauhaus sans signage, empty functionalist space.
- Earnest historicism: Neoclassical pediments and columns, Gothic pointed arches, Rococo cartouches used as revival (rare period blends exist, but they are not the style's core).
- Saturated primaries, neon, plastic, photorealistic unstyled botanicals.
- Rigid bilateral symmetry in the French/Belgian curve mode.
- Cubist fragmentation or futurist speed lines; the motion here is growth, not velocity.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Arts and Crafts**: shares floral motif and craft ethics, but keeps rectilinear joinery, matte medieval/folk simplicity and static line; Art Nouveau adds the dynamic whiplash, urban iron-and-glass, and the sensual female figure.
- **Glasgow Style**: the British flank of Art Nouveau itself — rectilinear grids, elongated verticals, gesso white/grey with sparse rose-lilac; straight-lined pale imagery is Glasgow-end Art Nouveau, not the French/Belgian core.
- **Modernisme**: the Catalan flank — heavier masonry mass, broken-tile trencadís mosaic, parabolic arches, Gothic and Mudéjar memory; earthier and more sculptural than Parisian iron lightness.
- **Aestheticism**: the 1870s–80s precursor with sunflowers, lilies, blue-and-white porcelain and peacocks, but painterly and loose, lacking structural whiplash line and total-design ironwork.
- **Art Deco**: the successor — symmetry, zigzag and sunburst, chrome and lacquer, bold contrast, streamlined arc; its curves are machined sweeps, never growing vines.
- **Vienna Secession trap**: white cubes with gilded openwork domes read geometric, but the gold is organic laurel foliage and the era pre-Deco; check for plant logic inside the geometry before reclassifying.
