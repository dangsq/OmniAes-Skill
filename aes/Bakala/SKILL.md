# Bakala (Makinero / Ruta Destroy / Rutero / Pastillero)

Bakala is the visual and tribal culture of the Spanish youth who turned the Valencia club circuit — Barraca, Chocolate, Spook Factory, Puzzle, ACTV, Espiral, NOD along the El Saler road (CV-500), later the Catalan temples Pont Aeri, Chasis, Scorpia, Xque — into a continuous weekend ritual from Friday to Monday morning. It is a double aesthetic: on one side a genuinely sophisticated graphic-design movement ("Diseño Destroy") of flyers, posters and compilation covers produced by art-school designers; on the other a hard, functional party uniform evolved from New Romantic and post-punk roots into a militaristic sportswear look under Gabber and Skinhead influence. Binding the two is a third register: the amateur snapshot archive — flash-lit group photos in parking lots, in front of club façades, in bedrooms and on dancefloors — which is how most surviving imagery of the scene actually looks. Born from the post-Franco liberation of the Movida Valenciana, peaking as a mass phenomenon in the early-to-mid 1990s, stigmatized by media over speed, ecstasy and fatal road accidents, and later absorbed into Pokero and Cani stereotypes, the style now also lives as a nostalgia industry ("Remember" events, the 2022 series *La Ruta*, the IVAM "Ruta Gráfica" exhibition).

## Visual Identity

### Palette rule

The palette is high-voltage flat colour colliding with black ink and paper white. The infobox anchors it: the safety orange of the reversed bomber lining, black, neon green, yellow and denim blue. The printed matter widens this with signal red, hot magenta and cyan as flat overprint colours. Two handling modes exist and may be mixed:

- **Graphic mode (flyers, posters, logo sheets, compilation covers):** unmodulated, flat saturated fields; often a strict two-colour split (blue/yellow, pink/black), or black-and-white ink plus a single violent overprint (usually red or orange); bone-white or photocopy-grey paper grounds; no gradients, no shading except halftone dots.
- **Photo mode (snapshots, club interiors):** faded film colour where the orange lining, a bleached blonde top or a neon garment pops against night black, denim blue and concrete grey; club interiors lit by a single gel colour (green, red, pink); dawn light washed pale after a 72-hour session.

Approximate anchors (use as a range, not a formula): safety orange `#E85D04–#FF7A00`; ink black `#0B0B0C`; bone paper / photocopy grey `#F4F1E6–#C9C9C9`; neon yellow `#F2D300`; acid/neon green `#39E600–#7FFF00` (documented in text and in club gel light, rarer in printed matter — treat as accent); denim blue `#3E5F8A–#6F8CAF`; signal red `#D92318`; hot magenta `#E5007D`; cyan `#19C3D6`.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **The "Cenicero" (Ashtray) / "Uve" haircut:** shaved sides, longer flat or spiked top, distinct bang combed forward; aggressively bleached tops from platinum blonde to "butane orange." The single most identifying body marker.
2. **The MA-1 bomber jacket worn reversed** to expose the orange lining; alongside it the status-symbol "Pedro Gómez" puffer jacket and leather jackets.
3. **Macro-discotheque iconography:** club logotypes as tribal heraldry — Spook's bat wings, Barraca's hut and rocket, the ACTV oval, Puzzle, Chocolate, Espiral, NOD, El Templo; route maps of the CV-500; logotype sheets listing the whole circuit; "entrada libre / entrada gratuita" flyer conventions.
4. **Diseño Destroy graphic vocabulary:** high-contrast halftone comic faces (often a woman with red lips or red nails), crosshair/target marks, jagged ink shapes, film-strip and checkerboard border strips, rockets and missiles, bat silhouettes, Bauhaus-derived geometric typography, hand-drawn comic lettering, collage of found vintage illustration over photo fragments.
5. **Brand-tribe sportswear:** Kill Off (wizard logo), Rottweiler, Dj's Band, Scottish Clan, Boy London, Nike Air Max / Air Max BW; customized sportswear as group loyalty.
6. **Gold jewelry:** chains and medallions worn over tight tops — modest hardware, not iced-out bling.
7. **Women's silhouette:** extremely wide bell-bottoms ("pata de elefante") or straight-leg trousers, Art Company platform boots, tight tops with manga-inspired illustration (Welcome 2 Paradise).
8. **Cars and speed:** hatchbacks of the era, convoys on the route, aggressive parking-lot parties, the exhausted dawn drive home; speed as value and as threat.
9. **Compilation artefacts:** megamix covers (Maquina Total, Bit Music Greatest Hits, Bolero Mix, Lo + Duro) sold at gas stations, often with low-budget early-3D renders and chrome type — a deliberately cheaper sub-register of the graphic language.
10. **Dance and endurance cues:** the Pont Aeri school's jumping, high knees and flying kicks at the drop; the Levantine "Masiero" shuffle with hands near the head; paella served at dawn; Monday-morning bodies still standing.

### Materials and textures

Screen-print ink on paper with visible fold creases, tape marks and staple holes; photocopy and halftone grain; coarse newsprint offsets. Nylon bomber sheen and quilted puffer gloss; stiff denim; worn leather; rubber platform soles. In photography: 35mm compact-camera grain, on-camera flash hotspots and red-eye, slight motion blur on dancefloors, overexposed daylight on parking-lot concrete. In club interiors: haze, strobe stutter, single-colour gel light. Compilation covers add cheap early-CGI chrome and 3D bevels as a knowingly low-budget texture.

### Recurring subjects

Group portraits of the tribe (arms crossed, shoulders squared, facing the camera as a unit); club façades and their signs at night or in flat daylight; parking lots full of hatchbacks between clubs; bedroom shrines with club posters and flags on the walls; dancefloor and stage moments under gel light; flyer and poster surfaces themselves as the subject (posted, stacked, collaged); logo sheets and route maps; cassette and CD megamix objects; dawn exteriors after the session.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

Graphic work favours modernist structure: constructivist grids, bold diagonal splits, oversized logotypes anchored top or bottom, border strips framing the sheet, collage layers of ink face + photo fragment + geometric block. Photography favours the unschooled snapshot: centred groups, straight-on framing, horizon at chest height, everything in focus, flash flattening the night. Lighting is therefore either **flat graphic colour with no modelled light at all**, or **hard direct flash / flat overcast daylight / single-gel club light**. Soft studio lighting, golden-hour romance and cinematic rim light are foreign to this style.

## Mood & Values

Hedonism without apology; a "destroy" mentality that treats the weekend as a campaign to be survived; tribalism expressed through brands, club loyalty and haircut; endurance as prestige (72 hours continuous); speed as pleasure and as identity; genuine music connoisseurship beneath the hard exterior (from EBM and New Beat to 160+ BPM Mákina). The mood is euphoric aggression: grins and shaved heads, confetti-free ecstasy, communal exhaustion turned into pride. Underneath runs a current of nostalgic melancholy in the revival era — the same imagery now also means memory, loss and rehabilitation of a stigmatized generation.

## Era & Origins

Valencia, Spain, late 1980s to early 2000s, rooted in the post-1975 democratic transition and the Movida Valenciana. Three evolutionary stages shape the visual range: the **Bakalaero** (80s–early 90s) — sophisticated, avant-garde, post-punk/EBM/New Beat, darker and more "Moderno"; the **Makinero** (mid-late 90s) — the defining archetype with sportswear, military elements and Mákina euphoria; and the **Hardcoriano** — the hardest subset, shaved heads and bombers longest retained, gabber/terrorcore taste. Mid-90s massification brought media panic, police checkpoints, political polarization (Españolistas vs Catalanistas, visible in fractured club logos) and club closures by ~1995; the residue flowed into Pokero and Cani. The 2020s revival (Remember events, *La Ruta*, IVAM exhibition) reframes the aesthetic as heritage, apolitical and music-first.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Pick a register first: Diseño Destroy graphic artefact, tribe snapshot, or club/compilation object. Each has its own rules above; mixing registers (a snapshot of someone holding a flyer) is idiomatic.
2. Honour the palette rule: flat saturated fields against black ink and paper white in graphic work; orange lining, bleached hair and denim as the colour events in photographic work.
3. Keep an analog substrate: print grain, halftone, fold and tape marks, film grain, flash hotspot. Nothing may look rendered, vector-clean or app-filtered.
4. Use period-plausible Spanish text sparingly and correctly in spirit: club names, "entrada libre", "verano 89", "nochevieja", route names, BPM-era slogans. Geometric modernist type or hand-drawn comic lettering, never contemporary sans-serif branding.
5. Dress the body by the rules, not by costume cliché: cenicero cut with bleached top, reversed bomber showing orange, gold chain, bell-bottoms or straight trousers with platform boots on women, Air Max-era sneakers.
6. Light it flat: direct flash, overcast daylight, single-gel club light, or no light modelling at all in graphic mode.
7. Carry the values: endurance, speed, tribe. Dawn light, parking lots, convoys, sweat and Monday-morning eyes sell the culture better than any logo.

**Variation axes (what may change)**

- *Subject matter:* any flyer, poster, logo sheet, route map, compilation cover, comic panel in the Makinavaja vein, bedroom, parking lot, dancefloor, car convoy, beach-club terrace, runway homage, group portrait, solo portrait, object still-life of cassettes and patches.
- *Composition:* modernist grid, diagonal two-colour split, dense collage, single monumental logotype, centred snapshot group, candid dancefloor moment.
- *Lighting:* flash night, flat daylight, dawn pallor, single-gel interior, strobe stutter, or pure graphic flatness.
- *Colour handling:* monochrome ink plus one red overprint; strict two-colour split; full neon block ensemble; faded film snapshot; cheap chrome-3D compilation mode.
- *Mood:* euphoric, aggressive, tender-nostalgic, avant-garde cold, exhausted-after-72-hours.
- *Era register:* dark post-punk Bakalaero, neon Makinero, hardened Hardcoriano, or contemporary Remember-revival staging.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A poster with no people, a parking lot with no logos, or a bedroom with only a bleached fringe and an orange lining can all be perfectly Bakala.

**Avoid**

- Clean vector gradients, glassy 3D, corporate minimalism, modern sans-serif brand systems, smartphone-era UI or selfie framing.
- UK acid-house smileys, baggy Madchester clothes, whistles and glo-sticks; US rave candy-kid gear.
- Modern EDM festival grammar: LED walls, confetti cannons, main-stage pyro, headliner drop poses.
- Iced-out hip-hop bling, trap/drill fashion, post-2010 sneaker silhouettes, smartphones in hand.
- Tourist-Spain clichés: flamenco dress, guitars, feria postcard colour.
- Cyberpunk neon-glow futurism; Bakala neon is printed flat or worn as fabric, not emitted by cities.
- Political or extremist symbols of any faction: they belong to the scene's terminal fracture, are banned by the revival, and are not part of the aesthetic identity.
- Generic Dutch gabber or German EBM uniformity without the Spanish colour, flyer culture and club heraldry (see discrimination).
- Soft, romantic, cinematic lighting and shallow-depth "portrait lens" bokeh.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Pokero:** Pokero is the 2000s Spanish successor — tracksuit sets, caps, the "pokerito" dance, suburban street framing. Bakala keeps the 90s bomber-with-orange-lining, the cenicero cut, platform boots and bell-bottoms, and above all the canonical flyer/print culture; Pokero has no equivalent graphic canon and reads a decade later.
- **Vs Cani:** Cani (2010s) is street-centred: fake-designer sportswear, caps, sunglasses at night, no club-ritual endurance and no macro-club heraldry. Bakala is its ancestor but is defined by the route, the 72-hour ritual, bleached cenicero fringes and the Diseño Destroy legacy.
- **Vs Biker:** Biker culture is leather, motorcycles and rock/metal, older and road-romantic. Bakala borrows leather jackets and aggression but pairs them with nylon bombers, sneakers or platforms, electronic music iconography and flat neon print — never chrome-and-chopper imagery.
- **Vs Electronic Body Music:** EBM aesthetics are Teutonic-cold: black leather, crew cuts, grey industrial minimalism, austere type. The Bakalaero phase shares the taste (Front 242, Nitzer Ebb) but the Spanish scene adds neon colour blocks, comic halftone collage, mass daylight parking-lot tribalism and euphoric hedonism that EBM's severity rejects.
- **Vs Gabber:** Gabber (Dutch hardcore) overlaps on shaved heads, bombers and aggression, but its visuals are rawer and darker: Australian tracksuits, hardcore logos, no art-school flyer tradition, no women's platform-and-bell-bottom silhouette, no orange-lining colour event. Bakala's hardcoriano edge always sits inside a colour-saturated, designer-printed club culture.
- **Vs generic Rave / New Beat:** Second-summer-of-love rave is smiley, baggy and pastel-bright; New Beat is Belgian and slower-cool. Bakala's tell is the Spanish club logotype sheet, the CV-500 route mythology, the flash-snapshot parking lot and the orange-on-black print language uniting all of it.
