# Photorealism

Photorealism is the American movement, emerging in the late 1960s and early 1970s and named by dealer Louis K. Meisel, in which painters and sculptors reproduce the camera's image by hand: a photograph is projected or gridded onto canvas and translated with such precision that the handmade copy rivals its mechanical source. It reacts against Abstract Expressionism and Minimalism by returning to representation, but its truth-source is the lens, not the eye — so its pictures carry photographic behaviors: mirror reflections, depth-of-field, snapshot cropping, flash-flat light. Its content is deliberately unheroic: cars, diners, storefronts, cosmetics, candy, fruit, tools, ordinary workers. The movement's deeper subject is perception itself — a painting of a photograph asking what "real" means.

## Visual Identity

### Palette: a fidelity rule, not a fixed set
The style's only color law is documentary accuracy to the source photograph; local color is reported, not interpreted. Within that law, the documented range runs between three poles:
- **Saturated commercial pole** — candy and produce primaries: lipstick red `#C8102E`–`#E4572E`, tangerine `#F2A007`, lemon `#F5D000`, apple green `#7FB800`, cobalt `#1F4E9C`, cosmetic pink `#E88FA0`; high chroma, glossy, lit to show specular dots.
- **Muted documentary pole** — street and diner tones: Formica beige `#D8CDBA`, counter teal `#4E7A72`, ketchup maroon `#7E2B1F`, mustard `#C9A227`, wood brown `#6B4A2F`, aluminum gray `#C4C9CE`.
- **Grayscale pole** — the full tonal ladder `#0B0B0B`–`#F7F7F7` for photographic portrait work.
Neutral grounds recur across poles: seamless warm gray `#D9D4CC`, cool gray `#BFBFBF`, off-white `#F2F1EE`, and, for isolated refraction studies, near-black `#101214`. Chrome and plate glass supply a constant accent family of whites `#E8EAEC`, mid grays `#6E767D` and mirror darks.

### Signature motifs, ranked as the text documents them
1. **Reflective surfaces** — storefront plate glass, car bodies, chrome trim, mirrors; reflections double the world and embed pictures within pictures.
2. **Urban landscapes and storefronts** — shop windows, turnstiles, awnings, letter-perfect commercial signage and logo typography.
3. **Portraits** — frontal, unidealized heads, every pore and hair reported; also lifecast-scale ordinary figures (the movement's sculptural branch: workers, cleaners, painters posed deadpan on neutral grounds).
4. **Still lifes of everyday objects** — fruit, cosmetics and perfume bottles, crayons, candy and gumball machines, desserts, diner condiments, marbles and glass spheres; arrangements may fill the frame edge-to-edge or sit isolated on black.
5. **Diners** — counters, napkin dispensers, ketchup and mustard bottles, pie plates.
6. **Motorcycles and vehicles** — documented in the movement's motif list and artist roster (chrome-and-enamel close-ups), though less frequent in surviving exemplars; treat as licensed subject matter.

### Materials and textures
Chrome, plate glass, mirror, vinyl, Formica, enamel paint, foil, sugar glaze and whipped cream, wax crayon, refractive glass, printed paper labels, dew drops on fruit skin, paint-spattered cotton work clothes. The paint surface itself is a material statement: airbrush-smooth gradients, razor edges, gloss-varnish sheen, zero visible brushstroke or impasto.

### Composition and lighting
Single-lens viewpoint always: one camera position, one moment. Cropping behaves like a snapshot — objects cut by the frame edge, signage sliced mid-letter. Portraits and figures are frontal and centered on seamless neutral grounds; still lifes are overhead or close-cropped, packing the frame with glossy solids; street scenes stack reflections into layered grids. Light is even and diffuse — daylight through storefront glass, studio flatness, or flash-like neutrality — with specular highlights doing the descriptive work on every curved glossy surface and soft contact shadows anchoring objects. No theatrical chiaroscuro; drama comes from detail density, not lighting.

## Mood & Values

Cool, deadpan, democratic. The style values technical virtuosity, objectivity and documentary precision, and rejects abstraction's gesture and Minimalism's reduction alike. Its emotional temperature is uneventful clarity: a quiet reverence for the mundane — a housepainter mid-stroke, a delivery man resting on his cart, a counter of condiments — rendered with the care once reserved for history painting. Beneath the neutrality runs an epistemological curiosity (painting as a question about photography and vision) and, in the crowded vanity still lifes, an undercurrent of old vanitas tenderness toward perishable things.

## Era & Origins

United States, late 1960s–1970s; term coined by Louis K. Meisel. A reaction against the dominance of Abstract Expressionism and Minimalism, and a sibling-of-opposition to Pop Art: it shares Pop's commercial subject matter but refuses Pop's graphic flatness. Method is constitutive — photograph first, then projection or grid transfer onto canvas, airbrush and fine brushwork to erase the hand. Key figures: Richard Estes, Chuck Close, Audrey Flack, Ralph Goings, Robert Bechtle, Robert Cottingham, Charles Bell, and sculptor Duane Hanson.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
1. Imply a photographic source: one lens viewpoint, photographic focus behavior (uniform sharpness or slight falloff), snapshot cropping, color as the camera recorded it.
2. Erase the hand: smooth blended gradients, knife-sharp edges, no stroke, no canvas tooth.
3. Let reflections and refractions carry the virtuosity — mirror glass, chrome, glass globes that bend and double the scene.
4. Choose mundane, mass-produced, commercial American matter; treat it with monumental care regardless of scale.
5. Light evenly; describe gloss with specular highlights and soft contact shadows.
6. Render typography letter-perfect when signage appears.
7. Keep the affect deadpan: no sentimentality, no heroics, no narrative staging.

**Variation axes (all licensed)**
- *Subject*: any storefront, street fragment, vehicle, diner counter, cosmetic or candy or fruit arrangement, tool, worker figure, frontal portrait — including subjects absent from any reference set.
- *Composition*: full urban scene, edge-to-edge close crop, overhead still life, frontal figure on seamless ground, sliced signage detail.
- *Lighting*: daylight storefront, flat studio, flash-neutral interior, or black-ground isolation for refractive objects.
- *Color*: anywhere on the saturated-commercial ↔ muted-documentary ↔ grayscale span, unified by fidelity logic.
- *Mood*: from clinical neutrality to quiet vanitas warmth.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**
- Visible brushwork, impasto, painterly looseness, canvas texture.
- Invented theatrical lighting:Rembrandt chiaroscuro, golden-hour glow, rim-lit drama.
- Idealized, beautified or allegorical subjects; moralizing narrative.
- Abstract or gestural content; geometric reduction.
- Pop graphic devices: ben-day dots, flat poster color, comic outlines, ironic collage.
- Multiple or cubist viewpoints; dreamy soft focus; heavy film grain or lo-fi degradation.
- Decorative ornament, fantasy creatures, anachronistic props.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates Photorealism |
|---|---|
| **Realism** (traditional) | Realism observes directly, permits painterly handling and interpreted tone, favors historical or narrative subjects; Photorealism obeys the camera — reflections, depth-of-field, snapshot crops — with an invisible hand and contemporary commercial matter. |
| **Minimalism** | Minimalism reduces to bare geometric form and industrial fabrication with no illusionism; Photorealism shares only the cool deadpan attitude while filling the surface with maximal descriptive detail. |
| **Pop Art** | Pop flattens commercial imagery into bold outlines, dots and poster color with ironic appropriation; Photorealism renders the same commercial world with illusionistic depth, smooth gradients and straight-faced fidelity. |
| **Precisionism** | Precisionism (1920s US) geometrizes architecture into clean simplified planes, semi-abstract and orderly; Photorealism keeps snapshot randomness, clutter, letter-exact signage and complex mirror refraction that Precisionism edits out. |

Where evidence is thin: motorcycles and vehicles are documented in the movement's motif list and roster but appear rarely among surviving exemplars, so treat them as licensed rather than central; the black-ground refraction still life is attested by the image record (glass spheres, gumball globes) as a compositional pole within the still-life branch.
