# Visual Kei (VK, V-Kei, V系)

Visual Kei ("visual style") is a Japanese music movement, born in the 1980s, in which look is not packaging but half the art: visual presentation is an integral and equal component of the musical performance. It is an umbrella aesthetic, not a sound — the same visual codes can sit over glam metal, post-punk, gothic rock, industrial, J-pop or extreme metal. Its unifying grammar is the constructed, theatrical performer-body: engineered hair, painted face, layered costume, and a group portrait logic in which a band reads as one palette with several voices. The term itself was coined in 1991 by the editor-in-chief of SHOXX magazine from X Japan's slogan "Psychedelic Violence - Crime of Visual Shock," consolidating the earlier "okeshou" (makeup) band scene into a self-declared movement that fans and artists consciously join.

## Visual Identity

### Palette: the rule of committed poles

Visual Kei color is bipolar-to-quadripolar, and any single image commits to one pole; the infobox itself states the key colours "vary widely by sub-genre, from all-black to vibrant, multi-colored palettes." The working rule: **one costume palette pole per image, with hair dye allowed to cross poles as an accent** (a crimson or violet head on an all-black body is classic).

- **Void gothic pole (Kote Kei, Nagoya Kei):** near-black `#08080A–#17171A`, oxblood and wine `#4A0D12–#7A1226`, deep plum `#2E1F3A–#4A2C5E`, cold silver-white highlights `#D8D8DE–#F2F2F5`. Often rendered monochrome or cold duotone.
- **Aristocratic pole (Tanbi Kei):** ivory and parchment `#EFE6D0–#F7F0E1`, antique gold `#B08D2E–#D4AF37`, bordeaux, antique rose, cream lace over brocade.
- **Pop pole (Oshare Kei):** candy pink `#FF6FA5–#F9A8D4`, turquoise `#2EC4B6`, lemon `#F5D547`, orange `#FF8C42`, laid on white or pastel grounds with clashing patterns.
- **Traditionalist pole (Wafuu Kei):** black, vermillion, indigo and gold on kimono silk.
- **Hair accent dyes (any pole):** crimson `#C1121F`, magenta `#D6218C`, violet `#7B2CBF`, platinum `#E5E4E2`, ash blonde, jet black — usually as solid blocks or streaks/segments, not ombré softness.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Extravagant hair — the loudest element.** Flashy, spiked, backcombed, gravity-defying silhouettes; colorful segments or streaks; asymmetric fringes sweeping over one eye; long straight hime-cuts; teased crowns and feather or ornament inserts. Hair silhouette is read before anything else.
2. **Dramatic makeup on a pale canvas.** Pale skin or full shironuri whiteface; heavy kohl-rimmed, lined eyes; dark lips (wine, black, deep red); occasional face paint, glitter or drawn motifs around the eyes; piercings.
3. **Highly elaborate layered outfits with accessory density.** Gloves, rings, platform shoes and boots (New Rock, Demonia, Gordon Jack platforms), lots of patterned cloth; corsets, harness straps with O-rings, buckles, chains and studs as gothic-punk hardware (the scene's vendors — h.NAOTO, Punk Rave, Sex Pot Revenge — sit in that lineage).
4. **Androgyny.** Masculine-presenting performers in feminine or gender-ambiguous silhouettes: lace, corsetry, gowns, sheer sleeves, doll-like faces.
5. **Historical and fantastical themes.** Rococo, Baroque and Victorian princely coats, jabots and ball gowns (Tanbi Kei); modernized kimonos, yukata, samurai attire, fans and masks (Wafuu Kei).
6. **Pattern as identity.** Checkers, stripes, damask, florals, polka dots — worn in cloth or used as flat graphic backdrops in promo art.

### Materials and textures

Gloss against matte is the core texture contrast: vinyl, PVC and patent leather sheen against matte shironuri skin and dead-black cloth; lace jabots, ruffles and frilled cuffs; corsetry and boning; brocade, damask and velvet on the aristocratic pole; fur and feather trim; fishnet and torn gauze on the grotesque edge; heavy metal hardware (rings, O-rings, chains, studs); chunky platform soles with visible tread.

### Recurring subjects

Nearly always the performer: band group portraits of four to five members, solo performer portraits, staged promotional tableaux, and — a documented and legitimate medium — illustrated promo art in which chibi or anime-stylized avatars of the same costumed figures stand centered on flat decorative fields (polka dots, checks, florals, damask, vignette frames). Street-snap shots of coordinated fashion appear at the margins. The subject is a persona, not a character cosplay: an invented stage self.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

- **Group lineup:** horizontal row or staggered depth stack of members, each with a distinct hair color and costume variant inside one shared palette; posed stillness, direct or averted theatrical gaze, no candid action.
- **Framing:** three-quarter or full-body studio portraits; bust shots for makeup emphasis; centered solo figure on a patterned field in illustration mode.
- **Backdrops:** dark seamless studio cloth, painted or mottled canvas, satin curtains and period interiors on the aristocratic pole, flat graphic pattern fields in promo art.
- **Lighting:** hard direct flash with fast falloff into black; cold blue-white key with chiaroscuro for the dark pole; frequent monochrome or desaturated cold grading; even high-key brightness for the pop pole; soft warm interior light for Tanbi tableaux.

## Mood & Values

Theatricality as conviction: beauty is constructed, deliberate, artificial, and therefore free. The dark pole carries melancholy grandeur, menace, grotesque romance; the aristocratic pole carries regal decadence; the pop pole carries playful, cheeky brightness. Under all poles sit the movement's stated values — visual self-expression, artistic freedom, rebellion against conformity, musical diversity — and an androgyny that treats gender presentation as raw material. Membership is conscious and declared: the look says "I am in this scene."

## Era & Origins

Late-1980s Japan, crystallizing from early-80s Japanese goth and post-punk (Auto-Mod, Madame Edwarda, Phaidia, Die Zibet), which themselves absorbed theatrical Western goth and neo-glam (Japan, Hanoi Rocks, Bauhaus, Gene Loves Jezebel, Christian Death); Western glam rock, goth and punk are the acknowledged root stock, transformed into a uniquely Japanese phenomenon. Named in 1991; the 1990s Kote Kei (dark, gothic, grotesque, vinyl-and-lace) is the archetypal form; the early 2000s split off bright Oshare Kei from Harajuku street fashion, while Tanbi, Nagoya and Wafuu Kei codified other poles. The movement reacts against conformity and against the idea that music can be judged by sound alone.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the performer as one designed system: hair silhouette + painted face + layered costume + hardware, decided together.
2. Commit the image to a single palette pole; let hair dye be the only cross-pole accent.
3. Hair first: tease, spike, tower, asymmetrize — the silhouette must read at thumbnail size.
4. Pale canvas: matte pale or shironuri base, rimmed eyes, defined dark or bold lips.
5. Layer the costume: base layer, corset or harness, jacket or coat, then accessories (gloves, rings, chains, platform boots). Nothing is a single garment.
6. In group images, unify the palette but differentiate every member — identical uniforms are wrong.
7. Grade with intent: monochrome/cold for dark pole, clean saturated for pop, soft warm for aristocratic.
8. Pose theatrically: still, confrontational or melancholic; the body is on stage even in a still image.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be a group promo of three to six members, a solo portrait, a staged tableau, an illustrated promo card or chibi avatar, or a marginal street snap — a correct image can use any subject within this range, including invented bands that match no reference; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. Composition may range from horizontal lineup to staggered depth stack to centered solo to framed vignette. Lighting may range from hard flash on black to cold chiaroscuro to even high-key to candle-warm interior. Color handling may range from strict monochrome to full candy saturation, per the pole rules. Mood may range from menacing to melancholic to regal to playful. Subgenre pole (dark, aristocratic, pop, traditionalist, subdued Nagoya black-tailoring) is a free choice; note that the Nagoya and Wafuu poles are documented mainly by text rather than by abundant imagery, so treat their visual detail with restraint.

**Avoid.**
- Casual normcore: plain tee, jeans, sneakers, unstyled natural hair, tan or "no-makeup" skin.
- Minimalism of any kind — single-garment outfits, sparse accessories, clean negative space as a virtue.
- Candid documentary realism: motion blur, snapshot body language, golden-hour naturalism, beige film-desaturation.
- Identical uniforms across members; or a lone generic "goth model" with no band-promo logic and no hair architecture.
- Western glam sequin jumpsuits or glitter bodysuits worn alone, without VK layering and hair volume.
- Pure Gothic Lolita street coordinate presented as fashion documentation rather than performer costume (see discrimination).
- Sci-fi futurism, steampunk gadgetry, boho earth tones, sportswear, business attire — none belong to any pole.
- Soft ombré or balayage hair; VK dye is blocky, streaked, saturated.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Angura Kei:** the traditionalist, underground-theater-rooted neighbor; it leans Showa-retro Japanese folk mood, rags and kimono with shironuri, and a rawer theatrical poverty. Visual Kei at large is glossier, band-promo formatted, hair-driven, and Western-goth-derived; its own Wafuu Kei keeps kimono but pairs them with rock-band styling, platform boots and polished promo finish. (Textual documentation of Angura is thin — treat it as the folk-theater extreme of the traditionalist pole.)
- **Glam Rock:** 1970s Western ancestor — sequins, satin jumpsuits, glitter, bob or mullet hair, bright stage light. VK inherits the flash but adds Japanese post-punk darkness, corset-and-harness hardware, pale shironuri skin, far bigger teased-and-spiked hair, and the group-lineup promo composition.
- **Gothic Lolita:** a street fashion, not a music scene: bell-shaped petticoat skirts, bonnets, modest doll makeup, coordinate photography. VK is performer costume: masculine tailoring, military braid or harnesses mixed with lace, heavy kohl, spiked hair, stage gaze. The overlap is real at the Tanbi pole (ball gowns, aristocracy), so decide by context — band promo with varied gender presentation and dramatic makeup is VK; a lone coordinate street snap is Lolita.
- **New Romantic:** early-80s London club ancestor — pirate frills, satin, asymmetrical fringe, soft eyeliner. Softer, warmer, less armored: no platform-boot hardware density, no shironuri, no spiked tease. VK is sharper, darker, more layered.
- **Rokku:** Japanese rock-inspired street style — band tees, leather riders, skinny denim, at most smudged liner. It is fashion borrowed from rock; VK is rock's own total costume system with theatrical makeup and unnatural hair. (This neighbor is thinly documented; use the elaboration threshold as the test.)

**One-line test:** if the hair silhouette, painted pale face and layered costume read as a designed stage persona inside a committed palette pole — and the image feels like a band declaring itself — it is Visual Kei.
