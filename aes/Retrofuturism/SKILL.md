# Retrofuturism

Retrofuturism is the "remembering of anticipation": art that shows tomorrow as yesterday saw it. It is not prediction but memory-of-expectation — the future frozen in the design language, materials and social codes of the era that imagined it. Because of this, every retrofuturist image is built from two stacked layers: a **period layer** (medium, palette, costume, architecture, typography of some past decade) and a **speculative layer** (machines, cities and garments that never existed, drawn at that era's technical imagination-horizon). The style lives in the friction between them: a woman in a 1900s gown operating a videophone console; a Beaux-Arts dome sharing a street with a stepped Art Deco tower; a train with aircraft fins flying over a gorge. The umbrella spans many sub-dialects — Steampunk, Dieselpunk, Raygun Gothic, Space Age, Atompunk, Cassette Futurism, Y2K Futurism — so no single palette or subject defines it; what defines it is the anachronism rule and the period-consistent rendering of the impossible.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette.** The infobox concedes color "varies by subgenre," and the evidence bears this out: the honest rule is *one period-consistent palette family per image*, never a free mix. The recurring families are: (1) **antique print** — sepia and ink monochrome on aged paper (paper `#E8DCC0`–`#F2E8D5`, sepia ink `#6B5B45`–`#2E2A24`); (2) **chromolithograph pastels** — cream grounds with rose `#D98A8A`–`#E8B4B4`, mint `#A8C8B4`, powder sky `#A9C6D9`, brass `#C9A227`; (3) **atomic-age vibrant** — teal `#1F7A8C`–`#2AA5A0`, coral-pink `#E86A8A`, mustard `#E0A526`, chrome silver `#C0C4C8`–`#E8EAEC`, deep space navy `#10254F`–`#1B3A6B`; (4) **Space Age plastic** — molded white `#F5F2EC` and warm monochrome apricot/peach interiors `#E8B48C`–`#F2C9A8`; (5) **muted industrial** — soot and oxide greys `#3A3A38`–`#55524C` for the dystopian register. Across all families, metallic accent (chrome, brass, polished steel) is the constant thread, and saturation is always mediated by a medium texture — paper grain, halftone dot, gouache brush, plastic sheen — never raw digital neon.

**Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence.** 1) **Flying vehicles**: airborne cars, trains and personal conveyances that keep the bulky, streamlined bodies of their terrestrial counterparts. 2) **Fantastical architecture**: exaggerated Art Deco, Streamline Moderne, Googie/Populuxe forms — sleek aerodynamic shells, rounded edges, domes, portholes, buildings shaped like landed spacecraft, world's-fair pavilions, stacked megastructures and pod/"UFO" houses. 3) **Robots, androids, cyborgs**: from clunky exposed-gear automatons to sleek stylized humanoids with visible circuitry or glowing components. 4) **Space exploration**: planets, rings, starfields, lunar-base habitats. 5) **Rayguns and energy weapons**: fantastical beams emitted from forms stylized with fins, domes and bright mid-century industrial color accents. 6) **"Futuristic old technology"**: advanced versions of obsolete devices — sophisticated shiny telephone operators, elaborate mechanical computing interfaces, dials, levers, pneumatic tubes, cathode-ray screens. 7) **Period fashion and domesticity met by machine**: streamlined jumpsuits with asymmetrical zippers, metallic textiles, chainmail, technical visors (the Cardin/Courrèges Space Age line), and bourgeois interiors invaded by apparatus. 8) **Visionary plans and cutaways**: cross-sections of shelters and utopian cities, aerial and isometric city views descended from world's-fair concept boards and Robida-style illustrations.

**Materials and textures.** Chrome and polished metal; bakelite and molded plastic; latex, metallic fabric and polished chainmail; concrete shells and glass domes. As *image* textures: etching cross-hatch, lithographic stipple, halftone newsprint dot, sepia print aging, pulp-magazine gouache, glossy plastic-era photography, and soft analog-style renders. The medium grain is part of the style, not an optional filter.

**Composition and lighting.** Machines are protagonists, drawn with catalog-like legibility — every dial and fin readable. Recurring structures: the central emblem (poster-style machine or building as hero), the interior tableau (period room plus anachronistic console), the aerial/isometric vista of the impossible city, the cross-section cutaway, and the small human figure in period dress dwarfed by or operating the machine. Geometry is bold: strong lines, symmetry, stacked verticals, sweeping curves. Lighting modes: flat even illustrative light in print modes; dramatic searchlight beams and light shafts in the noir/dystopian register (the *Metropolis* canyon); warm monochrome glow in pod interiors; starfield night for space subjects; localized glow from dials, tubes and energy beams.

## Mood & Values

Two poles, both authentic. The optimistic pole: wonder, naïve techno-utopianism, faith in progress, technology domesticated into comfort and spectacle — the world's fair promise, the "faux nostalgia" of a comforting future that never materialized. The critical pole: irony, hauntology, "nostalgia for the future" — melancholy for abandoned promises, the gap between the sleek tomorrow once expected and the messy present that arrived, technology as alienating rather than empowering. Most images sit nearer the optimistic pole in palette and brighter in mood; the dystopian pole darkens palette and adds grime, bureaucracy and scale-crushing monumentality. Either way the emotional core is *temporal dislocation*: tenderness toward an obsolete tomorrow.

## Era & Origins

The imagined futures themselves date from the late 19th to mid-20th century: Verne and Wells, Robida and Villemard's "France in the year 2000" cards, Alvim Corrêa's posters, 1930s "city in 2000" prints, pulp SF covers, World's Fair exhibits, Space Age advertising and Disneyland Tomorrowland architecture. The *label* is later: the term began circulating in the early 1980s (John M. Sladek credited), solidifying as a category once rapid, disorienting technological change and environmental and political anxiety from the 1970s onward made artists revisit old techno-utopian images with nostalgia, irony, or both. Retrofuturism therefore reacts twice: once in each era's hopeful projection, and again in our retrospective awareness that the projection failed.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. **Two-layer rule:** every image must combine a period style layer and a speculative technology layer; the anachronistic friction is the style. A purely historical scene or a purely modern sci-fi scene both fail.
2. **Medium-consistency rule:** choose one rendering medium (etching, chromolithograph, halftone print, pulp gouache, plastic-era photo, analog-style render) and keep its grain, palette family and drawing logic uniform across the whole frame.
3. **Machine-horizon rule:** technology must look like an extension of its period's industrial design — rivets, fins, domes, dials, vacuum tubes, chrome trim, bakelite panels, cathode-ray screens — never seamless, invisible computing.
4. **Palette-lock rule:** pick one of the five families above; keep metallic accent present; let the medium texture moderate saturation.
5. **Optimism/dystopia dial:** choose a pole and align palette, grime and scale to it.
6. **Legibility rule:** the speculative device should be readable and slightly proud of its setting, as if exhibited.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any of: domestic interior with machine, cityscape or megastructure, vehicle in flight, robot portrait, fashion plate, expo pavilion, cutaway plan, operator at console, pod-house exterior, space vista. Composition may range from poster-central emblem to aerial vista, interior tableau, cross-section, or quiet object still-life. Lighting may range from flat print light to beam-lit noir to warm pod glow to starfield. Color handling may range from sepia monochrome through lithographic pastel to atomic vibrant to plastic monochrome. Mood may range from naïve optimism to ironic melancholy. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a retrofuturist kitchen, harbor, concert hall or farm would all qualify if they obey the two-layer, medium and machine-horizon rules.

**Avoid:** seamless modern tech (bezels-less smartphones, flat touchscreens, contemporary drones, invisible AI interfaces); present-day clothing, streets or product design without period coding; digital-native gloss (glassmorphism, aurora gradients, soap bubbles, lens-flare cleanliness); pure cyberpunk neon-rain noir with no period mediation; generic modern sci-fi spacecraft with no historical design vocabulary; pure historical reproduction with no speculative layer; raw saturated neon untethered from any print or plastic medium; decay-porn ruin without the period-future frame; mixing multiple palette families or medium grains in one image.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Atompunk** is retrofuturism locked to the 1950s–60s Cold War: chrome tailfins, Googie diners, nuclear motifs, atomic-age domesticity. Retrofuturism is the wider umbrella — it can speak in an 1890s lithograph or 1930s sepia dialect and carries the meta-awareness of "the future that never arrived"; if an image is strictly atomic-age chrome-and-pastel with no older print sensibility or ironic distance, Atompunk is the sharper label.
- **Cyberpunk** speculates in the present tense: near-future digital dystopia, neon, rain, corporate netrunners. Retrofuturism's machines are analog-mechanical (dials, tubes, levers, CRTs) and the image reads as remembered anticipation; only a frozen-1980s cassette-futurist treatment pulls it back under this umbrella.
- **Dieselpunk** occupies the interwar 1920s–40s: riveted steel, soot, grease, Art Deco noir, war machinery. Retrofuturism also includes light pastels, chromolithograph whimsy and white Space Age plastic; soot-and-steel wartime grit alone is Dieselpunk.
- **Frutiger Aero** (mid-2000s–2010s) is glossy eco-optimism: bubbles, water droplets, aurora skies, grass-and-glass skeuomorphism, digital cleanliness. Retrofuturism keeps period print grain or analog machine bodies and never uses bubble/aurora gloss.
- **Raygun Gothic** is the Art Deco pulp-SF subset: finned rockets, zigzurat cities, rayguns, planet vistas in deco geometry. Retrofuturism additionally embraces domestic interiors, fashion, urban plans, 19th-century etchings and the melancholic register; if every element is deco pulp iconography, name Raygun Gothic first.

**Quick recognition test:** an obsolete medium's grain (stipple, halftone, hatch, plastic sheen) + an impossible machine drawn in that medium's own design vocabulary + a whiff of either wonder or wistfulness = retrofuturism.
