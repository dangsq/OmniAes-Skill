# Adventure Pulp

Adventure Pulp romanticizes the thrill of early twentieth-century exploration as it was sold on newsstands: cheap pulp magazines and film serials in which rugged, highly competent adventurers chase lost artifacts and ancient secrets through deserts, jungles and forgotten temples. It fuses two registers that normally repel each other — the dust-and-sweat action of the field expedition and the quiet scholasticism of the university study — and wraps both in a nostalgic, pre-digital lens. Its roots reach back to the late Victorian and Edwardian "Lost World" fiction of H. Rider Haggard and Arthur Conan Doyle; its visual identity was hammered into place by the painted cover art of 1930s–40s pulp magazines; and its modern form was fixed for popular memory by the Indiana Jones franchise and its descendants (The Mummy, Jungle Cruise, Tomb Raider, Uncharted). Everything in the style reads as an artifact of that lineage: either a painted cover, a printed page, an expedition photograph, or a modern homage shot through the same warm, worn filter.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is an earth-dominated base with small, hot focal accents. Roughly three quarters of any frame should sit in the warm neutral band — leather browns, dust khaki, olive drab, ochre sand, parchment off-white — while saturated color is rationed into focal points: a masthead yellow, a signal-red shirt, a slab of jungle green, the gold of an idol. A second legitimate handling collapses the palette entirely into sepia or grainy monochrome browns, the idiom of period expedition photography and dim archive interiors. Cool hues appear only as sky or shadow (dusty lavender-blue skies, umber blacks), never as a modern teal grade.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **The fedora hat** — the single most essential element; visual shorthand for the whole aesthetic. A tropical sun/pith helmet is the accepted warm-climate variant.
2. **The adventurer's kit**: well-worn leather jacket or khaki safari shirt, sturdy trousers, tall leather boots, leather satchel, coiled bullwhip at the hip.
3. **Old maps and esoteric artifacts** — rolled and folded maps, idols, tablets, reliquaries, cursed-looking stones; the MacGuffin around which scenes organize.
4. **Ancient ruins** — sun-scorched Egyptian desert dotted with broken columns and temple facades; overgrown jungle concealing forgotten temples and gorges.
5. **The cluttered scholar's office** — dim university professor's rooms stacked with artifacts, crates, books, pinned notices and old maps; the indoor twin of the ruin.
6. **Dieselpunk-era transport** — prop planes, steamships, vintage off-road vehicles; technology as background prop, never as hero.
7. **The pulp cover apparatus** — painted gouache scenes with bold masthead bands and title typography, the format that originally codified the style.
8. **Image-supported extensions** (present in the surviving artifact pool rather than spelled out in the text): desk still lifes of spectacles, pens and spread papers; hand-tinted or sepia studio expedition portraits; grainy monochrome interior studies; and "weird pulp" anomalies — impossible sky-filling phenomena witnessed by crowds in period dress — which stretch the text's esoteric/lost-world motif to its pulp-magazine extreme. Treat these as legitimate members of the family, ranked below the core seven.

### Materials and textures

Scuffed and creased leather (jackets, satchels, whip, boots, wingback chairs); pressed cotton twill and canvas (safari shirts, trousers, expedition gear); felt; brass buckles, clasps and compass housings; aged, foxed, creased paper (maps, letters, posters); rough-hewn wood crates, desks and table tops; weathered stone and sand; wet, dense foliage. Medium-level textures matter as much as object textures: halftone dot screens and paper grain in print idioms, film grain, dust specks and vignetting in photographic idioms, visible brushwork and gouache opacity in painted idioms. Nothing looks factory-new; patina is mandatory.

### Recurring subjects and settings

The adventurer (any gender) in competent pose — hand on hip, map in fist, whip ready; the expedition party moving through ruin or jungle; the professor amid archives; the vehicle paused at the edge of the unknown; the artifact held up to lamplight; the desk or crate table arranged as still life; the crowd of period-dressed witnesses looking up at something impossible. Settings cycle through three canonical rooms of the imagination: the desert ruin, the jungle temple, the cluttered study — plus their connective tissue (airfields, riverbanks, ship decks, museum stacks).

### Composition and lighting

Composition tends toward either heroic centrality or awe-scale contrast: a single dynamic figure anchored mid-frame with diagonal energy (the cover-art convention), or a tiny figure dwarfed by gorge, temple facade or sky event. Interiors are maximalist and cluttered, framed tight so objects crowd the edges; still lifes favor the overhead or three-quarter desk view. Lighting is era-coded: harsh near-noon sun with hard shadows in desert and jungle; dappled humid light under canopy; a single warm lamp or candle carving a pool of light out of a dark, crowded study; golden-key studio light for costumed portraits. Cover-format images may carry masthead bands, cover lines and period lettering as part of the composition.

## Mood & Values

The style celebrates adventuring, discovery, competency, scholasticism and escape from the mundane. Its hero reads books *and* outruns boulders: intellect and physical daring are the same virtue. The emotional register is romantic nostalgia with a serial's cliffhanger pulse — peril is real but ultimately survivable, wonder is always one map-fold away. There is tactile anti-modernism throughout: everything can be touched, folded, coiled, lit by flame or bulb, and nothing is wireless. The aesthetic is aware of itself as romance; it inherits colonial-era expedition iconography (pith helmets, porter-lined panoramas) as period costume vocabulary rather than documentary claim.

## Era & Origins

Literary origin: the Lost World genre of the 1880s–1910s (King Solomon's Mines, The Lost World), fed by real colonial expedition reports. Visual codification: the pulp magazines of the 1930s–40s (Adventure-type titles, Doc Savage 1933–1949), whose painted covers fixed the palette, poses and typography. Revival and modern codification: the 1980s homage films beginning with the Indiana Jones franchise (1981–), then The Mummy (1999), Jungle Cruise (2021) and game series (Tomb Raider, Uncharted, Strange Brigade). The style reacts against industrialized, screen-saturated modern life by offering an analog world where discovery still requires boots, paper and courage.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Earth-first palette: keep the frame dominated by browns, khaki, ochre and parchment; spend saturated color only on focal accents (masthead yellow, signal red, jungle green, artifact gold).
2. Patina everywhere: crease, scuff, dust, foxing, fade. No surface may look newly manufactured.
3. Technology ceiling circa 1950: prop planes not jets, maps and compasses not GPS, typewriters and telegrams not screens.
4. Scholar-adventurer duality: pair at least one intellect prop (book, map, spectacles, artifact label) with one action prop (whip, satchel, boots, revolver-era tool) whenever a character is present.
5. Choose one medium idiom and obey its texture rules end to end: gouache cover painting with brushwork; halftone offset print with dot screen and misregistration; sepia or grainy monochrome photograph with dust and vignette; or clean modern homage photography in period costume with warm natural light.
6. Light as period signal: single-source warm lamplight indoors, harsh sun or dappled canopy outdoors; deep umber shadows, never flat even illumination.
7. Cover energy when illustrative: dynamic diagonals, heroic central figure, optional masthead band and period lettering.
8. Awe by scale when scenic: dwarf the human against ruin, gorge, temple or sky event.

**Variation axes (allowed range)**

- *Subject*: desert ruin expedition, jungle temple infiltration, cluttered study or museum archive, airfield or riverbank departure, desk still life, costumed portrait, crowd-witnessed anomaly scene, period pulp sibling drift (war, western, weird-tale inflections) so long as the explorer palette and print language hold.
- *Composition*: heroic central figure; tiny figure vs. vast setting; maximalist cluttered interior; overhead still life; masthead cover layout; witness-crowd viewpoint.
- *Lighting*: hard noon sun, dappled jungle light, single-lamp noir interior, golden studio key, monochrome shadow play.
- *Color handling*: full earth palette with accents; saturated gouache cover color; sepia or hand-tinted photo; grainy monochrome.
- *Mood*: swaggering action, quiet scholastic absorption, hushed awe, low dread in a dark room.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A still life of a fedora and map on a crate in monochrome lamplight is as valid as a painted jungle chase with a masthead — neither needs to reproduce a known exemplar.

**Avoid**

- Modern gear and materials: nylon shells, plastic buckles, LEDs, smartphones, laptops, GPS units, digital cameras, jets, helicopters, modern SUVs.
- Modern color science: teal-orange blockbuster grades, neon, cyber-saturation, pastel dreaminess, cool white gallery light.
- Pristine luxury crispness: unworn linen, spa-lodge minimalism, fashion-editorial polish (that is Safari Chic).
- Steampunk signifiers: brass goggles, corsets, clockwork exoskeletons, steam-powered fantasy machines.
- Dieselpunk centrality: chrome art-deco machines, greasy hangar noir as the subject rather than background prop.
- Tiki kitsch: tiki bars, cocktails, mid-century Polynesian pop ornament.
- Minimalism and flat design: empty rooms, vector shapes, clean sans-serif or gradient typography on cover variants.
- High-fantasy magic: spell-slinging, glowing portals, non-human fantasy races; keep the esoteric artifact-based (idols, tablets, implied curses).
- Factory-new condition on any prop, costume or paper.

## Discrimination Cues

- **vs. Adventurecore**: Adventurecore is contemporary outdoor recreation — synthetic bright shells, trail signage, modern packs, daylight snapshot photography. Adventure Pulp is period costume, analog medium texture, ruins-as-mystery and narrative peril; swap the hiking backpack for a leather satchel and the trailhead for a temple gate.
- **vs. Dieselpunk**: shared technology era (prop planes, steamships), but Dieselpunk makes the machine the protagonist with art-deco chrome and industrial grease. Adventure Pulp keeps a human explorer and ancient stone in front; the plane is parked in the background.
- **vs. Safari Chic**: Safari Chic is lifestyle luxury — crisp linen, lodge interiors, neutral beige calm, leisure. Adventure Pulp is worn, dusty, cluttered and threatened; its khaki is sweat-stained, its rooms are stacked to the ceiling, and its palette admits hot pulp accents or noir monochrome.
- **vs. Soft Colonial Wanderlust**: that neighbor is faded-pastel postcard romance, parasols and gentle strolls in washed-out light. Adventure Pulp keeps pulp saturation or stark contrast, action poses, hard shadow and a sense of imminent danger or discovery.
- **vs. Steampunk**: Steampunk is Victorian brass-and-gears retro-futurism with goggles and clockwork. Adventure Pulp sits in the 1930s–40s: felt, leather, twill and paper, dieselpunk-era engines, no anachronistic steam fantasy.

**Quick recognition check**: fedora or sun helmet + worn leather and khaki + map/artifact/spectacles + earth palette with rationed hot accents + pre-1950 technology + patina or print grain = Adventure Pulp.
