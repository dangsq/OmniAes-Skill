# New England Gothic

New England Gothic revolves around strange and sinister secrets in the American Northeast: the inherited guilt of Puritan theocracy, the Salem Witch Trials, witchcraft and the occult, and the slow decay of colonial towns, harbors and mills. It is first of all a literary tradition — Washington Irving's swamp-side Faust (1824), John Neal's Salem novel (1828), and above all Nathaniel Hawthorne, credited with creating the style in 1835 — that later acquired a visual life through film, archival photography, the tempera realism of Andrew Wyeth, and the Regional Gothic trend that took off on Tumblr in early 2015, which added liminal space and formatted text posts to the canon. Its mood thesis is Hawthorne's own: the Past "lies upon the Present like a giant's dead body." Everything in the image language — weathered shingles, leaning slates, foxed paper, off-season emptiness — is that dead body, still warm under a gray sky.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** The style lives in a desaturated, dry band: overcast gray-greens and pewter skies, cool near-black shadow, bone white and snow glare, with warmth spent sparingly and never evenly — dried-grass ochre, leaf-rust and brick red, oxblood, and the single amber point of a candle or lit window. Period figures and signage may carry the Puritan "sad colors": dun brown, slate, mulberry, deep bottle green. Saturation stays low everywhere except small heraldic accents (a sign band, a barn door, autumn foliage); there is no candy color and no glossy highlight.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.** (1) Puritan and witch-trial memory: black-clad congregations, trial broadsides and sermon title pages, gallows associations, the meetinghouse. (2) Witchcraft and the occult: candlelit rites, hidden marks, night woods where faith fails. (3) Old graveyards and burying grounds: slate and sandstone stones, iron railings, leaf litter or snow between them. (4) Colonial-era vernacular architecture: saltbox, gambrel and cape massings, many-gabled dark houses, covered bridges, white steeples. (5) Run-down harbor and mill towns: wrecked hulls on mudflats, wharf pilings, tarred rope, lighthouses seen from shore, brick mills and stone asylums. (6) Supernatural entities — witches, ghosts, demons, the Devil, stranger things — usually implied rather than shown. (7) Woods, swamps and fieldstone walls: the Goodman Brown path, boundary walls of cleared colonial fields, bare hardwood stands. (8) Liminal roadside and off-season emptiness: welcome signs with mottoes, empty two-lane roads, patched snow, closed coast — the 2015 revival register. (9) Archive and antiquarian print: foxed laid paper, blackletter and serif title pages, engraved winter villages, glass-plate photographs with plate borders — "terrible knowledge" made physical.

**Materials and textures.** Weathered cedar shingle and clapboard; slate, sandstone and lichen; dried grass blade by blade (tempera crispness); bare branch lace against sky; snow crust, fog and sea haze; hull wood, rope, tar; aged paper with foxing and ink; engraving hatch marks; matte paint, film grain, plate emulsion flaws. Surfaces are matte and dry — dryness is both a palette and a value.

**Composition and lighting.** Horizon-dominated frames: a small house on a rise under a vast sky, a road receding to a vanishing point, headstone rows marching into depth, a symmetrical institutional facade, a document scanned flat and centered. Figures are dwarfed by terrain, turned away, low to the ground, or absent entirely. Light is diffuse overcast by default (no cast shadows), flat winter glare, blue-hour dusk, or night lit by one warm practical — candle, lantern, single window. Candlelit chiaroscuro interiors recall 19th-century genre painting. The threat stays off-frame or half-seen in a treeline; reticence is compositional, not just thematic.

## Mood & Values

The style expresses anti-Calvinism and anti-Puritanism, opposition to religious fanaticism, and an anti-colonial conscience aware of violence done to Native Americans and neighbors alike. Its emotional constants are isolation, predestination, reticence and dryness: dread held under restraint, a community that watches and says nothing, knowledge that arrives too late and cannot be unlearned. There is no sentimentality and little spectacle; the horror is moral and historical, carried in inheritance rather than in monsters. Even the region's practical Yankee plainness — simple food, seafood from a weird sea, a music tradition limited to shanties and sea ballads — feeds the aesthetic's spareness.

## Era & Origins

Origin decade 1835, with Hawthorne (shadowed by his unrepentant judge-ancestor of the Salem trials), building on Irving (1824) and Neal (1828) and on Dark Romanticism; extended by Lovecraft's seaside Massachusetts (from 1921), Shirley Jackson, Stephen King and others; on screen since 1910, with modern touchstones such as *The Witch* and *The Lighthouse*. The mid-2010s Tumblr Regional Gothic wave resurrected it, first through borrowed Southern Gothic clichés, then through liminal-space photography and formatted text posts. The style reacts against Puritan providence myth, colonial cruelty, and the Yankee suspicion of art for art's sake. Honest caveat: the wiki names only Andrew Wyeth among visual artists and documents no painting school or photographic movement; the archival, cinematic and liminal registers above are supported by the style's film lineage, its revival, and the surviving image record, but rank below the literary canon in textual authority.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Set weather before subject: overcast, fog, or flat winter light is the default sky; hard sun and golden hour are foreign.
2. Embed history in the frame: every surface should imply age and use — weathered shingle, leaning slate, foxed paper, a stone wall marking an old field boundary.
3. Practice reticence: suggest the supernatural (a shape in the treeline, a light in a sealed room); show it whole only rarely, and then in period dress or spectral pallor.
4. Spend warmth sparingly: one candle, one rust patch of leaves, one oxblood door against a field of gray and bone.
5. Keep it dry and matte: tempera crispness, desaturated film, print grain; no gloss, no humid haze.
6. Build with vernacular architecture: saltbox, gambrel, cape, meetinghouse steeple, covered bridge, brick mill, stone asylum — never castle or cathedral.
7. Allow liminal emptiness: empty road, sign, off-season beach, vacant lot are valid subjects in the revival register.
8. If text appears, keep it period serif, blackletter title-page, or plain roadside sign lettering with motto-like brevity.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any motif from the ranked list, or ordinary New England vernacular (sign, bridge, wall, harbor) made ominous. Season: dry late summer through deep winter; autumn foliage and snow both belong. Time of day: overcast noon, dusk, candlelit night, storm night. Medium: tempera painting, desaturated photograph, glass-plate archive, engraving, document scan, digital liminal photo. Supernatural dosage: none implied → hint → explicit entity. Human presence: absent, one distant or turned figure, or a small period-dressed group. Color handling: monochrome plate at one extreme, muted full palette with a single warm accent at the other. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Spanish moss, live oaks, plantation columns, bayou humidity and heat shimmer; medieval castles, cathedral naves, gargoyles, baroque gold; neon signage, candy saturation, chrome diner glamour, teal-orange blockbuster grading, lens flare; tropical or desert flora; glossy wet surfaces and bokeh fairy glow; cartoon Halloween kitsch (plastic skeletons, party bunting); crowds, festivals, cheerful summer beach scenes; coal tipples, banjo porches and rusted trailers (Appalachian leakage); glass towers and bright vinyl suburbia; gore and slasher blood spray; warm Mediterranean light.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Southern Gothic:** humidity versus dryness is the fastest tell. Southern Gothic brings moss-draped oaks, bayous, plantation verandas, heat and florid grotesquerie; New England Gothic brings bare hardwoods, fieldstone walls, slate and shingle, cold fog, and reticent Puritan guilt.
- **Appalachian Gothic:** mountain hollows, coal works, economic-abandonment folklore and a strong string-band music culture; New England Gothic is coastal-colonial, literate and archival, institution-heavy, and notably poor in music beyond shanties and sea ballads.
- **Dark Naturalism:** bleak nature with no haunting past. In New England Gothic every landscape element is indexed to human history — the wall marks a cleared field, the grove hides a burying ground; remove the past and you have left the style.
- **Dark Nautical:** the sea as subject. New England Gothic keeps maritime elements shore-bound and town-anchored (wreck on mudflat, lighthouse seen from land, wharf beside a graveyard); the sea is where the town's secrets arrive from, not the essence.
- **Gothic (European):** castles, cathedrals, aristocratic ruin and ornate sublime. New England Gothic is Protestant and vernacular — meetinghouse steeple instead of spire, clapboard instead of masonry, moral and historical dread instead of aristocratic decay.
