# Stonepunk

Stonepunk names works set roughly in the Stone Age whose characters wield Neolithic-Revolution-era technology — materials consistent with the period, but complexity and function that are deliberately anachronistic. Cars, houses, appliances, even computers appear re-imagined as if cave people built them from what lay to hand: stone discs for wheels, wood for axles, muscle, water, wind and tamed beasts for power. The style is a thought-experiment in inventory: everything a modern machine does, a stonepunk contraption must do with flint, bone, hide, reed and rope. It spans registers from domestic parody (creatures as living dishwashers and elevators) to post-apocalyptic awe (tribal hunters with antler bows facing robot beasts in a ruined world), but the material grammar never changes.

## Visual Identity

**Palette.** Warm mineral earth dominates the canvas, with bone as the structural neutral, oxide red as the signal accent, charcoal as line and shadow, and cool earthy blue held in reserve for sky and atmospheric depth. A correct image keeps roughly three-quarters of its surface in the earth/bone family; red appears in war paint, feather plumes, banners and ochre pigment; charcoal echoes the outline logic of cave painting; blue never leads.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how centrally the style documents them.**
1. *Anachronistic stone-age machines*: animal-powered vehicles on stone-disc wheels, rock or bone "computers" and control panels, mammoths rigged as cranes, dinosaurs doing the work of cars and elevators, hide-and-rope lifts. Function first, Paleolithic matter always.
2. *Material grammar*: rough stone blocks, carved menhirs, knapped flint, bone, antler, wood, leather, fur, woven reeds, crude clay and mudbrick; joints made of rope lashings, wooden wedges and pegs — never nails or bolts.
3. *Bone architecture and totems*: gates and fences of skulls, tusks and gigantic bones, rib-bone arches, menhir skylines, caves, cliff dwellings, stacked stone huts, timber frames infilled with hide or mud, megastructures carved into rock faces.
4. *Tribal engineers*: loincloths, fur mantles, leather wraps, bone jewellery, teeth and claw adornments, woven pieces, war paint, tattoos, tribal hairstyles — sometimes grafted with surprising "advanced" gear: goggles, stone armour plates, mechanical prosthetics carved from bone and scrap.
5. *Megafauna as partners and motors*: mammoths, dinosaurs, reptilian mounts in harness, saddles, panniers and plume-headgear.
6. *Dramatic prehistoric landscapes*: steppe, canyon, badlands, desert rock, tundra boulder fields; settlements perched, stacked or carved into them.
7. *Ritual and myth layer*: skull masks, altars, processions, cave-wall glyphs and pictographs.

**Materials and textures.** Surfaces read heavy, chipped and handmade: conchoidal flint scars, spalled stone edges, cracked mudbrick, hide grain and fur clumps, reed crosshatch, rope twist, bone polished at the grip but raw at the marrow end, wood split rather than milled. Nothing is smooth, cast, machined or glossy. Wear, soot and dust sit on everything.

**Composition and lighting tendencies.** Silhouette-first shapes — tusk curves, rib arches, menhir profiles — carry the read at distance. Framing devices recur: a cave mouth or a bone arch acting as a proscenium around a vista; horizontal processional friezes for battles, migrations and crowds; full-body character stands that inventory a figure's props and armour; isolated artifact studies on neutral ground for tools and weapons. Lighting ranges from hazy diffuse daylight thick with dust, to warm low sun raking rock faces orange, to cool teal dusk for machine-beast encounters, to firelight chiaroscuro in cave interiors, to flat even light for artifact studies. Rendering is most often painterly digital concept art with soft atmospheric depth; stylized cel/anime treatment, editorial costume photography and clean product-style renders all occur, and the flat ochre-and-charcoal graphic of Paleolithic cave painting persists as an ornamental lineage.

## Mood & Values

The emotional core is ingenuity under scarcity — "high concept, low materials." Stonepunk celebrates cleverness without industrialization: the lever, the lashing, the trained beast, the carved peg. It holds awe for the natural world and its megafauna, treating animals as colleagues and machines at once. Myth, ritual and survival sit close to the surface; in its serious and post-apocalyptic mode the style trades humour for reverence and dread at rediscovered technology. Even at its funniest, the handmade joint is the punchline and the point: human brains, stone-age hands.

## Era & Origins

The term emerged in the 2000s, credited to Frank Wu, as a branch of the retro-futurist "punk" family that pushes steampunk's anachronism back past brass and steam to the Neolithic. Its comic lineage runs through mid-century domestic parody of prehistoric life; its serious lineage through prehistoric fiction and, more recently, through games and animation that pair tribal hunters with machine beasts or rebuilt worlds. Its deepest visual ancestor is Paleolithic cave painting itself — ochre beasts outlined in charcoal on pale rock — which supplies the style's palette DNA and its instinct for bold silhouette line.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Any modern function may appear, but only executed in stone, bone, antler, wood, hide, fur, reed or mudbrick, joined by lashings, wedges and pegs.
2. Keep surfaces chipped, worn and handmade; weight over precision.
3. Hold the palette rule: earth and bone dominate, red signals, charcoal lines, earthy blue cools the sky only.
4. Let animals be motors, cranes, computers and conveyances; harness them visibly.
5. Dress people as tribal engineers — fur, war paint, bone jewellery — optionally grafted with bone goggles, stone plates, scrap prosthetics.
6. Build architecture from bone and rock: skull gates, rib arches, menhirs, cliff-carved megastructures.
7. Choose a register deliberately: parody, mythic awe, or survival dread — but keep the material grammar identical in all three.

**Variation axes.** Subject may run from a single knapped blade on neutral ground, to a costume study, to a vehicle or machine, to a dwelling or settlement, to a landscape megastructure, to a battle frieze, ritual procession, domestic parody scene or machine-beast encounter. Composition may be object study, character stand, proscenium-framed vista, horizontal frieze or low-angle awe shot. Lighting may be hazy noon, raking orange sun, teal dusk, firelight or neutral studio. Color handling may be dust-desaturated epic, saturated editorial, or flat graphic pictograph. Mood may be comic, reverent or fearful. Medium may be painterly concept art, stylized cel, photography or product render. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.**
- Smelted metal of any kind: steel blades, brass gears, iron nails, rivets, chainmail, plate armour, chrome, copper wire; bronze or iron-age warbands break the Neolithic premise.
- Machined perfection: smooth cast surfaces, moulded plastic, rubber tyres, glass screens, LEDs, neon, holograms, seamless white sci-fi interiors.
- Steam engines, boilers, smokestacks, clockwork gear trains — those belong to steampunk.
- Classical or medieval building: cut marble columns, Gothic arches, tiled roofs (mudbrick and stacked stone are fine).
- Firearms and modern branding, logos or typography.
- Dense green forest canopy, moss-everything, living-tree architecture as the dominant environment.
- Pure documentary prehistoric realism with no anachronistic function — without machine logic it is period art, not stonepunk.

## Discrimination Cues

The wiki names Forestpunk as stonepunk's closest relative without defining it, so the contrast below is drawn from material logic and palette rather than a documented checklist; treat it as working guidance. Both styles share tribal dress, natural materials and pre-industrial craft, and the overlap zone is exactly a woodland tribe in hide and bone. Break the tie on matter and environment: Forestpunk centres the *living* forest — green-canopy palette, moss, bark, walkways and dwellings grown into or suspended from standing trees, technology in symbiosis with woodland. Stonepunk centres *dead mineral and faunal* matter — knapped flint, rough stone, bone, antler, hide — set in open, dramatic rock landscapes under wide skies: steppe, canyon, badlands, desert. Stonepunk's tell-tale extras are bone megastructure (skull gates, rib arches, menhir skylines), megafauna harnessed as machines, anachronistic machine logic played for parody or awe, and the ochre-and-charcoal pictograph lineage. If the canvas is green-dominant and the structures are alive, it is Forestpunk; if it is earth-, bone- and red-dominant with lashed stone and bone doing a machine's job, it is stonepunk.
