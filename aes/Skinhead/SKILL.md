# Skinhead

Skinhead is a dress-code-first subculture: before it is a music scene or a political headline, it is a uniform of working-class smartness — cropped hair, polished boots, tucked shirt, rolled hem — assembled from affordable British workwear and sportswear brands (Fred Perry, Ben Sherman, Lonsdale, Everlast, Dr. Martens, Levi's). It emerged from London's hard-mod and Jamaican rude-boy overlap in the late 1960s, absorbed ska, reggae and rocksteady, then re-fused with punk through Oi! and 2 Tone in the late 1970s, and spread internationally (Brazil, Germany, the US, post-Wall Eastern Europe) in fractured political variants. The aesthetic's core tension is between neatness and threat: pressed shirts and mirror-shined leather worn with squared shoulders and a confrontational chin. Any reading of the style that reduces it to far-right iconography is incomplete — the wiki record stresses apolitical and left-wing wings (SHARP, redskins), multicultural ska roots, and female and queer participants — but the political shadow is part of its history and must be handled deliberately, never defaulted into.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The palette rule is **solid, unsaturated working-class colour with at most one check accent**. Base solids: near-black and charcoal (#101010–#262626), oxblood/burgundy (#4A1010–#6E1A1A), navy (#141C2E–#23304A), shirt white and off-white (#EDEDE6–#F5F4EE), and mid grey (#7E7E7E–#9A9A9A). Pattern accents come from tartan and check: red-black tartan (red around #8E1B1B crossed with black and bottle-green lines), houndstooth and Prince of Wales in grey-black, and gingham-scale checks on shirts. Secondary cloth colours are denim indigo (#2C3A55) and its bleach-splattered pale (#A9B6C9). Shine is carried by material, not hue: satin bomber nylon in black or oxblood, and two-tone tonic mohair suiting that shifts grey-green-blue with the angle of light. There are no pastels, no neons, no prints other than checks and tartan; a specular highlight on leather or satin is the only "bright" note the palette allows.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Shaved or closely cropped head** — the first and non-negotiable signifier, for all genders. The female variant in the visual record is a cropped cut with a short, blunt fringe.
2. **Dr. Martens lace-up boots** — polished smooth leather, black or cherry oxblood, tall shafts kept visible by rolled trouser hems or by skirts; the footwear must read as heavy, rounded, utilitarian leather.
3. **Braces (suspenders)** — narrow webbing or leather straps in solid red, black or white, worn over shirt or polo; in documentary mode they may cross a bare chest.
4. **Button-down and polo shirts** — Ben Sherman button-downs, Fred Perry polos (laurel wreath at the chest), tucked in, collar crisp; also grandfather shirts and plain or band-text T-shirts.
5. **Straight-leg trousers** — Levi's 501 or Sta-Prest, straight cut, hem rolled into a deep turn-up above the boot shaft; no skinny, no baggy.
6. **Harrington and bomber jackets** — cropped, zip-front; the bomber in shiny satin nylon (black, oxblood), the Harrington in matte cotton twill.
7. **Checks, tartan and tonic suits** — fitted blazers and suits in houndstooth, Prince of Wales or shiny two-tone tonic; tartan as skirt, shirt or trim.
8. **Brand and crew text** — Lonsdale, Everlast, Warrior, Brutus, Jaytex logos; sweatshirts and tees carrying subculture or band text; visible brand labels function as badges of belonging.
9. **Knitwear layers** — V-neck sweaters, sleeveless tank tops and cardigans worn over shirts.
10. **Secondary outerwear** — denim jackets (sometimes bleach-splattered), donkey jackets, Crombie overcoats, sheepskin three-quarter coats, monkey jackets, short macs, parkas.
11. **Setting objects** — football terraces, pub and club interiors, brick walls with graffiti, ska/reggae/Oi! record culture, and print ephemera: photocopied zine pages and genealogy-style "family tree" diagrams of the subculture's factions, line-drawn figures with typewriter captions on off-white paper.
12. **Tattoos** — forearm sleeves and chest pieces appear repeatedly in the visual record but are not documented in the text; treat as period-plausible optional texture, never as a required motif.

### Materials and textures

Polished smooth leather (boots) against shiny nylon satin (bombers) against matte wool knits and cotton piqué against raw or bleached denim against mohair-tonic sheen. The style's texture rule is **at least one hard, glossy element grounded by matte cloth**: shine comes from boot polish, satin weave or tonic mohair, never from sequins, vinyl gloss or metallic paint. Brass zip pulls and simple metal buckles are the only hardware.

### Recurring subjects

Individual skins posed square-on; crews and groups clustered shoulder-to-shoulder; skinhead girls in tartan or check mini-skirts, bare legs or short socks, boots and cropped fringes; couples and affectionate pairings, including queer subjects documented in the record; dancers at ska, reggae or Oi! nights; terrace and street crowds; and, in ephemera mode, diagrammatic full-body figures cataloguing outfit archetypes. Ages run from teens to thirties. Posture vocabulary: chin up, shoulders squared, hands in pockets, thumbs hooking braces, arms folded, leaning back against a wall.

### Composition, lighting and medium

Framing tends to keep the **whole outfit grammar legible**: full-body or three-quarter views that include boot shafts and rolled hems, because the hem-and-boot junction is a primary signifier. Group shots overlap bodies into a single mass rather than spacing subjects evenly. Lighting in the surviving record is honest and unglamorous: direct on-camera flash in cramped interiors with hot highlights and fast shadow falloff; flat overcast daylight outdoors; or high-contrast, grainy black-and-white documentary treatment. A neutral plain-backdrop studio portrait (lookbook mode) is equally acceptable, as is the print/ephemera mode: line illustration, typewriter type, genealogical connecting lines, off-white paper. Medium feel is 35mm film or compact-camera snapshot with visible grain; avoid softbox beauty lighting and golden-hour glamour. Note honestly: the text specifies garments, not photography — lighting and medium cues are distilled from the visual record, not from written doctrine.

## Mood & Values

Hard-but-smart. The style expresses working-class pride through discipline: boots polished, shirts pressed, hems rolled to a precise depth — neatness as self-respect and as a rebuke to both middle-class hippie dishevelment and mod peacockery. It values crew loyalty and territorial belonging (terraces, streets, local pubs), music-first joy (skanking to reggae bass, pogoing to Oi!), and a confrontational readiness to defend that belonging. The record also preserves tenderness inside the toughness — embraced couples, quiet domestic interiors — and the style's multicultural, anti-racist and queer strands are as authentic as its notorious far-right segment. A faithful image carries class confidence and contained aggression without needing violence; the politics, when present at all, should be a deliberate curatorial choice, not ambient decoration.

## Era & Origins

Late 1960s London, from hard mods pushed out of the mod scene and Jamaican rude boys' sharp dressing; first wave peak around 1969 with ska, reggae and rocksteady on the turntable. 1970s: suedehead smartening and the 2 Tone crossover that fused ska with punk. Late 1970s–1980s: Oi! second wave, football-terrace entanglement, and the political splintering that produced far-right factions (and their redskin/SHARP opponents). 1980s–90s: international spread — Brazilian carecas, German and US scenes, US hardcore-punk crossover, post-1989 Eastern Europe — plus periodic revivals. The aesthetic reacts against hippie counterculture long hair and middle-class softness, and against mod elitism, by turning workwear and sportswear into a uniform of class defiance.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Hair and boots carry the read: a cropped or shaved head plus polished lace-up leather boots. If one is hidden, compensate with the braces + tucked polo + rolled-hem cluster.
- Keep the silhouette fitted and tidy: shirts tucked, hems rolled, jackets cropped. Drape and oversize break the style.
- Obey the palette rule: solid black/oxblood/navy/white/grey base, at most one tartan or check accent, shine only from leather, satin or tonic.
- Make the outfit grammar visible: vertical brace lines, crisp collar, turn-up depth, boot shaft, brand badge at the chest.
- Light honestly: direct flash snapshot, flat overcast, grainy high-contrast monochrome, or neutral studio flash. No glamour lighting.
- Set the scene in vernacular space — brick, graffiti, terrace, pub, club floor, street, cramped interior — or in plain-backdrop lookbook mode, or in zine/diagram print mode.
- Pick a wave and stay coherent: 1969 traditional (tonic suit, Crombie, reggae), 1979 Oi!/2 Tone-adjacent (bleach denim, band tees), 1980s–90s revival (harder punk inflection).
- Prefer group mass and squared-on posture; the style is collective before it is individual.

**Variation axes.** Subject: solo portrait, crew cluster, skinhead girls, queer or affectionate pairings, dancers, terrace crowds, diagram figures. Composition: candid snapshot, posed full-body lookbook, wall-leaning street portrait, genealogical print layout. Lighting: on-camera flash, overcast flat, high-contrast black-and-white, studio flash. Colour handling: saturated flash colour, muted film colour, or monochrome. Mood: defiant, celebratory, tender, documentary-neutral. Geography and era: UK core plus Brazilian, German, American and Eastern European variants across four decades. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a tender studio portrait, a terrace crowd and a photocopied family-tree page are equally valid outputs despite sharing no composition.

**Avoid**

- Long hair of any kind, mohawks, liberty spikes, feathered glam cuts (punk/metal signifiers); a cropped fringe on women is fine, flowing hair is not.
- Baggy or oversized streetwear, skater or hip-hop silhouettes, hoodies as the hero garment, skinny ripped jeans.
- Trainers as hero footwear and designer terrace sportswear (Stone Island, C.P. Company, Adidas tracksuits) — that is the Casuals uniform.
- Neon, pastels, glowsticks, rave sportswear and club-laser lighting (Bakala).
- Black-and-white checkerboard as the dominant pattern, pork-pie hats with thin ties as a uniform (2 Tone / rude-boy revival cues).
- Scooter-plus-parka-plus-coiffed-crop combinations (Mod); a parka alone is acceptable only when boots, braces or cropped hair anchor the read.
- Hate symbols — swastikas, fascist gestures, RAC or white-power logos. They belong to a documented political segment, not to the core aesthetic; default to excluding them, because their presence recasts the image as political documentation rather than subcultural style, and never erase the female, queer and anti-racist presence the record confirms.
- Haute-couture drape, luxury monograms, editorial softbox gloss, golden-hour romance.
- Grunge flannel, cowboy or American workwear cues, military camouflage as fashion (undocumented in the text; keep absent or marginal).

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared ground | What separates Skinhead |
|---|---|---|
| **2 Tone** | Ska/reggae roots, cropped hair, suits | 2 Tone owns the black-white checkerboard, pork-pie hats and thin ties; skinhead owns tartan, braces, polos and heavy boots |
| **Mod** | British working-class smartness, parkas, sharp tailoring | Mod keeps coiffed short hair, scooters, three-button suits and target tees; skinhead shaves the head and swaps scooter shoes for polished boots |
| **Rude Boy** | Jamaican sharpness, ska dance culture | Rude boy wears trilby/pork-pie, thin tie, sunglasses and lightweight suits; skinhead is British terrace wear — braces, denim, leather boots, no hat uniform |
| **Bakala** | 1990s crew energy, buzzed crops in some cases | Bakala is Valencia rave: neon sportswear, glow colour, club-laser light; no boots, braces, tartan or analog flash realism |
| **Casuals** | Football terraces, working-class masculinity | Casuals wear designer sportswear and trainers with styled short hair; skinhead wears plain heritage brands, leather boots and cropped heads |

Against punk generally: shared Oi! territory and occasional bleed, but punk's mohawks, ripped skinny denim and safety-pin hardware are foreign to the skinhead grammar of neat, solid, polished workwear.

## Evidence Notes

The written record fully documents garments, brands, palette and subgenre history; it is silent on photography, lighting and female dress, which the visual record supplies (flash snapshots, monochrome documentary frames, a lookbook-style studio portrait, skirt-and-boots grammar for women, and a diagrammatic family-tree print). Tattoos appear only in images and are ranked accordingly low. Where the record is thin — e.g. interior domestic scenes, dance-floor movement — generation should extrapolate from the stated principles rather than invent new motifs.
