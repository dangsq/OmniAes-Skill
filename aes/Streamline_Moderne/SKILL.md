# Streamline Moderne

Streamline Moderne is Art Deco put on a diet and set in motion. Born in the United States in the early 1930s as a direct answer to the Wall Street crash and the Great Depression, it traded Deco's lavish ornament and expensive materials for smooth, cheap, machine-made surfaces — and borrowed its shapes from wind tunnels instead of ziggurats. Teardrops, bullets and cylinders: the forms that reduce drag on locomotives, aircraft and ocean liners were applied to bus terminals, diners, factories, refrigerators, toasters, radios and chairs, so that even a stationary object looks like it is doing ninety miles an hour. The 1933–34 Chicago World's Fair launched the style into public consciousness; its prophets were industrial designers like Norman Bel Geddes, Raymond Loewy and Henry Dreyfuss; its French nickname, *Style Paquebot*, names the source outright — the ocean liner. It flourished through the 1940s and faded after WWII into the Atomic Age vocabularies of Raygun Gothic and Mid-Century Modern.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule: the body of a thing stays light, neutral or metallic; colour arrives as a band, a rail, a dial, a seat, a sign — never as overall decoration. Dominant fields are white and pale earth tones because smooth plaster and painted steel read as clean, modern and inexpensive. Metals form the second voice. Accents are thin and strategic, or — in posters and painted renderings — bold flat fields behind a neutral object.

- **Field neutrals:** ivory to cream (#F7F3E8 → #EFE7D4), sand to taupe (#D8C8A6 → #B9A98F), pale warm gray (#C9C4BA).
- **Metals and lacquers:** polished chrome (#D6DADD), stainless steel (#A7ADB3), gunmetal (#6B7178), gloss black lacquer (#121417) as the dark variant for products and car bodies.
- **Accents (bands, trim, upholstery, signage, poster fields):** machine blue (#1E6EB5), mint / pastel green (#A9D3A5), butter yellow (#E9C65B), oxblood / maroon banding (#6F2B28), chartreuse (#B9C94E), leather red (#A63A2E); neon turquoise (#46D8C8) and neon red (#E8443A) as glowing sign light.
- **Legitimate monochrome subset:** period documentary photography in black and white — gray scales with silver highlights on chrome and wet asphalt.

Two-tone horizontal banding (a dark base band under a light upper wall, or the reverse) is a documented wall treatment; so is an ombre gradient on lacquered casegoods. Expect clear saturated skies — cobalt or pale turquoise — behind white buildings.

### Signature motifs, ranked as the text documents them

1. **Aerodynamic curvature.** Rounded corners everywhere: teardrop, bullet and cylinder volumes; the curved corner of a building wrapping a street intersection is the style's hero shot.
2. **Horizontal "speed lines."** Long parallel grooves, ribs, louvers and painted bands that wrap around curved forms and pull the eye sideways.
3. **Smooth, unadorned surfaces.** No moldings, no relief, no applied pattern; continuity of skin is the ornament.
4. **Chrome and stainless accents.** Bumpers, grilles, railings, trim bands, plated hardware — metal as the only jewelry.
5. **Glass block / glass brick.** Translucent gridded panels and walls that glow from within at night.
6. **Porthole windows** and other round openings.
7. **Nautical liner cues.** Flat roofs with pipe railings, deck-like terraces, bridge-style control towers, flagpoles.
8. **Transportation iconography.** Bullet-nose locomotives, teardrop-fendered cars, liners, aircraft noses — the sources of the vocabulary, often pictured directly.
9. **Domestic machines.** Radios, toasters, refrigerators, vacuum cleaners, clocks, cigarette lighters, club chairs, desks: streamlining at hand size, with horizontal ribs and rounded shells.
10. **Signage and graphics.** Vertical pylon or tower signs, neon tubing, and diagonal italic "speed lettering" on travel posters and title cards.

### Materials and textures

Smooth stucco and plaster; painted steel and duco lacquer (high gloss); chromium-plated steel; stainless steel and aluminum; glass block; Bakelite and early plastics; lacquered wood (walnut duco) with varnished brass in interiors; tubular steel frames; leather and vinyl upholstery; parquet and linoleum underfoot. The texture rule: surfaces are continuous — matte-smooth or glossy — and the only permitted "texture" is machined repetition: ribs, louvers, fluting, the grid of glass block, the slots of a grille. No hand-worked trace, no grain celebrated, no patina.

### Recurring subjects

Architecture of movement: bus and train terminals, airport terminals, roadside cafes and diners, port buildings, factories, cinemas and clubs, maritime museums. Residences exist but are rarer (the text is explicit on this): curved-corner houses and row-house variants rather than grand villas. Products: the household machine list above. Graphics: travel posters, world's-fair promotional art, film title cards. Interiors: curved reception desks and benches, glass-block partitions, lobby spaces with horizontal ribbing. Period-adjacent painted film matte work and airbrushed promotional renderings extend the vocabulary into imagined cityscapes of clustered cylinders — a cinematic interpretation of the same rules.

### Composition and lighting

Horizontality first: wide formats, low horizons, elongated massing stretched across the frame. Buildings are typically shown in three-quarter view so the curved corner wraps, or straight-on from street level slightly below eye line against a clear sky. Products sit centered and isolated on a seamless neutral ground with a soft falling shadow. Posters use diagonal thrust and italic type leaning into motion. Lighting ranges widely: harsh clear daylight with crisp short shadows on white stucco; soft diffuse studio light for product shots; dusk and night scenes where glass block, neon and interior light glow; airbrushed rendering and matte-painting modes with pastel gradient skies; and straight-on black-and-white documentary light.

## Mood & Values

Optimism under austerity. The style expresses speed, efficiency, modernity, technology, motion and functionality — progress made affordable. Because it was born of cost-cutting, its sleekness is democratic rather than luxurious: cheap materials made to look inevitable, not rich. There is a sober hopefulness here, a "world of tomorrow" promise addressed to ordinary people at bus stations and diner counters. Objects at rest imply velocity; cleanliness implies morality; the machine is a friend.

## Era & Origins

United States, early 1930s, as a reaction to the 1929 crash and the Depression, and simultaneously an evolution away from Art Deco's opulent, vertical, ornate character. Its formal source is aerodynamic engineering from automobiles, locomotives and aircraft; its spirit owes something to Futurism's dynamic lines and bright colors. The 1933–34 Chicago World's Fair was the defining public launch. The style ran through the 1940s, declined after WWII, and handed off to the post-war boom aesthetics of Raygun Gothic, Space Age and Mid-Century Modern. In France it travelled as *Style Paquebot*. Note that "streamlining" as a design doctrine stands apart from European Functionalism of the same decade: same machine age, different school — Streamline Moderne dresses objects in the *shape* of speed even when they never move.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Begin with a smooth volume, then round every corner; a sharp vertical edge on a primary form is a warning sign.
2. Push horizontality in massing, banding, grooves and framing — the eye should travel sideways.
3. Keep surfaces unadorned; the only ornament allowed is repetition (ribs, louvers, fluting, glass-block grid) or none.
4. Let metal be the jewelry: chrome trim lines, railings, grilles, plated hardware.
5. Discipline the colour: neutral or metallic field, accent as band, seam, seat or glow.
6. Imply motion at rest: bullet noses, wrapped curves, trailing speed lines.
7. Hold period honesty: 1930s–40s materials, graphics and type; nothing atomic, nothing post-war.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be any building type within the style's range, any household machine, a vehicle, an interior, a poster, or an imagined cityscape in the painted-rendering mode. Composition may be frontal, three-quarter around a curved corner, low-angle against sky, centered product isolation, or poster diagonal. Lighting may be clear daylight, soft studio, dusk with neon and glass-block glow, painted diffuse light, or black-and-white documentary. Colour handling may be all-neutral, neutral plus one accent band, saturated flat accent fields behind a neutral object (poster mode), or full monochrome. Medium may be photograph, linen postcard, airbrushed rendering, matte painting or lithograph poster. Mood may run from promotional optimism to documentary sobriety. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.** A white seaside villa, a pencil sharpener, a ferry terminal or a fictional radio set can all be perfectly Streamline Moderne without resembling any single archived example.

**Avoid.**
- Art Deco ornament: zigzags, chevrons, sunbursts, stepped ziggurat setbacks, gold leaf, figural relief, exotic veneers, marble lavishness.
- Vertical emphasis: skyscraper setback massing, tall narrow proportions as the hero gesture.
- Roughness and decay: exposed brick, rubble stone, trowel-marked plaster, rust, soot, grime, weathering, ruin.
- Brass-and-gear retro-futurism or ornate alt-history luxury (Decopunk territory).
- Post-1945 Atomic Age cues: rocket fins, tailfin excess, boomerang and atomic-burst patterns, sputnik stars, googie swooshes.
- Mid-Century Modern warmth: exposed wood structure, tapered dowel legs, free-form organic plywood, indoor-outdoor glass walls as the main statement.
- Handcrafted rusticity: wrought iron, carved wood, folk pattern, floral or plaid textiles — the style is anti-pattern.
- Contemporary anachronisms: frameless curtain-wall glazing, LED strips, seamless matte plastic without period gloss.
- Dystopian narrative: war damage, propaganda grit, abandonment.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | The tell that separates it from Streamline Moderne |
|---|---|
| **Art Deco** | Vertical, symmetrical, ornamental: chevrons, sunbursts, relief sculpture, stepped setbacks, lavish materials (marble, gold, lacquer, exotic woods). Streamline is horizontal, smooth, unadorned, curved, and built from cheap industrial materials. |
| **Decopunk** | Keeps and amplifies Deco's ornament into fantasy — brass, gold glow, alt-history luxury. Streamline strips ornament away and substitutes chrome and steel with a functionalist alibi. |
| **Dieselpunk** | Same machine era, opposite hygiene: rivets, soot, oil stains, weathered steel, wartime grit, dystopia. Streamline skins are seamless, rivet-free, polished and optimistic. |
| **Factory Pomo** | A postmodern (1980s+) quotation of industrial/streamline vocabulary: ironic oversized signage, arbitrary color blocking, mirror and tinted glass, playful historicism. Streamline Moderne is period-authentic 1930s–40s with sincere functionalism; the separation is era and irony more than form (documentary evidence for this neighbor is thin, so judge by date-feel and wink). |
| **Mid-Century Modern** | Post-war 1950s–60s: honest exposed wood, tapered legs, organic plywood curves, atomic and boomerang graphics, casual asymmetry, indoor-outdoor glass. Streamline is pre-war, machine-literal, stucco-and-chrome, banded and horizontal. |
| **Raygun Gothic** | The immediate successor: finned rockets, atomic dials, space-age plastic after 1945. If it points at the moon instead of the horizon, it is not Streamline Moderne. |

**Quick recognition check:** are all primary corners rounded? do horizontals dominate massing and detail? is the skin smooth and unadorned, dressed only with chrome, ribs or glass block? If yes to all three, you are looking at Streamline Moderne.
