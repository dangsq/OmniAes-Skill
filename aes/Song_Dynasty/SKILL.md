# Song Dynasty (Song China, 960–1279)

The Song aesthetic is the visual culture of a dynasty that deliberately chose the brush over the sword. Founded in 960 by Zhao Kuangyin on the policy of "valuing culture over martial power," it elevated scholar-officials to the backbone of society, and their taste — shaped by Neo-Confucian investigation of things, Chan Buddhist directness, and Daoist naturalness — became the dominant fashion of the age. The result is an aesthetic of restraint: clean, quiet, elegant. Painting favours water-ink over pigment; ceramics favour monochrome glazes over decoration; dress favours vertical lines and understated layers over ornament; a flower arrangement favours three branches over a bouquet. Even luxury is expressed as subtlety — a crackle in a glaze, a pearl on the cheek, a mist band left as bare silk. Northern Song (960–1127) gives the style its monumental fullness: mountains filling the scroll, dense forests, detailed rocks. Southern Song (1127–1279), after the court fled to Hangzhou, gives it its elegiac emptiness: large reserved whites of mist and water, content pushed to a corner, figures grown larger and more poignant.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The palette is a low-saturation field built on aged grounds, with ink as the structural spine and glaze colours as the signature family. Vivid colour exists but is always rationed.

| Role | Range | Notes |

| Aged silk ground | `#B9A67C` – `#D9CBA6` | warm tan-ochre of centuries-old silk |
| Paper / Ding white | `#E6DFC8` – `#EFE8D8` | ivory, thin-bodied, translucent at the rim |
| Ink spine | `#1E1E1C` – `#8C8B80` | from dense charcoal to pale wash; never pure black |

| Subdued mineral accents | malachite `#4C7A57`, azurite `#3C6A8C`, ochre `#A5793F` | court gongbi mode only, kept muted by aged ground |

Rule of thumb: value contrast soft, saturation low, surface matte. The only permitted exceptions are Jun kiln purple-red splashes and the bright rich colours of academic court painting, and even those sit on an aged, muted ground.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Monumental landscape** — the style's first subject. High mountains, deep valleys, streams, waterfalls, mist bands, riverbanks, lakes, snowscapes, rainy scenes; pines, willows, bamboo, reeds, rocks; sunrises and sunsets. Pavilions, boats, fishermen, scholars, woodcutters and herders appear but stay secondary to nature.
2. **Ru kiln and the tianqing glaze** — the dream-colour ordered by emperor Zhao Ji; matte jade-like surface on incense-ash grey bodies, veined by crab-claw crackles. With it the whole five-kiln family: Guan ice-crackle celadon, Ge "golden and iron wire" crackle, Jun transmutation, Ding incised white.
3. **Bird-and-flower** — blossoming branches of plum, orchid, bamboo, chrysanthemum, lotus, peony, hibiscus, peach; loquat and pomegranate fruit; golden pheasants, mynas, orioles, sparrows, mandarin ducks, egrets, kingfishers, butterflies, bees, dragonflies, fish, shrimp, crabs. Settings stay minimal: Taihu rocks, railings, lattice windows, flower stands.
4. **The moon** — a key motif in its own right, usually implied by light and emptiness rather than painted bright.
5. **Scholarly life objects** — qin zither, inkstone, brushes, scrolls, books, wine vessels, tea cups; painted screens and displayed porcelain in interiors; the black-glazed Jian bowl and bamboo whisk of diancha whisked tea; a hanging scroll on a plain wall (gua hua); a three-branch flower arrangement (cha hua) in a simple vase, bamboo tube or stone basin.
6. **Dress** — women: inner song-mo breast wrap, duijin-shan jacket with parallel collars and side slits, high-waisted wrap pleated skirts, hexiu and beixin casual jackets, formal beizi coat and changyi robe; noblewomen's faces dotted with tiny pearls at cheeks, forehead and temples. Men: the zhanjiao futou cap with two rigid wing-like horizontal flaps; flowers worn in hair or hat.
7. **Architecture and antiquarianism** — wooden halls with deep sweeping eaves, caisson ceilings, lattice doors and windows, railings; Southern Song hall-style frames neat and simple. A strong antiquarian current revived ancient bronze ritual-vessel shapes (zun, gu, tripods) in ceramic and metal.
8. **Calligraphy** — the Slender Gold script of Zhao Ji, handscroll colophons, collector seals as small vermilion punctuation.

### Materials and textures

Silk weave and paper fibre visibly carry the image. Ink arrives in gradations, with rock and bark texture built by cun-fa brush techniques and architecture by the ruled precision of jiehua. Ceramic surfaces are a texture catalogue: matte jade glaze (Ru), glossy glass-like crackle (Guan), oily sheen with stained large-and-small crackle networks (Ge), thick glaze flow leaving "earthworm track" lines (Jun), thin translucent bodies with bare firing rims and incised florals (Ding); purple rims and iron-black feet recur across Guan and Ge. Black lacquer and hare's-fur glaze on tea ware; wood grain on beams and furniture; bronze patina on antiquarian vessels. Age reads as tonal mellowing and fine crackle, never as grime or damage.

### Composition and lighting

Four compositional families cover the style: the vertical hanging scroll with a central massif filling top to bottom and a path, bridge or tree leading the eye (Northern Song); the diagonal one-corner arrangement with large reserved whites of mist, water or sky and bigger, more important figures (Southern Song); the horizontal handscroll unfolding narrative space — market streets, rivers, processions — segment by segment; and the intimate circle or square of round fans and album leaves. Still-life and object images sit centred on a plain, empty ground. Figure scenes favour courtyards, studies and market streets with tables, screens and displayed wares. Lighting is always diffuse: overcast softness, mist as luminance, moonlight, or even interior light through lattice paper. No cast shadows, no chiaroscuro, no single directional source. Emptiness is composed, not leftover.

## Mood & Values

Clean, quiet, elegant. The mood is contemplative self-cultivation: Neo-Confucian "investigating things to fathom principle" gives the style its meticulous observation of petals, feathers and rock faces; Chan Buddhism's "direct insight into one's original mind" licenses the freehand xieyi shortcut and the ink-only flower school; Daoism supplies naturalness, subtlety and understated elegance. Restraint is moral: simplicity signals refinement, and antiquarian bronze studies signal reverence for ideal governance. In Southern Song work a subdued elegy underlies the calm — mist standing in for a lost northern homeland. Conviviality exists (scholars gathered around a table, tea being whisked, a qin played for friends) but it is always low-volume.

## Era & Origins

Emerges in 10th-century China with the dynasty's founding in 960, as a deliberate reaction to the Tang's collapse into warlordism: culture over martial power, scholar-officials sharing state power. Mid-Northern Song sees literati painting rise as Su Shi and peers recast painting as poetic self-expression. Late Northern Song, under the artist-emperor Zhao Ji (r. 1100–1125), is the court zenith: the Imperial Academy of Painting, the Xuanhe catalogues, Slender Gold calligraphy, and the tianqing glaze ordered from a dream. After the Jingkang Catastrophe of 1127 the court moves south to Hangzhou; the aesthetic shifts toward reserved space, corner compositions, academic court painting, and woodblock-printed taste spreading beyond the elite. Modern revival arrives with the Hanfu movement of the late 2010s–2020s, which reproduces Song garments and re-popularises the style.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. Ground first, then air: choose an aged silk tan or paper ivory ground and reserve emptiness (mist, water, sky, bare wall) as a designed shape before placing anything.
2. Ink defines, colour seasons: structure the image with brush and ink gradation; let pigment enter as accent, never as foundation (court gongbi mode excepted, and even then muted by the ground).
3. Matte over glossy: surfaces should feel jade-like, oily-soft, or lacquered-dull; a single subtle glaze sheen is enough.
4. Ration objects: a plain wall, one hanging scroll, three branches in a vase, a bowl and a whisk. Restraint count is a style feature.
5. Keep the scale hierarchy: nature monumental and humans tiny (Northern mode), or humans moderate and placed at corners against void (Southern mode).
6. Age the material honestly: fine silk weave, paper fibre, glaze crackle, mellowed tone — patina, not dirt.
7. Light diffusely: overcast, mist-luminance, moonlight, lattice-filtered interior. No cast shadows.
8. Draw with the brush: calligraphic line, cun-fa texture strokes, jiehua ruling for architecture; smooth gongbi precision only when painting in court mode.

**Variation axes**

- *Subject*: landscape, bird-and-flower, scholar figures and study interiors, market streets, tea ceremony, flower arranging, ceramic or bronze still-life, costume portrait, architecture, calligraphy handscroll.
- *Composition*: monumental vertical, one-corner diagonal, horizontal handscroll, round fan, album leaf, centred still-life on plain ground.
- *Colour handling*: pure monochrome ink → ink with faint tint → muted mineral court colour → glaze-colour still-life (tianqing, celadon, Ding white) → Jun rose-purple splash as sole high note.
- *Lighting*: overcast diffuse, mist as light source, moonlight, soft interior light.
- *Mood*: serene, austere, elegiac, quietly convivial, contemplative.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A steel-grey snowscape with a single boat, a cream-glazed bowl on an empty table, or a robed scholar holding a book against blank silk are all equally Song, though none copies a surviving work.

**Avoid**

- Saturated candy or neon colour; vermilion or gold used as field colours rather than accents.
- Gilded grounds, gold-leaf halos, heavy gilding of any kind.
- Horror vacui ornament, busy patterned textiles, crowded decorative registers.
- Hard directional light, cast shadows, chiaroscuro, dramatic spotlighting.
- Glossy plastic or render sheen; crisp digital sharpness that erases silk, paper and glaze materiality.
- Dunhuang Buddhist mural vocabulary: flying apsaras, halos, narrative wall registers (see below).
- Tang dynasty markers: plump figure canon, sancai drip-glazes, cosmopolitan opulence.
- Qing dynasty markers: dense enamel florals, European perspective, porcelain crowded with pattern.
- Anachronisms: modern furniture, glass, electric light, contemporary styling in costume shots.
- Confusing plum blossom with cherry-blossom festivity: Song plum is a gnarled ink branch with sparse round petals, an emblem of endurance, not a pink cloud.

## Discrimination Cues

**Against Dunhuang Feng (closest neighbour).** Both styles use mineral pigment on aged grounds, so decide by four tests. *Surface*: Dunhuang is pigment on dry cave plaster — flaking, soot-darkened, wall-bound; Song is ink and light pigment on silk or paper, or glaze on porcelain. *Palette*: Dunhuang runs saturated azurite blue, malachite green, cinnabar red and lead white with gold; Song runs ink gradations over tan silk with glaze cyans, saturation rationed. *Subject*: Dunhuang is Buddhist — Buddhas and bodhisattvas with halos, flying apsaras with streaming ribbons, jataka narrative bands, caisson ceiling patterns; Song is secular and scholarly — mountains, mist, plum branches, pheasants, tea bowls, scholars in wing-flapped caps. *Space and line*: Dunhuang fills the wall edge to edge with flat colour fills inside iron-wire contours; Song composes emptiness as deliberately as form and builds volume with calligraphic brushwork, ink wash gradation and cun-fa texture. If the image has a halo, an apsara ribbon or a narrative register, it is Dunhuang; if it has a mist band, a crackled glaze or a tiny fisherman dwarfed by a massif, it is Song.

**Secondary neighbours.** Tang aesthetics differ by body and glaze: fuller figures, splashed sancai colour,外向 cosmopolitan splendour versus Song's slender silhouettes and monochrome restraint. Qing court taste differs by density: enamel-packed porcelain and busy ornament versus Song's plain walls and single objects. Japanese Zen-derived minimalism shares Song's emptiness (it descends from it) but tends to strip out the Chinese scholarly apparatus — seals, colophons, lattice interiors, hanfu layers — that keeps an image anchored in Song rather than in its downstream echo.

**Evidence note.** The documentary record here is strongest for painting, ceramics, dress and the tea/flower/hanging-picture arts; music and theatre are thinly documented (a single musical), and architectural evidence in surviving samples leans on reconstructions, so architectural images should stay generic to the described features — deep eaves, wooden hall frames, lattice fittings, caisson ceilings — rather than claiming a specific building.
