# Supergraphic Ultramodern

## Overview

Supergraphic Ultramodern is the future imagined as a friendly, color-saturated lounge. Emerging in the late 1960s from New Hollywood science-fiction sets, world's-fair pavilions and experimental interior installations, it succeeded Raygun Gothic, Space Age, Googie, Atompunk and Mid-Century Modern, and ran dominant through the 1970s into the early 1980s before being supplanted by Cyberpunk/Laser Grid, Cassette Futurism and Memphis Lite. Its grammar is the molded envelope: rooms, furniture, radios and buildings all behave as if vacuum-formed from one continuous sheet of plastic, then painted in flat, high-key color and wrapped in oversized environmental graphics — the "supergraphics" — that treat walls, floors and partitions as a single printed surface. A nostalgia wave in the late 1990s to mid-2000s (the Ultramodern Revival) restaged the vocabulary on compilation sleeves, club interiors and film parody sets, and the style fed onward into Y2K Futurism and Cassette Retrofuturism. Both the original film-grain era and the cleaner revival era count as this style.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is flat, saturated, evenly-lit color applied in large fields, organized in one of three schemes: (a) a bone-white or cream envelope punctured by two to four warm accents; (b) a monochrome hue room in which walls, floor, carpet and cut-outs all sit inside one color family (all-coral, all-avocado, all-tangerine, all-cobalt); or (c) a sequential rainbow, where many hues appear only as ordered bands, rings or dot rows, never as scattered confetti.

Saturation stays medium-high and value medium-high; color reads as printed ink or molded plastic, never as gradient shading. Concentric banding is the only licensed "gradient."

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Curves and rounded shapes** — the defining constant. Coved ceiling-to-wall transitions, arched doorways, rounded-corner partitions, spherical and capsule volumes, torus and ball objects; primary volumes never meet in a visible right angle.
2. **Rainbow and flat supergraphics** — oversized environmental graphics: concentric arcs, parallel stripes running floor-to-wall, dot matrices, color-blocked zones, and silhouette cut-out screens (profile heads, organic apertures) that function as room dividers.
3. **Flat iconography** — simple geometric pictograms, dot-matrix letterforms, ring-and-circle emblems; graphics behave as symbols, not illustration.
4. **Plastics and shiny metals** — glossy ABS shells, fiberglass matte curves, chrome trim rings and stems.
5. **Inflatable furniture and plush** — sphere-cluster walls, padded and quilted rooms, ball seating, deep carpet pile underfoot.
6. **White/orange interior base** — the default envelope, with brown/orange earth-toned variants.

### Materials and textures

Glossy plastic sheen with a single soft specular highlight; matte fiberglass; vinyl and plush upholstery; flocked or piled carpet; chrome accents; screen-printed flat ink on paper. Surfaces are seamless and synthetic — no grain, no weave, no patina. Photography carries slide-film character: fine grain, slightly warm cast, clean mid-tones.

### Recurring subjects

Interiors first: salons and shops with cut-out silhouette partitions, open-plan offices and cubicle villages wrapped in supergraphic stripes, club lounges with glowing rounded coves, TV and film sets (sci-fi corridors, control rooms, parody apartments), capsule dwellings and pod rooms. Product design: spherical portable radios, pod-shaped televisions on spindly legs, ball and torus chairs. Architecture: sculptural expo pavilions with swooping shells and circular grilles, capsule towers, rounded prefab houses. Print: album sleeves and posters built from concentric rings, stripe tunnels, dot-matrix type and rounded rectangles on cream grounds.

### Composition and lighting

Composition treats space as a continuous envelope: one-point perspectives down corridors of repeating rounded modules, elevated vistas over open plans, foreground cut-out screens layering depth, or — in product and print work — a single centered object on a seamless ground, or a purely flat poster plane. Lighting is diffuse and shadowless: luminous ceilings, coved light slots, glowing rounded panels; product shots use soft studio flash with one gentle highlight per shell. Club and revival variants substitute saturated gel washes (magenta, cobalt, amber) but keep the light even and the color fields legible. Hard directional sun, chiaroscuro and noir contrast are foreign to the style.

### Typography

Rounded geometric sans-serifs, wide caps or confident lowercase, set flat in one color against cream or a hue field; dot-matrix and segmented letterforms as display tricks; type sits as a plane within the supergraphic, not as ornament over it.

## Mood & Values

Optimistic technocracy: technology as pleasure rather than threat. The mood is clean, playful, faintly kitsch and wholly self-confident — corporate grooviness, synthetic comfort, leisure as progress. Spaces feel staged, sanitary and welcoming at once, with a wink of pop humor (face-shaped partitions, ball radios the size of toys). In revival-era work the same confidence turns affectionate, quoting the 1970s with lounge-era cool. Values expressed: faith in the manufactured future, pop accessibility, anti-monumental softness, collective leisure.

## Era & Origins

Late 1960s (New Hollywood sci-fi sets, Expo '70 pavilions, experimental visiona-style installations, capsule architecture) through the early 1980s; coined retrospectively by Evan Collins and Froyo Tam. It reacts against the pointed fins and atomic braggadocio of its Googie/Raygun Gothic predecessors by softening everything into rounded, interiorized comfort, and against modernist austerity by drowning the grid in color. Supplanted mid-1980s by Cyberpunk/Laser Grid, Cassette Futurism and Memphis Lite; revived late 1990s to mid-2000s alongside Millennium Disco and Gen X Soft Club; ancestral to Y2K Futurism and Cassette Retrofuturism.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Mold first, decorate second: every primary volume gets a radius — coved corners, capsule openings, spherical objects.
2. Treat graphics as architecture: stripes, arcs, dot grids and cut-out silhouettes should divide or wrap space, not hang on it.
3. Keep color flat: large unmodulated fields, ordered rainbow sequences, one keynote hue per zone; orange earns priority.
4. Furnish with the inflated and the spherical: ball seating, pod electronics, padded walls, sphere clusters.
5. Light evenly and shadowlessly; let slide-film warmth or saturated gel washes carry the mood.
6. Set type as a flat plane: rounded geometric sans, single color, generous scale.
7. Finish with synthetic surface character: plastic sheen, fiberglass matte, carpet pile, fine film grain.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any interior (salon, office, club, capsule home, sci-fi set), product, pavilion, poster or sleeve. Composition: corridor perspective, elevated vista, centered product, flat poster plane, layered cut-out foregrounds. Lighting: diffuse daylight, luminous ceiling, studio flash, club gel glow. Color handling: white-base-with-accents, monochrome hue room, earth-toned brown/orange variant, full rainbow sequence. Mood: corporate clean, club euphoric, kitsch-humorous, revival-cool. Era layer: 1970s film grain or late-1990s clean print. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a supermarket, a subway car, a swimming pool or a spaceship galley rendered under these rules is fully Supergraphic Ultramodern.

**Avoid.** Exposed right-angle minimalism, brutalist concrete and glass-curtain orthogonality; dominant wood grain, rattan, jute, terracotta or houseplant organicism; utilitarian beige hardware grit, chunky keypads and green-phosphor CRTs; mirror balls, sequins, satin and gold-chain glamour; confetti laminates and asymmetrical squiggle chaos; chrome blobs, translucent candy plastic and digital glitch; airbrushed soft-focus gradients and dim candlelit lounge haze; pointed googie fins, tailfins and atomic starbursts; weathering, rust, grunge, HDR contrast, teal-orange grading; serif revival type and hand-lettered script.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Earth Tones:** shares orange, brown and harvest hues, but Earth Tones leads with natural materials — wood paneling, shag, rattan, plants — and no futurism. Test: if synthetic molded surfaces and flat supergraphics dominate, it is Ultramodern; if woven and grown textures dominate, Earth Tones.
- **Cassette Futurism:** overlaps in sci-fi control rooms, but Cassette Futurism is utilitarian — beige/grey boxes, chunky buttons, labeled analog interfaces, working grit. Test: interface hardware grit foregrounded → Cassette; molded envelope curves and flat color fields foregrounded → Ultramodern.
- **Gen X Soft Club:** a revival-era neighbor in lounge nostalgia, but it softens everything — airbrushed gradients, dim warm lounge light, chillout haze. Test: hard-edged flat print color and molded geometry → Ultramodern; soft-focus gradient glow and low lounge light → Gen X Soft Club.
- **International Typographic Style:** shares flat sans-serif type and print objectivity (rainbow-ring Olympic-era posters sit in the overlap), but ITS holds to a strict asymmetric grid, whitespace restraint and near-absence of ornament. Test: concentric rings, stripes and rounded environmental ornament present → Ultramodern; pure grid discipline with no ornament → ITS.
- **Disco:** shares colored club light in revival interiors, but Disco's materials are mirror, sequin, satin, gold and black, with night-time crowd energy. Test: glitter, mirror balls and dancefloor glamour → Disco; molded plastic shells, supergraphics and even light → Ultramodern.
