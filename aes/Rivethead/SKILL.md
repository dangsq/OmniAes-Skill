# Rivethead

Rivethead is the fashion arm of a music scene: the electro-industrial, EBM and industrial rock underground that coalesced in North America and Europe in the late 1980s. Its clothing is assembled from military surplus, factory workwear and hardware-store finds, worn to mirror the mechanized, dystopian themes of the music. Where adjacent dark scenes court romance or spectacle, rivethead courts function: the ideal figure resembles a construction worker or a soldier, not a vampire or a rave kid. Authentic documentation is deliberately unglamorous — concert and club photography, band press shots, insider magazine spreads — because the subculture rejected theatricality and commercial appeal; much of its gear was worn only inside clubs, by people who dressed plainly in daylight.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The palette is value-driven monochrome: interest comes from texture and sheen contrast (matte cotton against gloss latex against dull metal), never from hue. Warmth enters only through material decay — rust, dust, sweat — not through dye.

| Role | Hex range |
|---|---|
| Base black (leather, cotton, latex, fishnet) | #0B0B0C – #1C1C1E |
| Charcoal, gunmetal, deep shadow | #26262A – #3A3A40 |
| Concrete grey, fog, flash-washed highlights | #6B6B70 – #A8A8AD |
| Olive drab and camouflage blotches | #2A2E1E – #5A6238 |
| Rusted steel, oxidized streaks | #6E4A32 – #A47148 |
| Hardware metals: dull silver, aged brass | #8E9296, #7A6234 |

Accents are rationed: metal hardware shine, brass casings on an ammo belt, at most a deep oxblood or red insignia trim. Fluorescent and UV-reactive color are categorically excluded; hair stays natural black, with at most a near-black blue or oxblood tint at the fringe of the rule.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual prominence

1. **Military surplus and workwear** — cargo and BDU trousers tucked into boots, rolled at the cuff, or cut to shorts above the knee; tactical flight jackets; trench coats; camouflage; webbing vests.
2. **Combat footwear** — combat, tanker, jungle and steel-toe boots (Dr. Martens, Gripfasts, Grinders), sometimes platform-soled, metal-plated or industrially re-laced. Boots are non-negotiable and should stay in frame.
3. **Band apparel** — shirts and patches of EBM/industrial acts; the tactical jumpsuit and dark eyewear of Front 242-style stage uniforms as an echoed quote.
4. **Protective headgear** — welding and flight goggles (often pushed up onto the forehead), respirators and gas masks, dark wraparound sunglasses.
5. **Hardware-store jewelry and fasteners** — chains across the chest or as belts, padlocks, dog tags, cogwheels, nails, screws, computer parts; suspenders, outdated computer cords and black electrical tape used as belts and arm bindings.
6. **Ribbed tank tops and bare-armed suspenders** — the blue-collar factory-worker quote, and the Nitzer Ebb/DAF lineage of black suspenders over cropped tanks.
7. **Body modification** — barcode tattoos symbolizing mechanization, heavy piercings, scarification (well documented in text, rarely legible in surviving photographs).
8. **Fetish crossover**, chiefly in femme presentations — PVC or vinyl corsets and bustiers, fishnets, knee-high stiletto boots and harness straps layered onto military hardware; severe, aggressive sexuality as empowerment.
9. **Spartan grooming** — shaved heads, military buzz cuts, aggressive undercuts, rigid mohawks and bihawks; makeup limited to sharp heavy black eyeliner or industrial grease-paint, never goth theatrical pallor.

### Materials and textures

Worn leather and rubberized PVC; matte ribbed cotton; canvas duck and nylon webbing; unpolished steel, oxidized iron, brass; fishnet; electrical tape. Surfaces carry use: scuffs, dust, sweat, rust flake, cigarette burns, frayed hems, DIY repairs. Film grain and scan artifacts belong to the material world of this style.

### Subjects and settings

Club dancers mid-stomp; band press shots of one to three figures, arms crossed, confrontational; a figure seated on rusted machinery or a flight case; tactical-fetish portraits; stage performers at mic stands in fog and strobe; outdoor documentary portraits against embossed metal doors, concrete walls, pipework and chain-link. The annotated magazine diagram page — a labeled "anatomy" of the uniform — is a legitimate documentary variant of the style.

### Composition and lighting

Functional, not posed: weight on one leg, arms folded, hands in pockets, gear worn rather than displayed. Full-body or three-quarter framing keeps the boots legible; group shots stand shoulder-to-shoulder in uniform black. Lighting is raw: hard direct on-camera flash with shadow fallout on the wall behind, a single bare work lamp, club strobes cutting through fog, flat overcast daylight. High-contrast black-and-white and desaturated color dominate; duotone or sepia scan tones appear. Scanned-print artifacts — white borders, caption type, halftone, vignetting — are idiomatic. A plain backdrop is acceptable only when the lighting stays harsh and unretouched.

## Mood & Values

Confrontation, discipline, endurance. The style expresses individualism, anti-conformity and anti-consumerism; a cynical, hyper-real critique of technological dependence and corporate mechanization; dystopian realism that finds beauty and rhythm in industrial noise. Militaristic and totalitarian signifiers appear satirically, in a Brechtian spirit of alienation — to intimidate and to provoke critical reflection, not to endorse. DIY survivalism matters: adornment is repurposed, not purchased as ornament. On the dance floor the mood shifts to communal, stomping intensity mirroring a mechanical pulse.

## Era & Origins

Roots in the experimental industrial music of late-1970s London (Throbbing Gristle, 1975); branching in the 1980s through German synthesizer aggression (DAF, Die Krupps) and Belgian EBM (Front 242's *Front by Front*, 1988, whose tactical uniforms fed the look directly). The subculture coalesced in the late 1980s around North American and European club nights; the name — a 1940s nickname for assembly-line workers — was fixed onto the scene in 1993 via Re-Constriction Records' *Rivet Head Culture* compilation and Chemlab's track "Rivethead." Sustained by labels like Chicago's Wax Trax! and by "industrial hard dancing"; today by festivals such as Cold Waves and Leipzig's Wave-Gotik-Treffen. Europeans historically said "Industrialists" or "EBM-heads," not rivethead.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

- Utility legibility first: every strap, pocket, buckle and boot should imply function or repurposing; ornament arrives only as hardware.
- Build contrast from value and sheen, not hue: matte cotton, gloss latex, dull steel, grey concrete.
- Keep the documentary register: grain, flash, candid framing, scan artifacts. No editorial gloss.
- Silhouette is columnar and armored — cropped or tucked trousers meeting high boots, layered vests and straps. Nothing flows.
- Let decay supply the only warm notes: rust, dust, sweat.
- Grooming stays spartan; hair natural black or a near-black tint.
- Transgressive iconography must read as critique: generic or defaced militaristic signifiers, barcodes as mechanization tattoos — never literal extremist emblems worn sincerely.

**Variation axes.** Subject: solo portrait, duo or band group, club crowd, mid-stomp dancer, tactical-fetish femme presentation, stage performer, annotated magazine diagram, worker-at-rest on machinery. Composition: full-body standing, seated on equipment, arms-crossed medium shot, candid floor shot, diagrammatic spread. Lighting: hard flash, bare bulb, strobe-in-fog, overcast daylight, high-contrast monochrome conversion. Color handling: pure black-and-white through desaturated color to duotone scan; rare deep-red trim; metal shine. Mood: cold stare, exhausted post-club realism, communal dance fury, satirical menace. Medium: 35mm snapshot, press shot, magazine scan, bordered print.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid**

- Neon or UV color, cyberlox hair falls, fluffy leg warmers, rave brightness.
- Goth romanticism: lace, velvet, mourning veils, crosses, frilled shirts, theatrical pallor, vampire styling, historical pastiche.
- Sleek futurism: chrome implants, LED strips, holograms, neon megacity signage, tailored tech-couture.
- Pristine modern techwear: unworn matte seamless garments, minimalist sneakers.
- Fine jewelry: gold, gems, polished filigree.
- Pastels, florals, bohemian or preppy notes.
- Sincere extremist emblems or reenactment-accurate uniforms with real unit insignia.
- Glossy saturated commercial lighting and beauty retouching.
- Long romantic hairstyles or bright hair color.
- Framing where restraint and erotic binding are the subject (that is Bondage, not rivethead).

## Discrimination Cues

- **Berlin Techno Fashion**: also black and club-functional, but contemporary and sporty — sheer mesh, bucket hats, sleek sneakers or minimal boots, anonymous cool. No surplus militaria, no band merch, no goggles or hardware jewelry; the silhouette is athlete, not soldier.
- **Bondage**: cuffs, rope, gags and binding harnesses make restrained bare skin the subject, usually in dungeon or studio contexts. Rivethead borrows harness and latex as one layer inside a clothed, equipped ensemble in club, street or industrial space. Test: is the gear binding the body or equipping it?
- **Cybergoth**: same accessory ancestry (goggles, boots, straps) fused with rave — fluorescent and UV color, massive synthetic hair falls, fluffy leg warmers, euphoric expressiveness. Rivethead keeps muted tones, natural hair, gritty realism and militancy.
- **Cyberpunk**: speculative future of chrome, implants, LEDs and neon rain; rivethead is analog present-tense decay — surplus cloth, oxidized steel, tape and cable. Its cyberpunk is literary mood, not chrome costuming.
- **Electronic Body Music**: the European precursor and sibling stage uniform — minimal black leather vests over bare chests, matching tactical jumpsuits, dark sunglasses, sweat-minimal dance wear, often band-coordinated. Rivethead individualizes: surplus layers, camouflage, hardware jewelry, goggles, fetish crossover, and the grain of North American club documentation. Coordinated vest-and-shades uniformity leans EBM; a personalized assemblage of surplus and hardware is rivethead.
- **Goth** (frequent mislabel): shared darkness ends at the surface — goth offers Victorian mourning, romance and vulnerability; rivethead offers the factory floor and the barracks.
