# French New Wave (Nouvelle Vague)

The French New Wave was a film movement born in France in the late 1950s, when a generation of *Cahiers du cinéma* critics — Truffaut, Godard, Chabrol, Rohmer, Rivette — plus allies like Varda, Demy, Resnais and Malle turned director and declared war on the studio-bound "Tradition of Quality." Inheriting Italian Neorealism's streets and non-actors, and funded on post-war shoestring budgets, they shot on location with handheld cameras, natural light and improvised equipment (tracking shots pushed in shopping carts), and cut with abrupt jump cuts that flaunted the film as a constructed object. The received visual identity of the movement is therefore the monochrome cinema still: silver-gelatin greyscale, visible grain, available light, real Parisian streets and rooms, and young faces that look back at the camera with irony rather than reverence.

## Visual Identity

**Palette and tonality.** The style is monochrome by definition: all colour information sits on a greyscale axis running from near-black (#0B0B0B) to paper-white (#F4F4F4), with most of the frame living in silver mid-greys (#5A5A5A–#A8A8A8). Highlights are allowed — even expected — to clip to pure white where windows, lamps or sky sit behind the subject, often with a soft halation bloom; shadows stay open enough to hold detail, never crushed into patterned noir blackness. The cast is neutral or faintly cool silver, never sepia or warmly tinted. A minority colour strand exists in the movement's later work (Demy's musicals, Godard's pop period): if colour is used at all, it behaves as flat, poster-like primary blocks — a red coat, a blue wall, a yellow sign — never as pastel grading or modern teal-orange.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.** (1) Natural lighting and location shooting: real apartments, cafés, classrooms, courtyards, shopfronts, city sidewalks with receding one-point perspective. (2) Handheld camerawork and implied movement: figures running, mid-stride blurs, tilted horizons, bodies caught between gestures. (3) Jump-cut fragmentation translated to the still: abrupt crops, elided action, an arm or hand intruding from off-frame, mismatched eyelines, a frame that feels cut rather than composed. (4) Improvisation and non-professional presence: bystanders, extras and onlookers left inside the shot, street clutter un-swept. (5) Self-reflexivity: a subject looking straight into the lens, breaking the fourth wall. (6) Unconventional framing that breaks classical axis rules: off-centre heads, amputated bodies, flat frontal staging against a bare plaster wall. (7) Consumer-modern urban life of the *Trente Glorieuses*: café tables, cigarettes, coffee cups, newspapers, paperbacks, film posters, record players, cars, shop awnings. (8) Youth as subject: adolescents, young women, couples and trios of friends. (9) A minority formalist strand (the Resnais/Demy current): symmetrical tableaus against monumental architecture, balustrades, clipped topiary, garden geometry — still monochrome, still lit by real daylight.

**Materials and textures.** 35mm acetate grain across the whole frame; silver-gelatin tonality with halation around practicals and windows; slight optical softness at frame edges; cigarette-smoke haze in interiors; wool roll-necks and knit jerseys, velvet pile coats, lace bustiers, cotton T-shirts and checked flannel; wire mesh, iron railings, plaster walls, café glass, stone balustrades.

**Recurring subjects.** Young women with bob, pixie or sculpted waved hair; young men in narrow suits with thin ties or casual jackets; adolescent boys; couples in close conversation or embrace across a table; small groups of friends in loose triangulations; a solitary figure held in a window's backlight; street scenes where passers-by glance at the camera.

**Composition and lighting tendencies.** Medium close two-shots and tight, edge-cropped close-ups dominate; deep-focus street vistas and full-length figures against architecture appear at the formal end; direct address to camera punctuates everything. Light is always motivated and found: window backlight blowing to white and rimming hair, soft overcast diffusion outdoors, a single practical lamp or bounce off plaster indoors. Shadows fall where the room puts them. There is no studio key, no fill card, no gel.

## Mood & Values

The emotional register is cool detachment laced with irony: alienation, existential searching, the absurdity of modern life — but equally a kinetic, joking playfulness (running, dancing, teasing) that keeps the melancholy from hardening. The values underneath are the movement's own: auteur theory (the frame as personal handwriting), realism, innovation, rebellion against inherited convention, and open cinephilia — images that quote and love cinema while reminding you that you are watching one.

## Era & Origins

France, late 1950s through the late 1960s, with debut features clustered 1958–1964. It reacts against the literary-adaptation "Tradition of Quality" of classic French cinema and inherits Italian Neorealism's location ethics; it feeds on post-war economic boom consumerism, existentialist philosophy, American jazz and classical Hollywood B-films. Wardrobe and props should read 1958–1968, never later.

## Generation Guidance

Principles:
- Build the image in monochrome first; greyscale is the identity, colour is a flagged exception.
- Light is found, not built: one window, one lamp, one overcast sky. Let highlights blow.
- Make the frame fallible: crop, tilt, intrude, leave the bystander in.
- Catch the moment mid-sentence, mid-stride, mid-drag — never a held pose.
- Populate the world: café clutter, street depth, extras, smoke.
- Dress the period as character, not costume: roll-necks, Breton stripes, checked shirts, white tees, narrow ties, lace, velvet; bobs, pixies, sculpted waves.
- Sign the medium: grain, halation, gentle edge softness.

Variation axes:
- **Subject:** solitary youth ↔ couple ↔ trio ↔ street crowd; interior ↔ exterior; casual snapshot ↔ formal architectural tableau.
- **Composition:** handheld asymmetry ↔ rigid symmetry; edge-cropped close-up ↔ deep one-point vista; direct address ↔ oblivious profile.
- **Lighting:** blown window backlight ↔ flat overcast ↔ single practical; high-key silver ↔ brooding mid-grey — always available light.
- **Colour handling:** default greyscale; optional flat-primary pop strand, never pastel or graded.
- **Mood:** kinetic play ↔ existential stillness; ironic ↔ tender.
- **Medium sign:** coarse grain ↔ fine grain; crisp ↔ slightly soft optics.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A rooftop, a record shop, a railway platform or a kitchen table all qualify if the light is found, the frame is fallible, the tonality is silver and the moment is unposed.

Avoid: saturated modern colour grading, teal-orange or pastel palettes; studio three-point glamour lighting and softbox sheen; film-noir shadow grammar (venetian-blind stripes, fog, night wet streets lit by signage); fashion-editorial polish and retouched skin; "French Girl" costume clichés (beret, baguette, Eiffel Tower backdrop); modern objects (smartphones, laptops, contemporary cars); digital cleanliness (noise-free gradients, oversharpening, lens flare, creamy bokeh circles); symmetrical quirky-prop indie tableaus; Mod graphic pop (Union Jacks, target logos, parkas, scooters as subject); sepia or cross-process filters and vignetting; heroic low-angle poster staging and clean advertising negative space.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared ground | Decisive difference |
|---|---|---|
| Film Noir | Monochrome, cigarettes, urban crime undertow | Noir designs its darkness: studio low-key, blind-stripe shadows, night fog, fatalism. New Wave finds its light: daylight locations, open shadows, grain, irony and youth instead of doom. |
| French Girl | Parisian youth, striped shirts, café tables | French Girl is a polished, often colour, fashion-lifestyle pose — manicured chic, aspirational. New Wave is a narrative still: greyscale, grainy, unmanicured, existential. |
| Indie (modern) | Naturalism, casual bodies, everyday rooms | Indie reads contemporary and digital-clean or pastel-film emulated, often symmetrical. New Wave is period-locked 1958–68, monochrome, grainier, and confronts the lens. |
| Minet | French youth in sharp suits and coiffed hair | Minet is fashion-first, colour-saturated, pose-first with clean backdrops. New Wave borrows the silhouette but subordinates it to found light, street clutter and narrative accident. |
| Mod | 1960s youth culture, sharp tailoring | Mod is British, graphic and colour-blocked — parkas, scooters, dance-club energy, studio pop photography. New Wave is greyscale, café-and-cinémathèque intellectual, looser and cooler. |

Quick test: greyscale plus grain plus available light plus a fallible frame plus period youth in a real location equals French New Wave; remove the grain and add polish and it drifts to French Girl; add designed shadow and night and it drifts to noir; add colour pop graphics and it drifts to Mod.
