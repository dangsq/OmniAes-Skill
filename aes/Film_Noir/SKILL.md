# Film Noir

Film Noir is a crime-film genre and visual style popularized from the 1930s through the 1950s, defined by cynical characters, stark lighting, and an existential philosophy. Its tropes descend from Great Depression-era "hard-boiled" crime fiction — urbanism, fast slang, sex, violence — which displaced the previous decade's country-house mystery, and its look descends from German Expressionism: sharp contrast, dramatic composition, psychological distortion. Everything in the frame conspires toward one effect: a modern city at night where desire and corruption intersect, lit by a single hard source that leaves most of the world in black.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

Strictly monochrome. The working range runs from crushed black (#050505–#101010) through shadow greys (#1c1c1c–#3d3d3d), fog and smoke mid-greys (#555555–#8a8a8a), to reserved paper-white highlights (#d9d9d9–#f2f2f2). The rule is tonal economy: the majority of any frame sits in the lower half of the range, and bright values are rationed — a face, a shirt front, a lamp shade, a curl of smoke, a wet reflection, a sheet of paper. Highlights read as events, not as ambient light. Optional variation: a faint cool steel or selenium cast in the greys; never saturated hue, never warm sepia postcard toning.

### Light and composition

Low-key lighting, deep shadows, strong contrast between light and dark. Signature devices, in order of textual prominence: venetian-blind slat shadows raking across faces and plaster walls; silhouette compositions; backlit figures edged against fog, smoke, or a bright doorway. Practical sources do the work: desk lamps, streetlights, neon signs, match flames, car headlights. German Expressionist inheritance licenses distortion — oblique angles, low and high plunging viewpoints, elongated shadows, architecture looming over figures. Frames-within-frames recur: doorways, stairwells, car windows, mirrors. Composition families observed across the corpus: the carved-out close-up of a face half in black; the two-shot of embrace or menace (hands gripping a shoulder, bodies locked in threat or desire); the object close-up (revolver, badge, papers, typewriter); the dwarfing cityscape of Art Deco towers; the backlit pair standing in smoke; and period title or prologue cards set in display serif or Deco-flavored lettering. Typography is a secondary, supporting element — the style's core is light, not type.

### Motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Lighting apparatus as subject**: window blinds, streetlights, desk lamps — light itself patterned into bars, cones, and pools.
2. **Atmosphere**: fog, cigarette smoke, rain, neon reflections on wet pavement; haze that traps light and compresses space.
3. **Urban night locales**: dim alleyways, smoky nightclubs, bars, gambling dens, rain-soaked streets, sparsely lit apartments; large cities of Art Deco skyscrapers and warehouses.
4. **Costume code**: dark suits, trench coats, tailored dresses, fedoras, silk gloves, fur-trimmed outerwear; figures polished yet worn, attractive yet troubled.
5. **Props**: cigarettes, rotary telephones, desk lamps, detective files, glasses of hard liquor; revolvers; alcohol generally.
6. **Character types**: the private investigator, the undercover policeman, the average man as fall guy, corrupt officials, the Mafia, the femme fatale.
7. **Narrative charge**: violence, sex, mystery — implied rather than shown: a gun laid on a desk, a grasp on an arm, a couple clinging in dread.

### Materials and textures

Wet asphalt mirroring light; felt fedora brims and wool trench cloth; silk and fur; Bakelite rotary phones; glass — tumblers, rain-streaked windows; blued revolver steel; paper files and typewriter ribbons; plaster walls carrying blind shadows. Surface quality is mid-century film: visible grain, halation blooming around practical bulbs, slight optical softness, deep blacks that swallow detail.

## Mood & Values

Pessimism, sexuality, corruption. The style expresses cynicism, betrayal, obsession, and the consequences of desire; secrecy and surveillance hang in the blind shadows and backlit silhouettes; fog and neon produce urban claustrophobia. Characters are simultaneously glamorous and doomed. The associated musical register — slow 1940s–50s jazz, heavy saxophone, melancholy and sensuality — names the emotional temperature: weary, smoky, seductive, fatalistic. ## Era & Origins

Emerged in the 1930s, defined in the 1940s, declined by the mid-1950s as color cinematography took over. Formed by two pressures: German Expressionist émigrés bringing new lighting techniques, visual distortion, and psychological storytelling to Hollywood, and the Great Depression breeding stories of morality, desperation, and survival in modern cities; wartime uncertainty and postwar disillusionment deepened the darkness. Hardboiled detective fiction (Chandler, Hammett) supplied tone and subject. Once obsoletely called "melodrama," it reacts explicitly against the orderly country-house mystery of the prior decade.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.** Light is the narrator: one hard source, everything else falling to black. Ration highlights. Let atmosphere (smoke, fog, rain) make light visible. Dress and props place the decade; lighting places the morality. Violence and sex are charges in the air, not depictions. Keep the grade monochrome and the mood pessimistic.

**Variation axes.**
- *Subject*: any person, place, or object of the crime world — office, bar, alley, staircase, train platform, rooftop, evidence desk, skyline, an empty room with a ringing phone. Even mundane subjects turn noir under the lighting rules.
- *Composition*: extreme close-up to sweeping cityscape; level or distorted angle; populated or empty; silhouette or modeled face; frame-within-frame optional.
- *Lighting*: from crushing single-key chiaroscuro to diffuse backlit fog; blind-slat patterns optional; practical sources varied.
- *Color handling*: any monochrome grade from hard black-white to misty grey; faint cool toning allowed; saturation near zero always.
- *Mood*: dread, seduction, weary melancholy, cold calculation.
- *Setting*: 1930s–1950s dress is the core code; modern-dress noir-inflected work exists (recent television revivals) but then monochrome and chiaroscuro must carry the style alone.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Saturated color or a color-photography look; warm sepia nostalgia; flat, even, high-key or shadowless daylight; softbox glamour with no shadow side; cheerful, wholesome, or comedic advertising mood; contemporary streetwear, smartphones, LEDs, modern vehicles (unless deliberately neo-noir); pastoral country-house settings (manor libraries, garden parties); explicit gore or splatter — violence stays implied or aftermath; HDR crispness, lens flare, creamy bokeh, noise-free digital sterility; candy-neon color dominating the frame (noir neon is monochrome glow and reflection); symmetrical pastel tableau composition; handheld overcast documentary snap.

## Discrimination Cues

- **After Hours**: also night-city, but bathed in saturated electric neon (blue, pink, green), contemporary, surreal and dreamlike in its emptiness. Noir is monochrome, period-dressed, crime-narrative, and lit by hard chiaroscuro rather than neon color wash.
- **Femme Fatale**: a character-centered glamour aesthetic — the seductress as subject, often in color, with red lips, satin, and flattering key light. In noir she is one element inside a shadowed crime world; if the frame is only glamorous seduction without black-weighted chiaroscuro and crime context, it is Femme Fatale.
- **French New Wave**: also black-and-white, but natural available light, overcast daylight streets, handheld casualness, 1960s dress, ironic playfulness. Noir stages its light (blinds, silhouettes, studio smoke) and wears 1940s tailoring and dread.
- **Mafia Aesthetic**: organized-crime family saga, often warm-toned or color, opulent suits, cigars, churches, weddings, loyalty and power celebrated. Noir's crime is individual, trapped, pessimistic, monochrome, and urban-night; it mourns rather than glorifies.
- **Spy Fiction**: Cold War jet-set gloss — gadgets, sleek mid-century modernism, geopolitical stakes, clean cool palettes or color, tailored but untroubled heroes. Noir stays domestic, decayed, and morally compromised, with shadow doing the talking.
