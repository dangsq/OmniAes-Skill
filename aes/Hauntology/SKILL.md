# Hauntology (Spectral Studies, Lost Future)

Hauntology is a loosely defined category of music, art and aesthetic theory organized around the "lost future": the persistence of cultural memory from a post-war Britain that imagined utopian, technologically optimistic tomorrows which never arrived. Coined by Jacques Derrida in 1993 and adopted in the 2000s by British electronic musicians and critics (Mark Fisher, Simon Reynolds, the Ghost Box label, The Caretaker, Burial), it became a visual language built from the residue of 1960s–70s institutional media — Public Information Films, children's educational television, civic print, library music sleeves — deliberately shown in a state of decay. The aesthetic's central act is *memory degradation made visible*: grain, fade, tracking error and foxing are not damage to conceal but the subject itself. A parallel satirical strand, embodied by the Scarfolk Council project, reuses the recognizable formats of 1970s government communication and subverts them with distressing instructions, producing a "dark alternate timeline" of British welfare-state modernism.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The palette rule is *institutional mutedness with decay as the only saturation source*. Work in one of three registered strata:

1. **Muted colour film/print** — dull greens and olives (#4E5442 → #8A8B6C), institutional mustard and yellow (#A98D2F → #D2BC6A, always faded), aged paper cream and taupe (#E4DCC6 → #9C8B74), faded pastels such as powder blue, dusty rose and mint (#A9B7B4, #C2A5A0, #B7C0AE).
2. **Xerox/photogram monochrome** — ink black (#0B0B0B) against paper white (#F1EFE8), midtones partially eaten by copier contrast.
3. **Broadcast grey** — desaturated cyan-grey of degraded tape (#6E7E80 → #C0C8C6), scanlines laying a cool veil over everything.

Within any stratum, at most one *decay accent* is permitted: stamp red, signal orange or light-leak amber (#B4402A, #C96F3B, #D98E32) appearing as flash burn, leaked film edge, or printed ink — never as design vibrancy. Neon, chrome and candy saturation are outside the style entirely.

### Signature motifs (ranked by textual prominence)

1. **Brutalist and decaying modernist concrete architecture** — the style's only named key motif: monolithic social-planning buildings, once beacons of futurity, now stained, cracked and emptied.
2. **Public Information Film and children's educational media framing** — grainy 16mm-style frames, flat didactic staging, ambiguously instructive graphics.
3. **Obsolete-media artifacts** — VHS tracking lines and scanline bands, tape warp, halftone dot, discolored and foxed print matter; the artifact layer is mandatory in spirit even when the subject is new.
4. **Subverted civic print (the Scarfolk strand)** — parody leaflets, posters and adverts in period grotesque type delivering official-sounding, disturbing messages.
5. **Institutional interiors and empty civic space** — corridors with peeling paint, checkered floors, waiting rooms, classrooms awaiting people who never come.
6. **Faceless, masked or erased figures and folk effigies** — blank oval faces, hoods, masks, wooden winged effigies in overcast fields. The written canon sanctions this only indirectly ("unsettlingly ambiguous graphics," Scarfolk's distressing imagery); the visual corpus carries it strongly, so treat it as a licensed secondary current rather than the core.
7. **Obsolete audio hardware and tape culture** — reel-to-reel decks, looped tape, library-music sleeves; the visual echo of vinyl crackle and tape hiss.
8. **Ambiguous abstract graphics** — simple painted or geometric shapes whose meaning is withheld, in the manner of Ghost Box sleeve abstraction.

### Materials and textures

Film grain and halftone dot; xerox toner blotches and photocopy ghosting; paper fiber, foxing and crease; VHS scanlines and tracking tear; peeling institutional paint; wet or chalky concrete; faded painted murals and animation cels; motion-smear blur of degraded video. Surfaces should feel *chemically aged*, never digitally clean.

### Composition and lighting

Deadpan frontal archive framing: centered solitary subject, flat overcast daylight, the camera as indifferent documentarian. One-point perspective down empty corridors. Overhead diagrammatic views of floors and ritual circles. Xerox collage layering — cut-out eyes, hands and profiles multiplied across a sheet. A centered silhouette dissolved in a scanline field. Snapshot aesthetics: direct flash at night, light leaks, motion blur. Thresholded monochrome with no midtones, as in photograms and overexposed profiles. Lighting is never glamorous: overcast flatness, underexposed interiors, harsh amateur flash, or backlight blowing the background to white.

### Typography and graphic voice

Period civic grotesque (Helvetica/Univers-family), all-caps, tight and authoritative, often stamped or stenciled; the voice is the gentle imperative of public instruction, optionally subverted into menace. A secondary hand-lettered, scratchy folk-occult lettering exists in the cult-poster strand. Clean vector type, chrome lettering and modern branding break the spell.

## Mood & Values

Nostalgia for lost utopianism; dread; cultural memory; temporal disjunction; mourning for the future. The feeling is eerie familiarity — something remembered wrongly, or remembered by no one. The style values sincerity of grief over irony of play: it trusts its artifacts enough to grieve them, and subverts civic trust slowly, with a straight face, rather than through shock cuts.

## Era & Origins

Term from Derrida's 1993 *Specters of Marx*; aesthetic movement formed in 2000s Britain around Ghost Box, The Caretaker and Burial, codified by Fisher and Reynolds, with the Scarfolk blog (later books, 2014 and 2019) as its print arm. Presaged by Boards of Canada and rooted in BBC Radiophonic Workshop-era library music. It reacts against neoliberal "terminal time" — the sense that no alternative social future is imaginable — by haunting the present with the welfare-state futures that were cancelled.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Invent the artifact first, then decay it: imagine a specific civic object (PIF still, school film frame, poster, archive snapshot, record sleeve) and apply one or two decay processes (grain plus fade; tracking plus warp; xerox threshold).
2. Keep color inside the muted institutional band; saturation may enter only as decay (light leak, stamp ink, flash burn).
3. Deny the face often: mask, hood, blank oval, erasure, overexposure, substitution by collage.
4. Let emptiness be a subject: corridors, fields, rooms, platforms.
5. Place anachronism quietly — a future that never happened — never loudly.
6. Monochrome and broadcast-grey strata are fully legitimate; color is optional.

**Variation axes.** Subject: architecture, figure, print artifact, object, rural landscape, abstract graphic. Composition: frontal archive, corridor perspective, overhead diagram, collage sheet, centered silhouette. Lighting: overcast flat, underexposed interior, night flash, backlight blowout, thresholded mono. Color handling: muted color film, xerox mono, broadcast grey, two-tone print. Mood temperature: melancholy ↔ dread ↔ eerie calm. Medium simulation: 16mm grain, VHS, photocopy, photogram, painted cel, ambiguous low-render.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A purely typographic poster, a purely architectural photograph, or a purely abstract scanline field all qualify if the palette, decay and institutional voice hold.

**Avoid.** Neon magenta/cyan grids, chrome type, Greek statues, palms, mall glamour; emergency-broadcast alert screens and jump-scare faces; CRT terminal green as primary palette; rust-scavenged junk assemblage and duct-tape DIY; pristine contemporary photography with no period media decay; clean vector flatness, 4K sharpness, smartphones, modern logos; candy saturation; lens-flare glamour; meme typography.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared surface | The tell that separates |
|---|---|---|
| Analog Horror | VHS artifacts, uncanny faces | Analog Horror (US-centric, 80s–90s) aims to frighten now: alert screens, jump cuts, threat. Hauntology (British, 60s–70s) aims to mourn: decay as metaphor, civic pedagogy as source, dread without a monster. |
| Signalwave | Broadcast artifacts, scanlines | Signalwave glorifies the broadcast surface — idents, test cards, glossy grids, saturated glow. Hauntology's signal is failing, paper-bound, and tied to institutional messaging rather than TV glamour. |
| Vaporwave | Retro nostalgia, sampling culture | Vaporwave ironizes 80s–90s consumer capitalism with neon, chrome and statues. Hauntology sincerely grieves 60s–70s public-sector futurism in earth tones; no neon, no irony-as-shield. |
| Liminal Space | Empty corridors, unease | Liminal Space is ahistorical: clean contemporary emptiness. Hauntology's emptiness is always dated and mediated — grain, halftone, tracking lines, period fixtures. |
| Junkpunk | Obsolete technology | Junkpunk scavenges and clutter-builds with rust and tape-on-the-side energy. Hauntology's tech is institutional, orderly and chemically faded: a reel-to-reel in grass, not a junkyard assemblage. |

Fastest single tell: if the image reads as a *grieving archive* of welfare-state modernism — muted olive and mustard, concrete, civic type, faces denied — it is Hauntology; if it reads as a scare, a joke, a sale, or a clean empty room, it is one of the neighbors.
