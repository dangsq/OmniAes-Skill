# Cyberpop

Cyberpop — originally just "cyber fashion" (サイバーファッション) in 1990s Japan — is retro-futurism played as pop music: a vision of the future that is incredibly bright, neon, and affiliated with dance and party culture. Where Cyberpunk and Cybergoth imagine tomorrow as post-apocalyptic and dark, Cyberpop imagines it as a candy-colored club. Its outline is stylized and clean, its aura positive, its hardware molded plastic rather than riveted steel. The style lives at the intersection of Harajuku street fashion, rave culture and Y2K Futurism, and it survives today in game key art, pop producers and a small vendor scene (Cyberdog, Takuya Angel, Kiss Me Kill Me, Coquetry Clothing, Product_C, Fötus).

## Visual Identity

### Color

Neon is the base coat, not an accent. The working palette runs across saturated synthetics: hot magenta and pink (#FF1E8E–#FF6EC7), electric cyan and blue (#00E5FF–#4DC9FF), neon lime and green (#A8FF00–#39FF14), neon yellow (#FFE600), tangerine orange (#FF8A00), lavender and lilac (#C9A7FF), plus pastel dilutions of all of these (bubblegum #FFB3D9, periwinkle #9CC9FF). The "neutrals" of the style are holographic silver, iridescent white and pearl vinyl (#E6E6F0 with a rainbow shift), not beige or gray. Black may appear as a small grounding base — a hoodie body, a corset panel — but it is always overrun by neon graphics, trim and accessories; a black-dominant image is not Cyberpop. Color blocking is bold and deliberate: one garment per hue, clashing on purpose.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the style documents them)

1. **Orbit / retro-future rings** — circular rings mounted around collars, sleeves, skirts, shorts and jackets to emulate orbital energy; the single most characteristic garment hardware.
2. **Space Age tailoring** — shoulder puffs, space-suit-like jumpsuits, neon overalls with giant plastic buttons.
3. **Space iconography** — alien faces and spaceships, rocket ships, stars, shooting stars and planets, cut from neon laser-cut acrylic into necklaces and hairpieces, or applied as stencils, prints and clips.
4. **Neon faux fur** — as trim on jackets and shrugs, or as entire skirts.
5. **UFO pants** — wide-legged, drawstring, neon; shared with Kandi Raver but core here.
6. **Platform footwear** — platform sneakers and high tops in neon, gogo boots paired with fluffy leg warmers, the leg warmers sometimes studded with giant buttons.
7. **The Cyberdog shield necklace** — a soft-plastic "V" line with holes along its surface, worn across the whole community in many colors.
8. **Cyberlox and neon hair** — heavily bleached or wigged neon shades; tubular crin and rexlace falls (cyberlox) replacing older synthetic dreads; spray-stencil stars, planets and hearts in the hair.
9. **Chunky headwear** — plastic goggles, futuristic headphones, translucent neon headbands and visors; space-motif hair clips (and plastic bunny-ear headbands) in Decora-leaning outfits.
10. **Y2K graphic layer** — pixel-art icons (8-bit UFOs, hearts, sparkles), chrome or bubble type, sticker-scatter on displays and flyers; an image-supported extension of the style's documented Y2K Futurism lineage.

### Materials and textures

Only synthetic surfaces: patent PVC and vinyl with hard specular highlights; holographic and iridescent laser-cut acrylic; plush neon faux fur; tubular crin strands; soft matte molded plastic (buttons, goggles, shield necklaces); metallic laminate and silver vinyl; glossy printed pixel graphics. The rule is absolute per the style's own definition: natural materials are never used as fashion fabric — no linen, wool, suede, raw leather or undyed cotton texture anywhere in frame (a neon-dyed canvas high-top is the tolerated casual exception). Texture contrast comes from shine-versus-fluff (vinyl against faux fur), never from matte organics.

### Recurring subjects

Solo or paired fashion models in full outfit; accessory still lifes and flat lays (headbands, hairpins, necklaces); retail display walls of mannequin torsos dressed in complete coordinates; club and stage performers; flyer, poster and magazine pages with chunky type and tracklists; TV-segment frames documenting handmade clothes and props; game and anime key art. Mundane backdrops — convenience-store aisles, event halls, checkered floors, diamond-plate metal — are welcome and are treated brightly, as if the future invaded the everyday.

### Composition and lighting

Full-body standing poses with playful gestures (peace signs, pointing, mid-dance); flat frontal display arrangements; square accessory flat lays; editorial pages with text blocks. Lighting is high-key and even: on-camera flash snapshots, fluorescent store light, seamless studio backdrops in white, gray or pink, or saturated club glow with neon signage. Shadows are soft or absent; the outline stays clean and stylized. There is no noir, no chiaroscuro, no grime.

## Mood & Values

Optimism as ideology: the future envisioned as incredibly bright and neon. Cyberpop values party and dance culture, playfulness, fantasy and DIY craft — handmade props and garments are celebrated, not hidden. It is communal (the shared shield necklace) and performative (flyers, club stages, game avatars). The emotional register ranges from kawaii-sweet to cool-futuristic to rave-euphoric, but never cynical, melancholic or threatening.

## Era & Origins

Emerged in 1990s Japan as "cyber fashion," within the Harajuku street ecosystem, absorbing Space Age revival and, by the turn of the millennium, Y2K Futurism. Touchstones of the imagination include films like *Hackers* (1995) and *The Fifth Element* (1997) and games like *Space Channel 5* (1999) and *Pop'n Music*. The style persists in a long tail: vendors such as Cyberdog and Takuya Angel, games (*Splatoon*, *Project Sekai*, *Muse Dash*, *Ninjala*), and pop artists (Slayyyter, Grimes, Moe Shop, PAiDA, 8485, iris day, CYBERSLUT, KAMON!!!, Shadow Cliq). It defines itself *against* the dark dystopia of Cyberpunk and Cybergoth: same future, opposite mood.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Neon is the ground color; pastels and holo-silver dilute it, black never dominates it.
2. Every surface reads synthetic: patent shine, holographic shift, faux-fur fluff, molded plastic.
3. Build retro-future hardware into the clothing itself: orbit rings, shoulder puffs, giant plastic buttons, drawstring UFO pants.
4. Keep outlines clean and stylized; light flat and bright; exclude dirt, decay and dramatic shadow.
5. Decorate with space-age kitsch: alien/rocket/star/shooting-star shapes in laser-cut acrylic, pixel-art stickers, stencil stars in hair.
6. Use imagined retro-future props (chunky translucent gadgets, visors, headphone shells), not sleek contemporary devices.
7. Carry a positive, party-ready aura — the subject looks like they are headed to a rave, not a riot.

**Variation axes.** Subject: any of fashion model, accessory still life, display wall, club performer, flyer/poster layout, game key art, candid street or convenience-store snapshot, handmade-prop tutorial frame. Composition: vertical full-body, square flat lay, grid display, editorial page with type, off-kilter flash snapshot. Lighting: high-key studio, fluorescent candid, on-camera flash, pastel gradient, saturated club glow. Color handling: full-saturation neon clash, pastel-neon kawaii mix, or holographic-silver-dominant with neon accents. Mood: sweet kawaii, cool futuristic, or euphoric rave. Gender, body type and setting are open. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Dystopian grime (rain, smog, rust, blood, noir shadow); black-dominant goth or industrial gear (rivets, spikes, mesh, coffin motifs); earth tones and matte natural fabrics (beige, olive, rust, brown; linen, wool, suede, raw leather); minimalist modern techwear with matte shells and sleek AR glasses; bead-kandi stacks, pacifiers and plushies as the focal accessory; rainbow clip-and-toy clutter with no space motifs; preppy collegiate staples (polo shirts, argyle, pleated khakis); punk hardware (safety pins, studs, ripped tartan); sepia or desaturated grades, heavy film grain, vignettes; ornate historical or boho-organic elements (lace, florals, rattan); earnest contemporary consumer tech.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Tell that separates it from Cyberpop |
|---|---|
| Cyberprep | Preppy collegiate silhouette (polos, sweater vests, tidy slacks) in pastel with clean corporate-tech accents; no platforms, faux fur, cyberlox or space kitsch. |
| Cyberpunk | Dystopian narrative: rain-slick noir, grime, utilitarian techwear, chrome implants; neon exists as signage accent on darkness, and the mood is cynical. |
| Cybergoth | Black-based industrial rave: mesh, rivets, dark makeup, neon only as accent; Cyberpop inverts the ratio — bright base, plastic not metal, positive aura. |
| Decora | Childlike maximalism of clips, toys and rainbow layers with no unified futurism; no orbit rings, shoulder puffs, holo vinyl or platform boots. Space-themed intent is the divider. |
| Kandi Raver | Overlapping neon palette and shared UFO pants, but Kandi centers bead-kandi stacks, candy cuffs, body glitter and sporty festival dress, and tolerates natural materials; Cyberpop forbids natural materials and adds structured Space Age tailoring and laser-cut acrylic space jewelry. |
| Shibuya Punk | Punk attitude and hardware — studs, rips, safety pins, rock sneer; Cyberpop replaces metal aggression with molded plastic play and a smile. (Evidence for this boundary is thinner; treat mood and hardware as the cue.) |

Extra nuance: space motifs alone do not make Cyberpop — literal space-print fashion (Uchuu Kei) can be monochrome or somber. Cyberpop requires the neon party palette plus rave-derived silhouette plus clean retro-future optimism, all three at once.
