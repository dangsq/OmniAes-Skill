# Rolinga

Rolinga, also called "Stone," is an urban youth subculture born in Argentina in the late 1980s and at its peak in the mid-1990s, organized around a near-religious devotion to The Rolling Stones and an imitation of the band's 1960s–70s look, Mick Jagger above all. It is deliberately androgynous: men and women share the same blunt fringe, tight band t-shirts and neck jewelry. It is also a neighborhood creed — working-class, suburban, barrio-centric, intertwined with football culture and the barra bravas — and a musical tribe devoted to "rock rolinga" (Callejeros, Ratones Paranoicos, Los Piojos, Intoxicados, Viejas Locas, Los Gardelitos, El Bordo, plus admired outsiders like La Renga and Patricio Rey y sus Redonditos de Ricota). Visually it is a street style of worn cotton, faded denim and screen-printed allegiance, photographed without polish: friends slumped in a row on a curb, a flag waving over a recital crowd, a sticker sheet of band logos spread on a table.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette
The canon is four colors: denim blue, black, white and red. Treat them as a rule, not a swatch: denim blue carries the lower body and jackets (roughly #2B4A6F to a sun-faded #7A93B4); black and off-white cotton carry the torso and hair (#0E0E0E–#2A2A2A against #E9E4D8–#F4F1E8, never pure optical white); red appears as allegiance, not decoration — the tongue-and-lips logo, a single red t-shirt, a band flag, lettering on a wall (#A6192E–#D8232A). Backdrops supply the only other tolerated hues: terracotta brick (#8A4A33–#B06A4A) and concrete gray (#8F8F88–#B9B9B2). Overall saturation stays low and slightly sun-bleached except for the red accent, which is allowed to scream. Pastels, neons, earthy boho browns and tropical color never belong.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the culture documents them
1. **The flequillo rolinga haircut** — the single most iconic feature: a long, shaggy mullet with a thick straight fringe covering the eyebrows; on women, long dark hair with the same blunt cut fringe. Androgynous by definition.
2. **The Rolling Stones tongue-and-lips logo and band t-shirts** — the core printed surface, extended to the logos of rolinga-rock bands on shirts, cloth flags, stickers and hand-drawn patches.
3. **Flared jeans**, often torn at knee or hem; accepted alternatives include jamaican pants, jogging pants and jackets.
4. **Worn platform sneakers**, classically Topper or Adidas, scuffed beyond repair.
5. **Neckwear**: a frayed scarf knotted at the throat, choker necklaces, plus bracelets at the wrist.
6. **Androgynous styling** across genders — tight shirts, long hair, jewelry on men and women alike.
7. **The barrio stage set**: brick walls, chalk and spray tags in Spanish, sidewalk curbs, football terraces and banners.
8. **Later-layer variants**: dreadlocks and Rastafari/villero inflections entering from the late 1990s onward — acceptable only when combined with the canon above.

Image-supported extensions of the same devotion: sticker and patch sheets mixing band logotypes with skulls, marijuana leaves and gothic lettering; cartoon parodies wearing band shirts; parents and children in matching logo tees; raised fists in a night-time pit. These are legitimate rolinga surfaces because they materialize the text's central value — tribe loyalty — even where the prose only implies them.

### Materials and textures
Sun-faded cotton jersey with cracked screen print and stretched collars; denim faded at the thigh and frayed at the hem; scuffed suede and canvas sneakers; the raw edge of a torn scarf; cheap metal chokers and beads; matte brick and mortar grit; chalk dust and spray paint; photocopied cloth flags; glossy vinyl stickers; cigarette smoke and concert sweat. Everything reads as owned for years, never bought new.

### Recurring subjects
Young working-class Argentines alone, in pairs, or in groups; bands posing informally; concert crowds and pit-raised fists; families passing logos between generations; still lifes of stickers, patches and flags; football-terrace crowds. The group outranks the individual: belonging is the subject.

### Composition and lighting
Amateur snapshot grammar. A horizontal row of friends seated on a curb against a tagged wall; three-quarter standing poses with hands in pockets or a cigarette raised; candid concert frames with warm stage bokeh behind a silhouetted fist; flat overcast daylight or harsh on-camera flash at night; slight grain, motion softness, compact-camera cropping, off-center horizons. No studio polish, no styled editorial pose, no seamless backdrop.

## Mood & Values
Rock and roll as a life creed, not a hobby. Neighborhood identity (barrio) worn as pride; football loyalty fused with band loyalty; authenticity measured in worn cloth and underground circuit membership; an open anti-pop, anti-"establishment" contempt that excludes even punk and experimental music. The mood swings between bored street slump and ecstatic recital defiance — tender inside the tribe, hostile to outsiders. Economic hardship is present but never pitied: torn jeans and dead sneakers are lived reality, displayed without shame.

## Era & Origins
Argentina, late 1980s, crystallizing when Ratones Paranoicos abandoned punk for rock and roll alongside the birth of Los Piojos and Viejas Locas; peaking mid-1990s. Ingredients: Jagger's 1960s silhouette, early Stones blues-rock, heavy metal's apology of "neighborhood life," the crowd behavior of underground audiences (Redonditos de Ricota, Sumo), the poverty and institutional decay of the Menem years, and barra brava culture. Fixed on film in *Pizza, birra, faso* (1998), *Okupas* (2000) and *Tumberos* (2002). Decline arrives after December 2001, when cumbia villera and the Cumbiero tribe take the poorest sectors, and after the 2004 Cromañon fire damages the tribe's public image.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**
- Hair first: the thick fringe and shaggy mullet silhouette identifies the style before any garment does.
- Allegiance on the body: at least one surface carries a band logo — printed tee, flag, sticker, patch, tattoo.
- Four-color discipline: denim, black, off-white, with red reserved for logos and flags.
- Worn, not styled: fade, fray, crack, scuff. Distress must read as lived, not purchased.
- Barrio ground: brick, mortar, curb, terrace, pit, Spanish-language tags.
- Snapshot optics: flat day light, harsh flash, stage bokeh, grain, imperfect framing.
- Belonging over individuality: groups, matching logos, shared flags; solitary figures only in defiance or devotion.

**Variation axes**
- Subject: portrait, group row, concert pit, family transmission, sticker/patch flat lay, flag-painting workshop, football terrace, cassette or fanzine cover graphic, record-stall still life.
- Composition: seated curb row, standing cluster, tight crop on fringe-and-logo, overhead flat lay, crowd seen from the stage.
- Lighting: overcast noon, on-camera flash at a night party, backlit stage bokeh, fluorescent hallway.
- Color handling: faded film wash, saturated red accent on muted ground, black-and-white shirt print against brick warmth.
- Mood: bored slump, raised-fist ecstasy, tender family moment, territorial stare.

A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image. A rooftop flag-hanging, a kitchen table of stickers, or a woman alone at a fence can be perfectly rolinga with none of the reference frames repeated.

**Avoid**
- Pastels, beige bohemian layering, florals, embroidery, wide-brim hats, artisanal silver.
- Neon sportswear, baseball caps, tropical prints, bailanta dance-hall settings (cumbiero territory).
- The grunge uniform: flannel over thermal, plaid dominance, straight ripped jeans with Converse, forest Pacific-Northwest backdrops, ironic apathy.
- Tie-dye, peace signs, beads, sandals, pastoral fields (hippie).
- Studio editorial lighting, seamless backdrops, luxury logos, minimalist composition, influencer-clean skin and hair, fashion-editorial "designer distress."
- Dreadlocks or Rastafari cues as the sole identifier, detached from the fringe-logo-denim canon.
- Generic Anglo suburbia: lawns, picket fences, English-language signage that erases the Argentine barrio.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared ground | Rolinga tell |
|---|---|---|
| Grunge | Torn denim, thrift economy, long hair | Flared jeans and platform sneakers instead of straight jeans and Converse; loud logo allegiance instead of plaid anonymity; brick barrio instead of forest; celebratory faith in rock instead of irony |
| Bohemian | Layered neck jewelry, long hair | Mass-print cotton logos instead of embroidery and florals; denim-black-red instead of earth tones; urban hardness instead of artisanal romance |
| Hippie | Long hair, counter-culture stance | Screen-printed electric logos instead of tie-dye and peace signs; sneakers and concrete instead of sandals and pasture |
| Cumbiero | Same working-class Argentine root, some sportswear overlap | Flequillo and band tee instead of baseball cap and neon track jacket; rock pit and flags instead of bailanta club; the two tribes are documented rivals, not cousins in dress |

Quick recognition check: thick straight fringe + tongue-or-band logo + flared worn denim + frayed scarf or choker + brick/curb/pit backdrop + snapshot light = rolinga. Remove the logo devotion and the fringe and the style collapses into grunge or generic rock casual; keep them and almost any subject will still read true.
