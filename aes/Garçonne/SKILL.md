# Garçonne

## Overview

Garçonne ("boy" with a feminine suffix) is the fashion and cultural movement of 1920s Paris that dressed the post-war "New Woman" in a deliberately androgynous uniform: short bobbed hair, a flattened chest, a linear rectangular silhouette, and clothes borrowed from the male wardrobe — tailored suits, blazers, trousers, stiff collared shirts — or, alternatively, simple drop-waist dresses that deny the curve of the waist. It is an aesthetic of subtraction. Where the preceding Edwardian mode added corsets, flounces and volume, Garçonne removes them, leaving a streamlined vertical line built for freedom of movement. The look carries a political charge: it emerged from French feminism and the self-reliance women acquired during World War I, was scandalized by Victor Margueritte's 1922 novel *La Garçonne*, and was championed in cloth by Coco Chanel. Its emotional register is cool, cerebral, self-possessed — a woman holding a hat at her hip or a cigarette between two fingers with total nonchalance, gaze level or turned away in profile.

The surviving visual record is overwhelmingly monochrome: matte gelatin-silver studio portraits against mottled painted backdrops, street and terrace snapshots, and a secondary strand of flat lithographic advertising posters in muted olive, ochre and cream with bold black lettering. Color information therefore comes chiefly from the style's own doctrine — neutral tones with a single pop of red at the mouth — rather than from saturated period color photography.

## Visual Identity

### Palette

The rule is neutrality plus one controlled accent. Roughly nine-tenths of any Garçonne image should sit in an achromatic-to-earth neutral band; the only permitted saturation is a deep red confined to the lips (and, rarely, a small accessory).

Monochrome handling is itself a palette decision: pure black-and-white, warm sepia, or olive-toned print all read as Garçonne. If color is used at all, it must stay desaturated except for the mouth.

### Signature motifs, ranked by textual authority

1. **Bobbed hair — the coupe à la garçonne.** The defining element. Sleek, helmet-close, sharply cut: shingle bobs, Eton crops, side-parted polished bobs, optionally finished with a spit curl (kiss curl) against the cheek. Hair has a pomaded sheen and a hard, clean edge at the nape and cheek.
2. **The flattened, linear silhouette.** Rectangular torso, dropped or absent waistline, no bust emphasis, no hip curve. The body reads as a vertical column.
3. **Menswear tailoring.** Tailored suits, single-breasted blazers and jackets with notched lapels, high-waisted wide-leg trousers, crisp white collared shirts, bow ties or neckties, breast-pocket squares, waistcoats. Held props continue the grammar: a round brimmed hat or bowler carried at the hip, a cane, gloves.
4. **Simple drop-waist dresses.** The feminine alternative: unadorned, straight, knee-length, waistline dropped to the hip; worn with long pearls at most sparingly, never with beading or fringe.
5. **The cloche hat.** Bell-shaped felt worn pulled low over the eyebrows, compressing the head into the silhouette.
6. **Contrasting feminine makeup.** Pale matte complexion, thinly plucked sharply defined eyebrows, and bold red lipstick drawn into a cupid's bow — the deliberate tension point against the boyish clothes.
7. **Leisure and dandy props (image-supported, secondary).** A cigarette held with loose elegance, hands thrust in trouser pockets, sprawled or leaning seated poses on plain chairs; sporty leisure wear in the Chanel orbit (sailor trousers, jersey, beach pyjama-style ensembles).

### Materials and textures

Wool crepe, flannel and tweed with a dry matte hand; crisp cotton poplin and piqué shirts; silk bow ties and pocket squares with a subdued lustre; pressed felt cloches; jersey for leisure wear; pomaded hair with a lacquered sheen against matte skin. Texture contrast is tonal, not decorative: the soft nap of wool against the hard glare of a white collar.

### Recurring subjects

Solitary women in menswear, full-length or bust-length; women in profile showing the geometry of the bob; pairs and small groups of women in easy companionship (on stools, terraces, steps); women in simple dresses with bobbed heads; dandyish seated figures with cigarette or cane; advertising-poster profiles doubling or mirroring the bobbed head. The subject is always a person asserting presence through restraint — never a crowd, never a party.

### Composition and lighting

Vertical formats dominate. Standing figures carry the weight on one hip-off axis, one hand in a pocket, the other holding a hat; bust portraits favor three-quarter or full profile against an empty ground; seated poses are openly dandyish — legs crossed or angled, elbows loose, on a plain bentwood or stool-like chair. Negative space is generous and empty: a mottled painted canvas backdrop, a bare wall, a soft gradient from light to shadow. Lighting is soft and diffused — frontal studio light with gentle falloff, or even daylight outdoors — producing modeled but never dramatic faces, matte blacks, and luminous unblown whites. The print strand uses flat, shadowless even light with solid color planes and heavy letterpress typography. Surface qualities that authenticate: fine silver grain, slight edge vignetting, matte print density, occasional period photographer's signature script in a corner.

## Mood & Values

Androgyny, independence, modernity, freedom of movement, and the rejection of traditional femininity as ornament. The Garçonne mood is intellectual cool: self-contained, slightly insolent, unbothered. Pleasure is expressed as ease of body — stride, slouch, sprawl — not as decoration or revelry. There is a quiet polemical edge: this clothing was once arrestable offense, and the images carry the composure of someone who knows it. Sentimentality, coquettishness and overt sexual display are foreign to the style; so is luxury signaled by sparkle. Elegance here equals cut, line and attitude.

## Era & Origins

Paris, France, 1920s, in the wake of World War I. The war pushed women into work and self-reliance; the "New Woman" needed unrestrictive clothing, and designers such as Coco Chanel supplied comfortable fabrics and menswear-derived garments. The term went public through Margueritte's scandalous 1922 novel *La Garçonne*; public outrage (Marlene Dietrich famously threatened with arrest for wearing a man's suit in public) only cemented the look as the decade's signature. Garçonne reacts against the corseted hourglass of the Belle Époque and, subtly, against the decorative hedonism of its American cousin, the Flapper.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Build the silhouette first: a straight vertical column, dropped waist, flattened chest, hem near the knee or trousers to the shoe. No cinching anywhere.
2. Cut the hair short and sharp; the bob's edge is a graphic element as important as any garment line.
3. Dress from the male wardrobe's grammar (jacket, collar, tie, trouser) or from a single unadorned drop-waist dress; keep surfaces free of pattern except at most a subtle pinstripe or herringbone.
4. Reserve all color saturation for the lips; everything else stays in the neutral band or in monochrome.
5. Pose for nonchalance, not performance: pocketed hands, a held hat, a loose cigarette, a level or averted gaze.
6. Keep the field empty: plain wall, mottled studio ground, quiet street; one chair at most.
7. Finish with period surface: soft diffused light, matte tonality, silver grain or flat lithographic planes — never digital gloss.

**Variation axes.** *Subject:* solo portrait, full-length fashion study, two friends, a small group, a poster-style doubled profile, a street or terrace snapshot, a leisure scene in trousers and jersey. *Composition:* bust in profile, three-quarter standing figure, seated dandy, paired figures, poster layout with type. *Lighting:* soft studio gradient, flat even poster light, overcast or open daylight. *Color handling:* pure black-and-white, sepia, olive-ochre print palette, or muted color with the red-lip accent. *Mood:* severe cool, wry amusement, dandyish play — all within restraint. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

**Avoid.** Corsets, cinched waists, hourglass shaping, crinolines or long trained skirts. Flapper ornament: beaded fringe, sequins, feather headbands, cascading pearl ropes, glittering dance dresses. Saturated color anywhere except the mouth; rainbows, pastels, jewel tones. Dominant Art Deco ornament — sunbursts, zigzags, chrome and gold filigree — as the image's subject rather than incidental background. Cabaret staging: fishnets, garters, satin stage costumes, smoky spotlight theatrics. Modern hair, makeup or eyewear; anachronistic objects and logos. Crowds, champagne, confetti, dance-floor energy. Soft-focus romantic sentimentality or sexualized posing. Busy prints, ruffles, lace, embroidery.

## Discrimination Cues

| Neighbor | Shared ground | Garçonne tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Flapper** | Same decade, bobbed hair, drop waists, liberated stance | Flapper is decorative and hedonistic: beads, fringe, sequins, feather bands, dance motion, party settings. Garçonne is severe, tailored, still; menswear fabric and line replace ornament. |
| **Art Deco** | Same era, geometric clarity | Deco is a decorative design language — sunbursts, zigzags, lacquer, gold, chrome. Garçonne is sartorial and austere; Deco motifs may sit in a background but never lead the image. |
| **Weimar Cabaret** | Shared androgyny (the Dietrich bridge), trousers and top hats | Cabaret is stage and nightclub: smoke, spotlights, stockings, garters, satin, decadent night mood. Garçonne is daylight or studio, wool and cotton, nonchalance instead of performance. |
| **Roaring Twenties** | The 1920s at large | Roaring Twenties imagery is collective celebration — crowds, champagne, charleston, brass. Garçonne is individual or small-group, French, intellectual, restrained. |

When in doubt, apply the subtraction test: if removing every ornament leaves a strong vertical line, a sharp bob and a white collar against an empty ground, the image is Garçonne; if removing ornament leaves nothing, it was one of the neighbors.
