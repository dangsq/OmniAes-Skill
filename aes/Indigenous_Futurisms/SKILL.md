# Indigenous Futurisms

## Overview

Indigenous Futurisms is a movement, not a single look: a plural ("s" deliberate) set of visual practices through which Indigenous artists imagine themselves in the future, the past and the folded space between. It spans painting, screenprint, photography, digital collage, beadwork and quillwork installation, fashion, ceramics, machinima, photogrammetry and film. What binds the visual output is an argument made in images: Indigenous peoples are not historical artifacts but authors of futurity, and ancestral knowledge — oral history, star knowledge, beadwork, land stewardship — is itself advanced technology. Works therefore refuse the sepia past-tense of ethnographic imagery and the empty-frontier conventions of mainstream science fiction at once, replacing them with regalia-clad figures under alien moons, overgrown ruins reclaimed as garden and home, and pop-culture icons (Yoda, Princess Leia, crashed star destroyers) repainted into Indigenous material culture with a confrontational, often humorous stare.

Because the movement insists that Indigenous cultures are not a monolith, the style's surface varies by nation and artist: formline-derived vector color fields from the Northwest Coast, Plains feather and ribbonwork, Pueblo adobe and ceramic geometry, Southwest turquoise and desert ochre, Métis floral beadwork and ribbon shirts. The unifying DNA is relational rather than chromatic: ancestral material + speculative frame + sovereign presence.

## Visual Identity

### Palette (a range, not a formula)

The infobox honesty — "key colours: varies by culture" — is itself a rule: palette authority belongs to the material culture of the nation being referenced, so no fixed scheme exists. Across the observed body of work, colors cluster into four bands, and a given piece typically combines one earth band with either the cosmic band or the ceremony band, plus optional pop accents:

| Band | Role | Anchor hexes |
|---|---|---|
| Earth & hide | ground, robes, dunes, hide lodges, adobe | tan `#D8B482`, ochre `#C08A3E`, red-earth sienna `#9A4A28`, formline black `#14100E` |
| Sky, water & stone | skies, rivers, turquoise ornament | teal `#2FA8A0`, bright cyan-teal `#3FB8C9`, deep teal `#14656B` |
| Ceremony saturation | ribbonwork, beadwork, regalia, quilt geometry | red `#C0392B`, orange `#E07020`, magenta `#D6489A`, purple `#6C4BA6`, yellow `#E8C020`, green `#6FBF44` |
| Cosmos | night skies, nebulae, space composites | indigo-black `#070B1E`, nebula violet `#2A1B4A`, star white `#F4F1E6`, moon orange `#D98A3A` |
| Metal & machine | space blankets, helmets, cables, scan ghosting | mylar silver `#C9CED6`, cable gray `#6E7480`, anaglyph cyan/red fringing `#35C8E0` / `#E03A3A` |

Handling varies widely and legitimately: flat matte acrylic fields with hard outlines and bold contour (painting and screenprint); formline-descended triads of black, red and teal with yellow accents; airbrushed smooth gradients on starfields; duotone or color-blocked pop photo manipulation (a single saturated skin tone against a hot ground); inverted-negative montages where regalia figures bleach to white against fire-orange horizons; and soft blue-hour ambient light in 3D-rendered environments. What never appears is a monochrome austerity or a desaturated "prestige" grade — even dusk scenes keep saturated ceremonial accents alive inside them.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how the texts document them

1. **Regalia and material culture as living technology.** Beadwork, quillwork, ribbon skirts and shirts, fringe, feather bustles and fans, hide robes, ceramics and woven textiles appear not as costume but as operative equipment: beaded panels with cables trailing from them like power leads, beadwork shields held up like interfaces, space-blanket dresses and helmeted figures with beaded visors. Craft is sci-fi props department.
2. **Decolonial land and regrown ruin.** Land is protagonist, not backdrop: rooftop gardens and geodesic domes on overgrown ruined high-rises with a broadcast tower on the skyline, desert homelands treated as lived-in rather than barren, pueblo dwellings defended and reframed as home, ice and shoreline as relation. The "post-apocalypse" is rendered as a present condition being lived through with continuity, not as extinction.
3. **Cosmos and star knowledge.** Starfields, crescent moons, ringed planets, comets and seeded night skies recur as navigation and kinship, alongside animal relatives rendered through Indigenous visual systems — a killer whale in formline-derived color fields beneath a stylized sun. Celestial bodies sit low and large over horizons, close enough to belong to the land's story.
4. **Critical pop-culture remix.** Recognizable science-fiction and Western iconography — Yoda in a feather headdress, Princess Leia in Hopi attire meeting the viewer's gaze with a firearm, sand people kneeling in a hide lodge, a fallen star destroyer half-buried in dunes — repainted to expose and reverse appropriation. The remix is argumentative, never fan service.
5. **Digital and machine media as self-representation.** Low-poly machinima figures in pale robes on glowing grid floors, web-born digital art, photogrammetric scans with red-cyan anaglyph fringing, composite photography with visible cut-out edges. The DIY digital artifact — polygon facets, scan ghosting, collage seams — is worn openly, echoing the movement's origin in personal computers and the internet as tools of self-representation.
6. **Non-linear time folded into one frame.** Ancestor and future occupant share a single image: regalia plus helmet, fringe plus firearm, oral-history iconography plus spacecraft. Tradition reads as active survival equipment, never as period dress.
7. **Presence and procession.** Figures appear counted and present: rows and lineups of regalia-clad people standing shoulder to shoulder, processional friezes against storm or fire skies, group portraits on flat colored grounds. Multiplicity itself is the statement against narratives of disappearance.
8. **Wit.** Frybread drifting in zero gravity, wry titles, deadpan stares: humor functions as survival technology and as a refusal of solemn ethnographic framing.

### Materials and textures

Hide grain and soft matte robe folds; satin ribbon sheen; glossy bead highlights and quill shimmer; feather barb softness against hard mylar crinkle; ceramic and adobe matte; gallery-white installation space with textile cords and cables running to the floor; acrylic flatness and silk-screen grain; airbrush smoothness; polygonal facets and scan-line artifacts; photographic grain and halftone speckle in montages. Juxtaposition is the texture rule: organic craft surfaces and machine surfaces touch directly, without transitional styling.

### Recurring subjects

Regalia-clad individuals and groups (often confronting the camera directly); women as protectors and knowledge-keepers; dancers and fashion figures in contemporary Indigenous design (sculptural black dresses, metallic cone dresses, ribbonwork gowns); animal kin (orca, bighorn and desert fauna); ruined-and-regrown cities; desert and shoreline homelands; spacecraft and celestial phenomena; beaded and textile objects installed as machines; pop-culture characters indigenized.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

- Frontal, centered single figures meeting the viewer's gaze — the anti-colonial-gaze portrait.
- Horizontal processional rows or friezes of standing figures, evenly spaced, full-length.
- Horizon-dominated landscapes with a large celestial body, hoop or disc craft hovering low in the sky band.
- Collage and composite logic: cut-out edges, layered planes, handwritten annotation scrawled across a corner, patterned border bands framing the field.
- Installation documentation: objects on white walls with cables, cords or shadows completing the circuit.
- Lighting ranges from completely flat graphic light (painting, screenprint) to studio-composite key light against cosmic backdrops, soft blue-hour ambient in rendered environments, pinpoint starlight, and high-contrast storm or fire backlight in montages. Any of these is valid; what persists is legibility of the regalia and the land relation under whatever light is chosen.

## Mood & Values

Sovereign presence: "we are still here, and we will be there." Resilience without martyrdom; defiance without dystopian cynicism; tenderness toward land, kin and animals; ceremonial seriousness fused with playfulness and joke-telling. The emotional register is relational — humans as one strand in a web of beings — so even solitary figures read as accompanied by ancestors, animals and stars. Hope here is not utopian gloss but practiced continuity: gardens on ruins, language in the mouth of a machine-age figure, beadwork wired into the grid.

## Era & Origins

Coined and theorized by Grace Dillon (Anishinaabe), most visibly through the 2012 anthology *Walking the Clouds: An Anthology of Indigenous Science Fiction*, with practice preceding the term: Skawennati's CyberPowWow website (1997–2004) as an early platform for Indigenous digital art, followed by her TimeTraveller™ machinima; the movement flowers across the 2000s to the present in painting, photography, fashion, ceramics, film (*Biidaaban: First Light*, 2016), games and literature. It reacts against three exclusions: the confinement of Indigenous peoples to the historical past, the digital divide and portrayal as pre-technological, and science fiction's inherited tropes of conquest, terra nullius and the lone colonizer-hero. Its philosophical core is *biskaabiiyang*, "returning to ourselves": discarding colonial psychological baggage and recovering ancestral tradition as future-making technology in a post–Native Apocalypse world that is already, historically, the present.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Fold time: put ancestral material culture and speculative technology in the same frame with no costume seam — beadwork and cables, fringe and helmets, ribbonwork and starfields.
2. Treat craft as technology: render beadwork, quillwork, ribbonwork and ceramics with the functional seriousness a sci-fi props department gives circuitry.
3. Make land a protagonist: regrown ruins, rooftop gardens over skylines, desert and shore as homeland; never an empty stage.
4. Place the cosmos in relation: stars, moons, comets and animal kin as companions and navigation, low and large in the sky, not as conquest scenery.
5. Remix pop culture critically when remixing at all: indigenized icons should confront the viewer and reverse the appropriation, not decorate.
6. Keep the plural: let palette and iconography follow the specific nation's material culture you are invoking; never blend nations into a generic "tribal" look.
7. Allow wit: a deadpan stare, a floating frybread, a wry title are style-correct survival humor.
8. Insist on presence: figures are counted, lined up, named, alive in the future tense.

**Variation axes.** Subject: single portrait, procession, group portrait, landscape with hovering craft or celestial body, installed object, still life of regalia-as-machine, comic panel, machinima frame, fashion shot, composite cosmos scene. Composition: frontal center, frieze row, low horizon with big sky, collage grid with borders and annotation, gallery wall. Lighting: flat graphic, studio composite, blue-hour ambient, starfield pinpoint, storm or fire backlight, inverted negative. Color handling: flat saturated fields, formline triad, duotone pop block, naturalistic dusk with ceremonial accents, bleached montage. Mood: defiant, tender, ceremonial, playful, mournful-but-continuous. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a rooftop garden with no figures, a beadwork panel with cables, or a lone dancer under a comet all read as Indigenous Futurisms if the relational and temporal folds are present.

**Avoid.**
- Sepia, ethnographic, past-tense documentary framing that confines Indigenous people to history — the single most style-breaking choice.
- Empty frontier, terra nullius landscapes, lone astronaut planting a flag, heroic colonizer narratives.
- Neon-rain megacity cynicism, corporate chrome and hacker noir without Indigenous material culture or land relation (cyberpunk drift).
- Generic green-tech utopian optimism with no regalia, no sovereignty content, no ruin-memory (solarpunk drift).
- Chrome-and-gold funk glamour, space-diaspora exodus iconography imported wholesale (Afrofuturist signatures standing in for regalia).
- Mid-century atomic-age chrome, rayguns and diner nostalgia played straight (retrofuturism without critical remix).
- Oil-money desert skyscraper dystopia (Gulf Futurism) mistaken for ancestral desert homeland.
- Pan-Indigenous mashups: mixing distinct nations' regalia into one generic costume, or feather headdresses as fashion props on unrelated subjects.
- Monochrome minimalism, corporate flat vector blandness, extinction-and-hopelessness narratives; regalia displayed as dead museum artifact with no living circuit around it.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Against Afrofuturism:** both use cosmos as liberation space, but Indigenous Futurisms grounds itself in land-back and ruin-regrowth, in beadwork/quillwork/ribbonwork/fringe as the futuristic material, and in non-linear time as ancestral return; Afrofuturism's signature chrome-gold-purple funk glamour, diasporic exodus mythos and jazz-electronic iconography are absent or replaced.
- **Against Solarpunk:** shared overgrown-city imagery (ruins reclaimed by gardens), but Indigenous Futurisms carries sovereignty, apocalypse-as-history and regalia; solarpunk's seamless eco-tech optimism, artisanal glass-and-leaf styling and absence of Indigenous material politics mark the difference.
- **Against Cyberpunk:** cyberpunk's rain-slick neon, corporate dystopia and lone antihero oppose this style's relational worldview, communal processions, daylight deserts and craft-as-tech; even its machinima low-poly looks DIY-communal, not corporate-gothic.
- **Against Gulf Futurism:** desert plus skyscraper appears in both, but Gulf Futurism's oil-capital wealth aesthetics, expatriate labor anxieties and hypermodern Gulf skylines lack regalia, star knowledge and decolonial land framing.
- **Against Retrofuturism:** retrofuturism nostalgically reproduces a past era's future (atomic chrome, rayguns); Indigenous Futurism may quote pop-culture futures (Star Wars) but only as critical appropriation commentary inside a non-linear time fold, never as period nostalgia.

**Quick recognition checklist:** regalia or beadwork functioning as technology + a speculative frame (cosmos, ruin-regrowth, machine media) + sovereign frontal presence + palette drawn from a specific nation's material culture + a fold of ancestral and future time in one image. Two or three of these, held together with wit and land-relation, is enough for the style to read.
