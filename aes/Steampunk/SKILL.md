# Steampunk

Steampunk imagines a world where steam power never lost: an anachronistic or alternate-history Victorian era in which 19th-century industrial machinery grew into sophisticated technology without ever becoming sleek, sealed or opaque. It is simultaneously a literary retrofuturism (Verne and Wells as ancestors, K.W. Jeter's 1987 coinage, Gibson & Sterling's *The Difference Engine* as consolidation) and a hands-on maker subculture, so its images split between painted speculative worlds and photographed handmade props — both obeying the same material logic. The aesthetic pairs ornate Victorian elegance with intricate, *visible* mechanism: the machine must be readable on the surface, celebrated rather than hidden.

## Visual Identity

**Color palette (rule, not recipe).** The dominant field is warm metal and aged organic matter: bronze, copper, brass and brown, keyed toward golden metallic highlights. Build images on a base of umber and tan browns (`#4E3620`–`#6B4A2F`), mid-tone copper and bronze (`#B87333`, `#CD7F32`), and brass gold (`#C9A227`–`#B5A642`), lifted by sepia creams and parchment (`#E8DCC4`, `#D2B48C`) for skies, paper and daylight. Accents stay period-plausible: gaslight amber (`#E8A33D`), verdigris and oil-lamp green (`#437B6A`–`#5F8D7E`), oxblood and rust red (`#7B3B2A`), and — in the foggy, dusk-end of the range — desaturated grey-blues (`#8A93A0`, `#5C6670`) for mist, overcast sky and shadowed iron. Shadows go deep umber (`#2B1D12`), never pure black-with-neon. Any single image may sit anywhere on the span from bright sepia daylight to gloomy gaslit night, but warm metal tones should remain the identifying constant.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.**
1. **Exposed gears, cogs and clockwork mechanisms** — the style's first named motif; mechanism worn on the outside of objects and costumes.
2. **Steam infrastructure** — pipes, boilers, valves, vents and the smoke or steam they emit; the genre's namesake power source.
3. **Analog timekeeping and measurement** — clocks, dials, gauges, pocket watches, needle instruments; no digital readouts.
4. **Steam-powered vehicles and machines** — airships and flying machines above all, plus locomotives, automatons and workshop contraptions.
5. **Victorian/Edwardian fashion customized with tech** — waistcoats, suits, top hats, corsets, bustles, long dresses, overlaid with brass goggles, pocket watches, leather harnesses and cog-and-gear jewelry.
6. **The material quartet** — brass, copper, polished wood, leather, plus glass (lenses, domes, greenhouse roofs) and riveted iron.
7. **Victorian architectural space** — iron-and-glass train sheds and exhibition halls, arcades, cluttered studies and laboratories, spired and domed city skylines.

**Materials and textures.** Patina over polish: tarnished brass, oxidized copper, oiled leather with creases and stitching, grained and turned wood, riveted and seamed plate, canvas and doped fabric for wings and sails, etched glass. Surfaces show hand-work — rivets, welds, patches, chains, bolt heads — consistent with the DIY ethos. Plastic, chrome plating, rubberized matte synthetics and seamless molded surfaces are absent by definition.

**Recurring subjects.** Costumed figures (portrait or in scene); flying machines and airships in sepia sky; vast glazed interiors with small silhouetted figures; cluttered inventor workshops and instrument panels; clockwork creatures and automatons; macro still-lifes of gadgets resting on diagrams or parchment; romanticized industrial cityscapes with smoke plumes and distant balloons.

**Composition and lighting tendencies.** Light is directional and warm: pools of lamplight in dark cluttered rooms, furnace or dial glow, and characteristic diagonal shafts of daylight cutting through glass roofs, windows or haze. Steam, smoke and fog soften edges and justify the sepia atmosphere. Composition ranges from intimate object close-ups and frontal costume portraits against plain or dark grounds, to low-angle heroics of machines, to wide establishing vistas where architecture and sky dwarf the figures. Clutter is virtuous: benches, walls and machines accumulate detail rather than resting on empty negative space.

## Mood & Values

The infobox values — craftsmanship, optimism, ingenuity, self-sufficiency, eclecticism — are the emotional contract. Steampunk images feel *made*, not manufactured: every gadget implies a tinkerer. The mood is romantic nostalgia with a can-do sparkle, curious and adventurous rather than dystopian; even the gloomy, fog-bound variants read as moody romance, not despair. The style's relationship to history is affectionate fiction, consciously filtered through film and modern sensibility rather than accuracy, and its community spirit favors politeness, creativity and individuality over purity — so eclectic hybrid costumes and whimsical inventions are in-genre.

## Era & Origins

Literary seeds lie in 19th-century scientific romance (Verne, Wells). The visual aesthetic emerged mid-20th century in film and television romanticizing a Victorian future (the 1960s *The Wild, Wild West* as early exemplar); the term was coined jokingly by K.W. Jeter in a 1987 letter to *Locus Magazine*, solidified by *The Difference Engine* (1990), and exploded from the mid-2000s into a full subculture of fashion, crafting, music and events. Steampunk defines itself *against* the present: a counterculture rejecting sleek, minimalist, incomprehensible and disposable modern technology in favor of ornate, durable, mechanically transparent design.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
- Anchor the silhouette in the Victorian/Edwardian era, then break it with anachronistic analog technology.
- Keep mechanism exposed and legible: gears, pipes, gauges and linkages on the surface, never sealed in a shell.
- Honor the material list: brass, copper, polished wood, leather, glass, riveted iron, canvas.
- Ornament coexists with function: engraving, filigree, turned wood and decorative ironwork alongside bolts and boilers.
- Light with period sources: gaslamps, furnace glow, daylight shafts through glazing; let steam or fog diffuse it.
- Show wear and hand-work: patina, stitching, rivets, patches, soot at the edges — craftsmanship, not factory finish.

**Variation axes.** Subject may be fashion portrait, vehicle, interior, cityscape, automaton, gadget still-life or collage; composition may run macro to epic vista; lighting may run bright sepia daylight to gaslit chiaroscuro to foggy grey-blue dusk; color handling may be saturated brass-rich or desaturated and misty; mood may be whimsical-optimistic or moody-industrial; medium may be photography, painterly concept art or clean render. A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image — a brass-and-walnut sewing machine in a sunlit parlor is as valid as an airship over a domed city.

**Avoid.** Plastics and modern synthetics; chrome streamlining and seamless minimalist surfaces; neon colors, LEDs, holograms, digital screens and keyboards; internal-combustion or jet-age forms (cars, airplanes with monoplanes of aluminum skin, rockets); modern skyscrapers, signage and infrastructure; pure cyberpunk rain-and-neon night; medieval or Renaissance settings with clockwork but no steam (that is clockpunk); 1920s–30s deco zigzags and bakelite as the dominant language (decopunk); gunmetal wartime machinery and oil grime as the dominant language (dieselpunk); post-apocalyptic rust-wasteland decay that erases craftsmanship and optimism.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Clockpunk:** earlier (Renaissance–Baroque) spring-and-gear technology in wood, iron and gold; no boilers, steam vents or soot, no Victorian fashion. Steampunk always implies steam power and a 19th-century silhouette.
- **Cyberpunk:** night, rain, neon cyan/magenta, chrome, digital interfaces, corporate dystopia. Steampunk is warm, analog, daylight-or-gaslit, and optimistic; its "punk" is DIY craft, not hacker despair.
- **Dark Cabaret:** Weimar-cabaret theatricality — black/red/stripes, corsets with fishnets, stage makeup and proscenium lighting — with little or no machinery. Steampunk costuming carries goggles, gears and leather tooling, and its settings are workshops, sheds and skies, not stages.
- **Decopunk:** 1920s–30s retrofuturism in polished chrome, bakelite, symmetrical zigzag geometry and streamlined glamour. Steampunk is a century older in silhouette, cluttered rather than streamlined, brass and wood rather than chrome.
- **Dieselpunk:** 1920s–50s internal-combustion world of steel, oil, gunmetal grey, olive drab and angular military-industrial mass. Steampunk's metals are copper-toned, its power source visibly steam, its ornament Victorian rather than wartime-utilitarian.
