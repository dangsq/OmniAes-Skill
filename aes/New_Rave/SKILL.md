# New Rave

New Rave was a short-lived, massively hyped British music scene and subculture that peaked between 2006 and 2008, born in London's New Cross warehouse-party circuit and amplified by NME, Angular Records and MySpace. The name is a press-coined pun on "New Wave": the music was indie rock and dance-punk bolted to synthesizers and sequenced beats, and the look was indie fashion bolted to the costume of 1990s rave. That double identity is the core of the aesthetic — glowsticks and neon paint worn over skinny jeans and band t-shirts — and it extends into graphics built from high-contrast digital manipulation, 8-bit abstraction, vector psychedelia and aggressive CMYK color. The scene knew it was partly a joke ("a joke that got out of hand," per its own founders), and that self-aware, garish, deliberately tasteless playfulness is as diagnostic as any single object.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette

The rule is fluorescent clash on a crushed ground: at least two neon hues at maximum saturation, set against either crushed black or one flat chroma-key field. Skin is not sacred — solarization recolors faces into violet, cyan or yellow, turning people into graphic elements.

- Fluorescent magenta / fuchsia: `#FF00CC` – `#FF2D95`
- Acid lime / chartreuse: `#CCFF00` – `#39FF14`
- Electric cyan: `#00FFFF` – `#00E5FF`
- Highlighter yellow: `#FFFF00` – `#FFE600`
- Ultraviolet / purple: `#7F00FF` – `#9933FF`
- Signal orange: `#FF6600` – `#FF9900`
- Key black, crushed: `#000000` – `#0A0A0A`
- Chroma white / paper: `#FFFFFF`

Acceptable handling ranges from full CMYK pile-up (magenta + lime + cyan + yellow simultaneously) to harsh duotones (green-on-black, magenta-on-yellow, cyan-on-magenta). What never varies: saturation is clipped, contrast is brutal, and no hue is allowed to sit at pastel or earth strength.

### Signature motifs and objects (ranked by how centrally the culture documents them)

1. **Glowsticks and "nu-glow" hardware** — bracelets, necklaces, tubes, LED-integrated gear; the single most cited signifier, worn stacked on wrists and raised arms.
2. **Neon / fluorescent face and body paint** — streaks, blocks and smears of Day-Glo across cheeks and foreheads.
3. **The indie base layer** — skinny jeans, band t-shirts, vibrant hoodies and tracksuits; rave props are applied *on top of* ordinary indie clothes.
4. **Whistles** — documented as a key motif of the scene's live shows (though they surface less often in surviving imagery than glowsticks and paint).
5. **Psychedelic graphic imagery, digital-native** — pixelated 8-bit sprites, geometric lattices, mirrored kaleidoscopic tunnels, checkerboards, lightning bolts, stars, polka-dot fields; plus Vectordelia: clean undulating vector lines recalling early computer screensavers and Op Art.
6. **Eyewear** — Wayfarer-style sunglasses in neon acetate, shutter shades, clear plastic frames.
7. **Oversized slogan tees and Day-Glo hoodies** — bold high-contrast, often rhyming neon slogans; "bed sheet hoodies" printed with 1980s cartoon motifs.
8. **Light-refracting synthetics** — lamé leggings, metallic fabrics, silk-screen psychedelic prints reworking 1990s rave and gaming iconography (the Cassette Playa blueprint).
9. **Clashing footwear** — Nikes and high-top sneakers in mismatched primary colors; striped and opaque neon tights.
10. **DIY found-object props** — cardboard cutouts, baby toys, repurposed household textiles, handmade cardboard crowns and chains.
11. **Typography** — bold sans-serif lettering with heavy drop shadows or neon-glow outer strokes, used on flyers, tees and video titles.

### Materials and textures

Synthetic sheen over natural fiber: lamé and metallic foil prints, silk-screened cotton jersey, neon acetate plastic, plastic glowsticks and bead chains, opaque nylon tights. The *image* texture is digital, not filmic: pixelation, compression artifacts, solarized highlights, strobe-cut motion, GIF-like repeating animation, flat vector fills. Where paint appears on skin it sits matte and streaky against glossy synthetic cloth.

### Recurring subjects

Groups of young people — bands, DJ crews, club kids — posed in lineup or huddle; dancers mid-jump with arms raised; crowd hands stacked with glowstick bracelets; solo performers reduced to silhouettes; deadpan lookbook or runway figures in printed sweatshirts; cartoon mascots and collaged vector graphics standing in for people entirely. Subjects are typically thin, young, British-club-coded, and styled with a masculine-feminine balance: the same paint, leggings and neon worn across genders.

### Composition and lighting

Two dominant modes, freely mixable:

- **Flash snapshot**: harsh direct on-camera flash in a dark club or against a saturated flat wall or grey studio backdrop; subjects face the camera, grinning or deadpan; blacks crush to nothing behind them; highlights blow out on foreheads and plastic.
- **Flattened graphic space**: chroma-keyed single-color fields (yellow, magenta, cyan) with subjects cut out and pasted in; silhouettes centered inside radiating kaleidoscopic tunnels or dashed geometric frames; sticker-collage chaos filling the frame edge to edge.

In both modes depth is denied: no atmospheric perspective, no motivated light source, no realistic falloff. Motion imagery strobe-cuts to high-BPM rhythm. Lo-fi maximalism is the default density — empty space only reads as deliberate flat chroma field, never as tasteful restraint.

## Mood & Values

Hedonistic, chaotic, inclusive and loudly DIY. New Rave valued visibility in low-light clubs, sensory overload over realism, and artificiality as honesty: it never pretended to be organic. Its playfulness carries an ironic wink — the scene half-knew it was a manufactured fad and leaned into the costume-party garishness anyway. It was a reaction against seriousness: anti-pretension, anti-muted-denim, pro-anyone-can-do-this-with-surplus-gear-and-neon-dye. Underneath the gloss sits genuine inclusivity — warehouse nights that welcomed kids alienated by the po-faced indie scene — and the melancholy of a fad that burned bright, got retail-codified ("neon and big cartoon chains"), and was abandoned by its founders within two years.

## Era & Origins

Mid-2000s United Kingdom, specifically London's New Cross scene, c. 2006–2008. Coined by Angular Records and NME as a pun on New Wave; popularized by Klaxons and peers (Late of the Pier, Trash Fashion, New Young Pony Club, CSS, Shitdisco, Hadouken!). It reacted against the stagnant, overly serious garage-rock revival of The Libertines and contemporaries, and against the muted denim-and-leather "vagabond" indie look, by appropriating signifiers of 1980s Madchester and 1990s acid-house rave. Home turf: warehouse parties and clubs like Foreign, Chalk and Troubled Minds; distribution: NME, MySpace, E4's *Skins*. By 2008 commercialization and critical backlash had toxified the brand; founding acts fled fluorescence (monochrome, or softer psychedelia), which is itself a useful dating cue — saturated New Rave visuals belong to 2006–2008.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles**

1. *Neon clash rule*: at least two fluorescent hues at clipped saturation; never let one muted tone anchor the frame.
2. *Indie-under-rave rule*: rave props (glowsticks, paint, whistles) layered over an indie base (band tee, skinny jeans, hoodie) — costume over everyday clothes.
3. *Digital grit rule*: crushed blacks, blown highlights, solarized skin, compression-era artifacting; the look of a 2006 digital compact or low-res video still, never analog film warmth.
4. *Flat space rule*: chroma-key fields, pattern fields, silhouettes as graphic shapes; refuse realistic depth and motivated lighting.
5. *Maximalist fill rule*: pack the frame with pattern, sprites, stickers, slogans, clashing prints; negative space only as flat color.
6. *DIY chaos rule*: handmade props and deliberate tastelessness beat polish; joy and irony beat cool.

**Variation axes** — a correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image.

- *Subject*: band lineup, club crowd, solo dancer, lookbook model, flyer or poster design, vector mascot illustration, video still, bedroom DIY portrait, runway shot.
- *Composition*: symmetrical group lineup, tight huddle, raised-arm crowd, centered silhouette in a radial tunnel, collage grid, flat chroma portrait, all-over pattern field with no figure at all.
- *Lighting*: direct flash in darkness; flat even studio/chroma light; or wholly self-luminous graphic space with no light source implied.
- *Color handling*: full CMYK pile-up, harsh two-color solarization, or single neon on crushed black — fluorescence and brutal contrast are the constants.
- *Mood*: euphoric chaos, deadpan ironic posing, cartoonish aggression, affectionate mockery.

**Avoid**

- Muted or earthy palettes: beige, olive, rust, heritage indigo, brown leather, sepia, pastels.
- Organic 1960s psychedelia: watercolor swirls, paisley, hand-painted softness, warm mandala glow.
- Soft natural light, golden hour, window light, gentle film grain, shallow cinematic depth of field.
- Monochrome minimalism, chrome sleekness, or generous tasteful negative space.
- Pure 1990s rave authenticity without the indie layer or digital manipulation: smiley-face-only iconography, baggy white-label sportswear, fog-and-laser warehouse realism.
- Indie Sleaze decay: cigarettes, smeared mascara, grime, exhausted murk.
- Modern flat-design smoothness: gradient meshes, clean 4K renders, millennial pastel UI.
- Luxury fashion polish presented straight; even silk-print garments must sit inside neon maximalist clash.
