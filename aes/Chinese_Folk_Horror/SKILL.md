# Chinese Folk Horror (中式民俗恐怖 / Minsu Horror)

Chinese Folk Horror is the Chinese branch of folk horror: dread manufactured not from monsters but from **custom** — funeral rites, marriage rites, ancestral ethics, regional taboos — turned wrong. It grew out of centuries of zhiguai (志怪) strange-tale literature and 1990s Hong Kong/Taiwan horror cinema, and became a recognizable visual genre in the 2020s through mainland indie horror games (PAGUI, Paper Dolls, Firework, the Paper Bride series, Mirages) circulated on Steam, Bilibili and Douyin. Because mainland policy bars literal ghosts from screen, the style is fundamentally **implicational**: the supernatural is suggested by an extra pair of red shoes, a second wedding cup, a paper figurine facing the door. Visually it lives in dim rural spaces — deserted villages, old mansions, ancestral halls, foggy graveyards, mountain temples — furnished with decayed ritual objects.

## Visual Identity

### Color palette: the red–black–white triad

The genre's declared key colours are red, black and white, and image evidence confirms the rule: **one saturated ritual hue (almost always red; rarely a sickly green) set against a desaturated field of soot black, earth brown and mourning-paper white.** Red never behaves like a background fill; it behaves like an object — a hood, a rope, a candle glow, a line of display type — burning inside darkness. White appears as pallor: paper, silk mourning cloth, porcelain skin. A ritual gold-yellow (talisman paper, embroidered thread, aged brass) is the licensed fourth note.

### Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them

1. **Funeral–marriage fusion (core).** Red bridal hoods and embroidered wedding silk used funerally: bound or gagged brides, closed eyes, wedding halls dressed like mourning halls, red candles in pairs. The genre's emblematic image is a wedding that is also a funeral.
2. **Dim rural space (core).** Deserted villages, old mansions, foggy nights, graveyards, mountain temples; adobe rooms, timber ancestral halls (祠堂), opera stages, ancient wells.
3. **Ritual objects (core object list).** Paper figurines (zhi ren) with painted cheeks; joss paper (纸钱), loose or half-burnt; incense sticks and burners; Taoist talismans and peach-wood swords; red embroidered shoes; family trees and ancestral tablets.
4. **Talismanic script and hanging paper.** Yellow paper strips with cinnabar characters pinned to beams and doors; red couplets gone black with age.
5. **Divination and mechanism hardware (text-adjacent, strongly image-supported).** Bagua and yin-yang discs, feng-shui compasses, carved wooden puzzle locks, altar cabinets — the genre's games are puzzle games, so mechanism close-ups are a recurring poster subject.
6. **Red cord, bells, tassels, birdcages, oil lamps (image-supported).** Small domestic-ritual props that clutter frames and catch the single light source.
7. **Poster typography (medium convention).** Vertical promo layouts with heavy red Chinese display type, a white sub-line, sometimes a ghosted duplicate line — part of the genre's public face, optional in pure illustration.

### Materials and textures

Aged paper (joss paper curling at the edges, talisman strips), rough adobe and mildewed plaster, dark lacquered and raw wood grain (beams, coffins, shrine cabinets), oxidized bronze and worn stone (incense burners, puzzle discs), silk and gold-thread embroidery, candle wax and soot, ash, dust motes in lamplight, fog, peeling paint, and — in rendered scenes — low-light film grain and sensor noise. Nothing is new; everything carries handling wear.

### Recurring subjects

Brides and grooms in ritual dress, frequently restrained, gagged, veiled or eyes-closed; paper effigies standing in for people; Taoist priests and fake mystics; villagers in Republic-era to late-20th-century rural clothing; dolls and child figures; implied corpses and coffins (shown as cloth, lid, or absence); investigators of the modern day entering old spaces with a flashlight. Faces are pale, features soft, expression suspended between grief and blankness.

### Composition and lighting tendencies

- **Ritual symmetry broken once:** altars, shrines and wedding halls framed dead-center with paired candles and couplets, then one wrong element — an open door, an extra cup, a missing figurine.
- **Claustrophobic centered portraits:** head-and-shoulders, face partly obscured by veil, cloth, hair or paper; eyes closed or hollow.
- **Environmental wides at human-minus scale:** fogged lanes, courtyards, graveyards where architecture dwarfs the figure.
- **Object still-lifes and mechanism close-ups:** discs, locks, shrine interiors filling the frame.
- **Framing through architecture:** doorways, lattice windows and moon gates used as prosceniums.
- **Lighting:** a single practical source — oil lamp, candle pair, lantern, flashlight — producing chiaroscuro with crushed blacks, red glow bouncing off silk and skin, fog diffusing the beam, heavy vignettes. Secondary fills are cold moonlight through lattice or, rarely, a monochrome wash: phosphor green for night/underworld beats, ash grey for overcast daylight despair. Even, flat, high-key light does not exist in this style.

### Medium

Painterly semi-real digital painting (game key art and promo illustration) with soft brushwork and edges dissolved into shadow; grainy low-light 3D render aesthetics imitating game screenshots; poster graphics combining painted scenes with display type. Clean vector flatness and glossy photorealism both read as outside the style.

## Mood & Values

The genre's dread is moral and domestic: Buddhist reincarnation and karmic retribution, amends for past misdeeds, revenge and settled old scores; romance entwined with death; the blurry boundary between living and dead. Beneath the supernatural furniture it is a critical realism — son preference, arranged marriage, superstitious healing, fraud by fake mystics, village-wide apathy and blind conformity. The true monster is human coldness and ignorance; the ghost is only the symptom. Consequently the mood is mournful, restrained, suspenseful: implication over viscera, silence over scream, tragedy over terror.

## Era & Origins

Literary roots run from Spring-and-Autumn-through-Qing zhiguai strange tales to Republic-era critical horror such as Lu Xun's *Diary of a Madman*; the 1980s Hong Kong jiangshi boom supplied talismans, corpse escort and feng shui but blended them with comedy and action; the 1990s saw mature Hong Kong and Taiwan folk-horror film (*The Beheaded 1000*, later *The Rope Curse*, *Incantation*). Mainland censorship's ban on on-screen ghosts pushed the mode toward psychological horror, suspense and dark fantasy, and the 2019–2020s indie game wave (PAGUI, Paper Dolls, Firework, Paper Bride, Mirages) fixed the current visual grammar: painted ritual iconography, dim interiors, puzzle mechanisms, red-on-black promo type.

## Generation Guidance

**Principles.**
1. Start from a custom, not a creature: the frame should read as a rite interrupted, misperformed or repeated after death.
2. Spend saturation only on ritual red (or, rarely, phosphor green); desaturate everything else into soot, earth and paper-white.
3. Light with one dim practical source; let shadow swallow the frame edges and fog soften the beam.
4. Age every surface: paper curls, plaster mildews, silk frays, brass oxidizes.
5. Horrify by implication: an empty chair, half-burnt joss paper, a second bridal shoe — never a confirmed monster.
6. Use authentic ritual vocabulary: cinnabar-scripted yellow talismans, paper effigies, incense counts, coffin orientation, inverted or faded double-happiness characters.
7. If typography is present, it is Chinese display type in red with a white sub-line, poster-style — never dripping Latin horror lettering.

**Variation axes.** Subject may range across brides and grooms, paper figurines, priests and mystics, villagers, dolls, object-only still-lifes, architecture-only scenes (well, opera stage, graveyard, ancestral hall), mechanism puzzles, or modern investigators inside old spaces; period costume may run from Republic era to 1990s rural. Composition may be poster-with-type, centered portrait, wide environmental, framed-through-doorway, or macro object study. Lighting may be candle-warm red-gold, cold lattice moonlight, green phosphor wash, ash-grey overcast, or near-total black with one lamp. Color handling may run full triad, near-monochrome with a single red accent, or desaturated grey with red type. Mood may span grief, quiet uncanny, tragic romance, suspense, or critical realism. Medium may be painterly key art, grainy render, or poster graphic. **A correct image can use any subject within this range; it should echo the style's rules, not any reference image** — a fogged well courtyard with no figure at all is as valid as a veiled bride.

**Avoid.**
- Generic Western horror kit: jump-scare monsters, gore and blood splatter, slashed killers, zombies, clowns, Ouija boards, crosses, churches.
- Japanese horror shorthand as the sole cue: Shinto shrines, torii, tatami and shoji, ofuda-shaped charms; the well-woman cliché standing in for Chinese ritual vocabulary.
- Chinese Dreamcore cheer: bright nostalgic saturation, liminal malls and classrooms, playful surrealism, vaporwave pastel haze.
- Wuxia/xianxia gloss: heroic rim-light, clean palaces, teal-orange grading, flawless costume-drama silk.
- Jiangshi comedy: hopping vampires, slapstick Taoist priests — the talismans may stay, the joke may not.
- Modern sleekness: glass, neon, clean interiors, smartphones as set dressing (modern props only as intruder tools like flashlights).
- Flat, even, high-key daylight; saturated multi-hue palettes; celebratory red used joyfully with nothing wrong in the frame.
- Confirmed on-screen ghosts doing the scaring; the style's censorship-shaped grammar demands suggestion.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Vs Chinese Dreamcore:** both mine old Chinese objects and memory, but Dreamcore is nostalgic-liminal and bright or pastel-hazy — empty malls, classrooms, CRT glow, playful uncanny — with no ritual or funerary vocabulary. Folk Horror always carries rite and dread: talismans, joss paper, bridal red in darkness. If the frame feels like a fond, eerie childhood recollection rather than a custom gone wrong, it is Dreamcore.
- **Vs (Western) Folk Horror:** the Western mode dreadizes open rural pagan Europe — wheat fields, stone circles, harvest crowns, daylight horror, Christian/pagan friction — in earthy beige-green palettes. Chinese Folk Horror is claustrophobic and indoor-ritual: ancestral halls, paper effigies, ancestor ethics, the red–black–white triad, candlelit chiaroscuro instead of sunlit fields.
- **Vs Horror (general):** general horror leans on monsters, gore, jump-scares and cold desaturated or high-contrast lighting. Chinese Folk Horror is psychological and custom-bound: saturated ritual red objects in soot darkness, implication over viscera, grief over shock, and a moral frame of karma and social critique.
- **Vs Jiangshi Horror (nearest sibling):** jiangshi fiction shares talismans, corpse escort and feng shui but adds hopping Qing-official corpses and comedy-action energy. Strip the comedy and the hop, keep the mournful rite, and you are in Folk Horror.
