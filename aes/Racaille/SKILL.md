# Racaille

Racaille — from the French pejorative for "scum," used in the invariable singular *la racaille* — is the street style of French housing-estate (*cité*) youth, born in the 1990s–2000s and still current: today's carriers are nicknamed «Lacoste TN» after the uniform's two anchor brands, or «les gars de cité», the guys from the hood. It is a working-class fringe style that answers insult with pride: the infobox values are French pride and debauchery, and the look mixes utilitarian sportswear with one deliberate signal of expensive taste (a coated-canvas bag, a monogram cap) worn without apology. Its soundtrack is French rap — Jul, PNL, Niska, Booba, Gazo, Koba LaD, Ninho, Damso and peers — and its daily life is hookah smoke, football on a screen, flexing about money and women, and talking too loud at the back of the bus.

## Visual Identity

**Palette rule.** A monochrome sportswear base carrying at most one loud accent. Blacks dominate: #0B0B0D–#26262B on tracksuits, puffers, caps, joggers. Whites #EDEDEA–#FFFFFF appear as crew socks, t-shirts, hoodies and sneaker midsoles. Greys #6E6E73–#B9B9BD come from the environment — concrete ledges, brick mortar, overcast sky, plaster hallways. Blues run navy #1B2440–#2A3A66 on hoodies and track tops. The sanctioned accents are red #B3202C–#D93025 as a trouser side-stripe or jersey lettering, and rarely a volt/neon tee (#C8F31D) as a single shock item. Beige-brown checkerboard or monogram canvas (#8A6F4D–#C9B08A) is permitted only on bags and caps, never as clothing field. An image reading as two or more competing color blocks has left the style.

**Signature motifs, ranked by how firmly the text documents them.** (1) Nike TN sneakers — the Air Max Plus with its ribbed synthetic cage and metallic swoosh; the style literally borrows its nickname from them. (2) Lacoste cap and Lacoste sportswear, crocodile logo visible; bucket hat as the accepted variant, cap often worn backwards. (3) Tracksuit sets, tapered joggers or tight jeans. (4) Bum bags and cross-body satchels, typically coated-canvas checkerboard or monogram. (5) Pants tucked into socks — white crew socks pulled high, often striped at the cuff, swallowing the jogger hem. (6) Phones on chains, handies in hand. (7) Crew grouping: two to eight bodies clustered, arms over shoulders, crouching in front of standing friends. (8) Football jerseys as an occasional top, consistent with the football-watching culture. Sneaker-only close-ups — shoe rested on a metal railing, sock cuff framed — are a canonical sub-format.

**Materials and textures.** Polyester tricot with a faint sheen; matte quilted nylon puffers; ribbed cotton sock; glossy coated canvas; rubberized sneaker cages against mesh; and as backdrop, raw concrete, painted brick, galvanized railings, stairwell metalwork, car interiors.

**Recurring subjects.** Young men from teens to late twenties, solo or in crews; back-of-head cap portraits; occupants shot from inside a car at night; courtyard gatherings that may include a leashed dog. Women appear only marginally in the evidence; treat the style as masculine-coded group culture while allowing mixed groups.

**Composition and lighting.** Candid phone-snapshot grammar: direct on-camera flash at night burning the foreground white-hot against a dark falloff of trees, parked cars and lit windows; flat overcast daylight; dim fluorescent hallways and stairwells. Bodies sit splayed on concrete ledges, lean on railings, crouch in tight clusters, or stare deadpan past the lens. Low-angle hero framing for shoes. Slight motion blur, compression artifacts and overlaid handwritten-script tag watermarks (repost culture) are authentic medium traits, optional but characteristic.

## Mood & Values

Defiant nonchalance. The look says: we are what you call scum, and we dress better than you on half your money. Pride in France and in the crew, pleasure in debauchery, loyalty expressed physically — shoulders locked, bodies piled into one frame. Flexing is precise, not total: one luxury object asserted against a uniform of mass sportswear. Night belongs to them; the flash-lit dark is home light.

## Era & Origins

Emerges in 1990s–2000s French banlieues as the dress of the socio-economic fringe named *la racaille*; part of a European family with Chav, Gopnik, Dresiarz, Cani, Bombardier, Dizelaši, Flaite and Ah Beng. Revived and codified post-2010s by French rap's commercial rise and sneaker collector culture, hardening into the "Lacoste TN" formula documented in 2021-era snapshots.

## Discrimination Cues

- **Bombardier (Romania):** same tracksuit family, but adds manele-era gold chains, Adidas three-stripe dominance and car-trunk culture; racaille keeps the Lacoste crocodile, the TN sole and the checkerboard crossbody.
- **Cani (Spain):** louder color blocks, heavy gold jewelry, slicked hair and flat-brim caps, beachtown rumba energy; racaille is monochrome, technical, night-cité.
- **MDLR:** the closest French cousin and a genuinely blurry border; MDLR drifts toward drill — balaclavas, camo, oversized black puffers, scooter nights — whereas racaille keeps caps uncovered, Lacoste/TN sportswear core and bright flash snapshots.
- **92i:** luxury-rap gloss — black designer layers, sunglasses at night, cash stacks, music-video grade; racaille is phone-grade utility sportswear in housing-estate space.
- **Baddie:** feminine US-influenced glam — makeup, nails, bodycon, ring-light beige; racaille is masculine crew sportswear under harsh flash.

Where evidence is thin — women's participation, exact logo placement rules, the MDLR/92i boundary — prefer the text's dress-code list over invention.
